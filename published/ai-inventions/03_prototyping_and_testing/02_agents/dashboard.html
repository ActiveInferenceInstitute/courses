Core Concepts

1. The Creator-Evaluator Transition

Building and evaluating require different agent configurations. The creator acts to reduce discrepancy between vision and reality. The evaluator must detect performance errors honestly. The inventor's strong priors that the prototype will work (because they designed it from those priors) create bias toward confirmation. Edison's commitment to tin foil phonographs delayed the discovery that wax cylinders were superior.

2. The Inventor's Observation Model

The inventor does not passively receive test data. Their observation model determines what is attended to, what is ignored, and how signals are interpreted. Sensory precision controls how much weight is given to data vs. priors. Attentional selection determines which aspects of behavior are monitored. Knowing the "right answer" creates top-down predictions that can override bottom-up evidence.

3. External Agents as Distributed Sensors

External testers bring different generative models, different priors, and different attention profiles. Naive users reveal gaps between the inventor's model and the user's model. Xerox PARC's Star workstation tests with office workers revealed that drag-and-drop was confusing to people who thought in terms of typewriters and filing cabinets. Beta testing deploys hundreds of independent inference agents across diverse environments.

4. Adversarial Testing

Most testing confirms that prototypes work. Adversarial testing inverts this: the tester's goal is to maximize surprise by finding failures. Red teams bring strong priors that the prototype will fail and creative search strategies for weaknesses. The de Havilland Comet crashes revealed that standard testing had not explored fatigue failure. Adversarial testers explore the regions of possibility space that optimistic testers never visit.

5. Managing Agent-Level Biases

Confirmation bias: strong priors dominate the posterior. Sunk cost bias: past investment is weighted over current evidence. Anchoring bias: early results disproportionately influence later interpretation. The countermeasure across all biases is pre-registration: committing to specific hypotheses, protocols, and success/failure criteria before testing begins, constraining interpretive degrees of freedom.

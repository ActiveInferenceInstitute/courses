Core Concepts

1. Prototype as Embodied Generative Model

A prototype is not a rough draft of a product. It is a physical instantiation of the inventor's beliefs about how the system works. Like any generative model, it encodes causal hypotheses and generates predictions that can be compared against observations. Dyson's cardboard cyclone cone was a generative model predicting that centrifugal force could separate dust from air. Each test generated prediction errors that guided the next iteration.

2. Markov Blanket as Prototype Boundary

Every prototype has a boundary that separates its internal workings from the testing environment. The sensory surface receives inputs (user actions, environmental conditions). The active surface produces outputs (movements, displays, effects). Internal states are the mechanisms shielded by this boundary. Deciding what falls inside vs. outside this blanket is the most consequential prototyping decision you make.

3. Fidelity Levels as Model Resolution

Low-fidelity prototypes are coarse-grained models with broad priors — paper sketches, cardboard models. High-fidelity prototypes are fine-grained models with tight priors — pre-production units. The right fidelity level depends on your current uncertainty: when priors are broad, use low fidelity for rapid, cheap updates. When priors are narrow and you need to detect subtle effects, invest in high fidelity.

4. Include/Exclude: Scope Discipline

Include components causally upstream of your test variables; exclude components that are causally independent. The "kitchen sink prototype" tries to include everything, resulting in confounded test results. A pharmaceutical company testing a drug molecule in cell cultures is applying minimal blanket scope — testing only the drug-receptor interaction before expanding to whole-organism complexity.

5. Modularity and Nested Blankets

Complex prototypes can often be decomposed into sub-systems, each with its own nested Markov blanket. Apple's iPhone prototypes tested multi-touch, radio, and UI separately before integration. Each sub-system prototype isolates specific hypotheses. Build and test sub-systems independently when their internal states are only weakly coupled to other sub-systems.

6. Prototype-Environment Interface

The sensory and active surfaces of a prototype define not just the prototype but the experiment. If the sensory surface does not include temperature sensitivity, testing will not reveal temperature-related failures. NASA's JPL built separate test rigs for gravity, atmosphere, and terrain because no single environment could replicate Mars. The interface design determines what you can learn.

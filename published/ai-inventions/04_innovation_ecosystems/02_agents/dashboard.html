Stakeholders as Inference Agents

Every stakeholder — user, investor, regulator, competitor — is an Active Inference agent with its own generative model, priors, and precision weightings. A consumer models utility and cost. An investor models returns and risk. A regulator models safety and compliance. Understanding these distinct models lets you design strategies that reduce free energy for each agent type.

Example: Tesla was modeled differently by early adopters (identity vehicle), mainstream consumers (practical transport), and oil companies (irrelevant niche) — each based on their own generative model.

Adoption Curves as Heterogeneous Inference

Rogers' adoption categories reflect differences in prior strength and free energy thresholds. Innovators have weak priors and tolerate high uncertainty. The early majority has strong priors requiring substantial evidence. The "chasm" between early adopters and early majority marks a fundamental shift in what evidence matters — from novelty and vision to reliability and social proof.

Example: Slack spread virally among tech teams (innovators with low thresholds) but required enterprise security certifications and IT admin tools to reach the corporate early majority.

Investors as Bayesian Agents

VCs and angel investors update beliefs through a specific evidence hierarchy: team quality (highest precision), market size and timing, product traction, and financial projections. Their priors include base rates of startup failure (~90%). Effective fundraising means providing evidence that systematically updates each level of the investor's model.

Example: Y Combinator's emphasis on "making something people want" is a prescription for generating the highest-precision evidence: demonstrated user free energy reduction.

Regulatory Agents and Risk Models

Regulators prioritize public safety in their generative models, requiring high-precision evidence before permitting market entry. The FDA's phased clinical trial process is a formalized inference procedure. Different regulatory traditions (EU precautionary vs. US balanced) reflect different prior structures about the risks of novel technology.

Example: GMOs are widely used in US agriculture but restricted in the EU — same evidence, different priors about the probability of harm from novel organisms.

Competitors as Overlapping Model Agents

Competitors model similar markets with overlapping but distinct generative models. Competitive advantage comes from having a more accurate model. Strategic signaling — taking actions designed to influence competitor beliefs — manipulates competitors' generative models. Intel's "tick-tock" development cadence made its trajectory predictable while maintaining technical uncertainty.

Example: Amazon's generative model predicted the value of the long tail of niche products, diverging from bookstore incumbents who modeled physical shelf space as the key constraint.

Multi-Stakeholder Coordination

Successful invention deployment requires reducing free energy not for one agent type but for an interconnected system. Stakeholder interests often conflict — what attracts investors may concern regulators, what delights early adopters may confuse the mainstream. The inventor must find strategies that navigate these tensions simultaneously.

Example: Airbnb had to simultaneously reduce safety uncertainty for guests, financial risk for hosts, and regulatory concern for cities — conflicts that required distinct evidence strategies for each agent.

Go-to-Market as Action Selection

Go-to-market strategy is the inventor's highest-level action selection problem. Each launch action has epistemic value (what it teaches) and pragmatic value (what it accomplishes). Early actions should prioritize learning; later actions shift toward scaling what has been validated.

Example: Dollar Shave Club's $4,500 viral video achieved both — testing market receptivity while driving 12,000 subscriptions in 48 hours.

Manufacturing Scale-Up Trade-offs

Scaling production means transitioning from exploration (flexible prototyping) to exploitation (precise manufacturing). Scale too early and you lock in a flawed design; scale too late and competitors capture the market. The key is validating the production generative model before committing to scale.

Example: Tesla's Model 3 "production hell" resulted from attempting full automation before understanding which processes required human flexibility.

Distribution as Active State Design

Distribution channels are the active states through which your invention reaches users. Direct channels give control but limit reach. Retail channels give reach but sacrifice control. The choice also signals positioning: premium retail signals quality, direct-to-consumer signals confidence.

Example: Warby Parker started online (testing demand) then added stores (reducing uncertainty for customers who needed physical trial).

Pricing as Multi-Stakeholder Signal

Price simultaneously affects customer, investor, competitor, and partner generative models. Too high increases customer uncertainty about value; too low increases investor uncertainty about capture and may signal low quality. Freemium models use free tiers as epistemic actions and paid tiers as pragmatic actions.

Example: Gilead's $84,000 hepatitis C cure reduced patient free energy (cured!) while dramatically increasing insurer and regulator free energy.

Market Entry Strategy

First movers shape customer models but bear epistemic costs. Fast followers learn from first-mover errors. Niche pioneers dominate a small segment before expanding. The right strategy depends on competitive dynamics, resource constraints, and the ecosystem phase.

Example: PayPal pioneered online payments with eBay power sellers — a high-pain, high-frequency niche that provided the evidence and infrastructure for broader expansion.

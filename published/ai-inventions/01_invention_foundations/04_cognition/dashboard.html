Inference Over Hidden Causes

When an inventor observes a problem (symptom), they must reason backward to the underlying causes. This requires a generative model that connects hidden causes to observable effects. A rich causal model allows precise diagnosis; a sparse model leads to fixation on the wrong cause. Building the causal map is the cognitive foundation of effective solutions.

Mental Models of Mechanisms

Inventors think in mechanisms — causal stories connecting inputs to outputs through intermediate steps. Mental models enable simulation before building. Tesla visualized rotating magnetic fields; every inventor simulates how their design will behave. Model depth, breadth, and accuracy determine the quality of these predictions.

Problem Reframing

The most powerful cognitive move is changing the question. Dyson reframed vacuum failure from "weak motor" to "bag clogs." Barenyi reframed safety from "rigidity" to "controlled deformation." Techniques include inversion, constraint removal, and abstraction. Reframing changes the generative model's structure, not just its parameters.

Cognitive Flexibility

Expert inventors hold multiple causal models simultaneously and let evidence decide. Barriers include functional fixedness (seeing only conventional uses), Einstellung (applying familiar methods to new problems), and confirmation bias (seeking supporting evidence). Overcoming these requires deliberately asking: "What if I am wrong?"

Counterfactual Reasoning

Invention requires reasoning about things that do not yet exist. By setting variables to new values in a mental model and propagating consequences, inventors predict how novel designs would behave. The power of counterfactual reasoning depends on generative model accuracy — which is why prototyping remains essential as a reality check.

Inventions as Systems

An invention is not an object but a system: an entity that maintains a distinguishable boundary between itself and its environment over time. Internal states (mechanisms, logic, materials), external states (users, environments, competitors), and a boundary (the Markov blanket) define the system. A mousetrap's spring, latch, and bar are internal; the mouse, floor, and room temperature are external; the trigger plate is the boundary.

Markov Blankets of Inventions

The Markov blanket separates internal from external states such that they are conditionally independent given the blanket. For inventions, the blanket consists of sensory states (how the invention receives information) and active states (how it changes the world). Where you draw this boundary determines what you can control vs. what you must adapt to. Drawing it too narrow misses critical interactions; too broad creates unmanageable complexity.

Sensory and Active Boundaries

Every invention has two faces at its boundary. Sensory boundaries gather information (a thermostat's temperature sensor, a search engine parsing queries). Active boundaries change the environment (a thermostat activating heating, a search engine displaying results). Together they form the perception-action loop — the heartbeat of any functioning invention. Inventions fail when this loop breaks at any stage.

Nested Systems and Compositional Design

Complex inventions are compositions of subsystems, each with its own Markov blanket. An electric vehicle contains a battery management system, motor controller, climate system, and navigation — each sensing and acting independently. Conditional independence between subsystems is what makes complex inventions tractable. The IBM PC's hardware-software boundary is a canonical example: clean nesting enabled an entire ecosystem.

Free Energy and Invention Persistence

An invention persists by minimizing free energy — the gap between what its design expects and what it actually encounters. A tent designed for calm weather collapses in storms (high free energy). Robust inventions minimize free energy across conditions through the accuracy-complexity trade-off: precise performance under expected conditions vs. adaptability across unexpected conditions. Over-engineering (excessive accuracy) often fails in practice.

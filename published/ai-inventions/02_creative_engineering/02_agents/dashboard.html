The Inventor as Active Inference Agent

An inventor maintains a generative model of how the world works and acts to bring that model's predictions into reality. The model includes beliefs about physics, user needs, markets, and aesthetics — each with different precision weightings that determine which prediction errors get noticed and acted upon.

Inventor Archetypes

Different inventor types arise from different Active Inference parameter configurations. The Methodical Engineer (strong priors, systematic refinement), the Tinkerer (weak priors, rapid building), the Accidental Discoverer (high anomaly sensitivity), and the Systems Thinker (meta-level model unification) each excel at different types of inventive challenges.

Prior Beliefs as Lens and Filter

An inventor's priors simultaneously reveal and conceal. Kodak's photography expertise made them the best film company — and blinded them to digital imaging. New engineer Steven Sasson lacked those rigid priors and could see digital's potential. Metacognitive awareness of one's own priors is the antidote to creative blindness.

Multi-Agent Team Creativity

Creative teams function as multi-agent Active Inference systems where complementary generative models cover more solution space than any individual. The Apple founding team worked because Wozniak's technical model and Jobs's experiential model caught different prediction errors. Shared artifacts bridge the gap between different models.

Expected Free Energy and Motivation

Inventors are driven by expected free energy with two components: pragmatic value (solving specific problems, achieving goals) and epistemic value (satisfying curiosity, reducing uncertainty). Edison was primarily pragmatic; Feynman primarily epistemic. Most inventors blend both, and the balance shapes project selection and approach.

The Accidental Discoverer Paradox

Percy Spencer discovered microwave cooking because he had both deep magnetron expertise (strong priors that could be violated) and openness to anomaly (high precision on the unexpected chocolate melting). The paradox: you need strong domain knowledge to recognize when something surprising happens, plus the flexibility to follow the surprise.

Markov Blanket of a Creative System

The boundary separating a creative system's internal states from the external world. Includes sensory inputs (information channels, materials, observations) and active outputs (prototypes, patents, demonstrations). Edison's Menlo Park was designed with an extraordinarily rich sensory surface to maximize the diversity of inputs available for creative combination.

Constraints as Generative Priors

Constraints do not block creativity — they focus the creative search space by functioning as priors in the generative model. The Post-it Note emerged because 3M's adhesive focus and Fry's bookmark need together narrowed the solution space enough for a weak adhesive to become valuable. TRIZ catalogs 40 such constraint-resolution patterns.

Free Energy Landscapes

Different creative environments produce different topographies of surprise. Structured R&D labs create smooth landscapes suited for incremental innovation. Loosely structured maker spaces create rugged landscapes suited for radical breakthroughs. Matching the landscape topology to the desired innovation type is a key design decision.

Feedback Loops and Self-Organization

Creative systems change over time through feedback: action produces artifacts, which become perception for the next cycle. IDEO's "build to think" method uses rapid prototyping to generate feedback. At larger scales, these loops drive self-organization — innovation clusters like Silicon Valley emerge from reinforcing loops without central planning.

Affordances and the Adjacent Possible

An environment's affordances — the action possibilities it offers — define the adjacent possible: inventions one combinatorial step from the current state. The Shenzhen electronics ecosystem affords same-day prototyping through geographic concentration of suppliers. Inventors expand the adjacent possible by importing affordances from other domains (exaptation).

Bell Labs as an Engineered Creative System

Bell Labs produced the transistor, laser, Unix, C, and cellular telephony by deliberately engineering a creative system: rich sensory inputs (cross-disciplinary hiring), focused constraints (telecommunications mandate), fast feedback (weekly colloquia, long hallways), and clear output channels (AT&T product lines). Seven Nobel Prizes resulted.

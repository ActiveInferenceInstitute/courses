Module 04: Cognition in Crochet Circles

Learning Objectives

  1. Explain how reading a crochet pattern engages a generative model that predicts fabric structure before a single stitch is made.
  2. Describe mental stitch counting as a form of internal simulation — running the generative model forward in time without acting.
  3. Connect the experience of "thinking with your hands" to embodied cognition in the Active Inference framework.

Introduction

Crochet is not mindless work. Even the simplest dishcloth requires a surprising amount of cognitive activity: reading a pattern, holding a stitch count in memory, predicting how the fabric will behave, imagining the finished object. In Active Inference, cognition is the process by which an agent uses its generative model to simulate, predict, and plan — to think about the world without necessarily acting on it. When you sit with a pattern and mentally walk through the instructions, when you count stitches in your head, when you predict that a certain yarn will drape beautifully in a shawl, you are performing cognitive operations that Active Inference describes with mathematical precision.

This module explores the thinking that happens alongside (and sometimes instead of) stitching. We will look at pattern reading as model engagement, mental simulation as internal prediction, and the fascinating phenomenon of "thinking with your hands" — where cognition is distributed between brain and body.

Key Concepts

1. Pattern Reading as Generative Model Engagement

When you open a crochet pattern, you encounter a compressed description of a future object: "Ch 4, join with sl st to form ring. Rnd 1: Ch 3 (counts as dc), 11 dc in ring, join with sl st to top of ch-3. (12 dc)." Reading this engages your generative model — the internal representation that allows you to predict what the fabric will look like at each stage.

In Active Inference, a generative model specifies how hidden states (the structure of the fabric, the arrangement of stitches) generate observations (what you see, feel, and count). When you read a pattern, you are essentially loading parameters into your generative model. Each instruction — "dc in next st," "skip 2 sts," "3 dc in corner sp" — adds a constraint that shapes your predictions.

Experienced crocheters read patterns like musicians read sheet music: they can "hear" the piece before playing it. They can look at "ch 3, skip 2, dc in next st, ch 1, skip 1, dc in next st, repeat" and immediately visualize a filet mesh pattern with its characteristic grid of open and filled squares. This ability to translate symbolic notation into predicted fabric structure is a cognitive feat — the generative model translating compact instructions into rich, multi-dimensional predictions about texture, shape, drape, and appearance.

Beginners lack this translation ability. They read each instruction as an isolated action: "chain three... now skip two... now double crochet." They cannot yet predict what the accumulation of these actions will produce. The generative model is too sparse to generate look-ahead predictions. As experience grows, the model deepens, and the crocheter can increasingly "see" the finished product in the pattern instructions — a hallmark of cognitive expertise.

2. Mental Stitch Counting as Internal Simulation

Internal simulation in Active Inference is the process of running the generative model forward in time without actually performing actions or receiving new sensory input. It is thinking, imagining, planning — using the model to explore possibilities.

Mental stitch counting is a clear example. When a pattern says "sc in each of next 15 sts," you do not need to physically count 15 stitches before starting — you hold "15" in your working memory and count down (or up) as you stitch. Each stitch decrements an internal counter. This counter is a mental simulation running in parallel with the physical action: the model predicts that after 15 motor actions (insert, yarn over, pull through), the row should be complete.

More complex simulation occurs when crocheters plan ahead. You are working a granny square and need to decide how many rounds to make before it is the right size. You imagine adding another round — your generative model predicts how much larger the square will become, based on your knowledge of how granny rounds grow (each round adds about 1-2 inches, depending on yarn weight and hook size). You simulate the outcome without actually crocheting it. If the simulation predicts the right size, you proceed. If not, you adjust.

This kind of simulation is what Active Inference calls counterfactual reasoning: evaluating outcomes of actions you have not yet taken. Every time a crocheter thinks "If I add one more repeat of the pattern, will it be long enough?" they are running their generative model in simulation mode, generating predicted observations (length, width, shape) and comparing them to their goals.

3. Thinking with Your Hands: Embodied Cognition

One of the most striking features of crochet cognition is how much of it happens in the hands rather than the head. Experienced crocheters report that their hands "know" what to do — they can hold a conversation, watch television, or listen to an audiobook while crocheting complex patterns. The hands seem to think independently.

In Active Inference, this is not mysterious — it is a natural consequence of how generative models can be distributed across the body. The brain is not the only location of the generative model; the hands, through years of practice, have developed their own local models of stitch mechanics. The hands predict what each stitch should feel like, execute the motor sequence automatically, and detect errors through tactile prediction errors — all without requiring conscious, top-down cognitive processing.

This is embodied cognition in the Active Inference framework. The generative model is not a single, monolithic structure sitting in the brain. It is a hierarchy of models distributed across levels: high-level models in the brain handle pattern-level predictions (which stitch comes next, how the pattern repeats), while low-level models in the motor system handle stitch-level predictions (how much force to apply, where to insert the hook, how to wrap the yarn). When these levels are well-calibrated, crochet feels effortless — the "thinking" is distributed.

The moment something unexpected happens — an unfamiliar stitch, a pattern error, a yarn tangle — cognition snaps back to conscious processing. The hands stop, the crocheter looks down, and top-down cognitive resources are deployed. This shift from automatic to controlled processing is precisely what Active Inference predicts: when low-level models generate prediction errors they cannot resolve, the errors propagate upward to higher-level models, demanding conscious attention.

Applications

In crochet, we see Cognition manifest in:

  • Predicting How Fabric Will Drape: Before finishing a garment, experienced crocheters can predict whether it will hang well or bunch up, whether it will stretch out or hold its shape. This prediction is based on their generative model's knowledge of how different stitch patterns, yarn fibers, and hook sizes interact to produce specific fabric properties. A crocheter who chooses a lace stitch for a summer top is cognitively simulating drape and breathability before making a single stitch.

  • Mental Pattern Modification: Many crocheters modify patterns as they work — adjusting length, changing stitch counts, substituting stitches. This requires real-time cognitive simulation: "If I add 6 more stitches to the foundation chain, how will that change the width? Will the pattern repeat still work evenly?" The crocheter runs their generative model in simulation mode, checks whether the modification is consistent, and then executes (or rejects) the change. This is sophisticated counterfactual reasoning grounded in embodied knowledge.

Conclusion

Cognition in crochet is richer than it appears. Pattern reading loads your generative model with predictions. Mental stitch counting runs that model as an internal simulation. And the remarkable phenomenon of "thinking with your hands" reveals that the generative model is not confined to the brain — it is distributed across the entire body-hook-yarn system. In the next module, we move from thinking to doing: how each stitch is an action that changes the fabric and the world.

Lab Protocol

Lab: Thinking in Loops and Chains

Objective

Explore the cognitive dimensions of crochet by engaging in pattern reading, mental simulation, and the transition between automatic and controlled processing — all while observing your own thinking.

Materials

  • Worsted weight yarn in a light color, approximately 30 yards
  • Crochet hook, size H/8 (5.0mm)
  • Two pattern cards (provided below — print or copy them before starting)
  • Pen and Cognition Log (template below)
  • Timer or phone
  • A podcast, audiobook, or conversation partner

Pattern Cards

Pattern A (Simple): Row 1: Ch 21. Sc in 2nd ch from hook and in each ch across. (20 sc) Rows 2-8: Ch 1, turn. Sc in each st across. (20 sc)

Pattern B (Complex): Row 1: Ch 22. Sc in 2nd ch from hook, dc in next st, sc in next st, repeat * to * across. (21 sts) Row 2: Ch 1, turn. Sc in dc, dc in sc, repeat * to * across. Row 3: Repeat Row 2. Rows 4-6: Repeat Row 2.

Cognition Log Template

Phase Task Could I think about other things? Did I lose count? Automatic or Controlled? Notes

Steps

Part 1: Pattern Reading as Model Loading (10 minutes)

  1. Read Pattern A without crocheting. Close your eyes and try to visualize the finished swatch. How clear is your mental image?
  2. Now read Pattern B. Try to visualize this swatch. Is the image clearer or fuzzier than Pattern A? Why?
  3. In your Cognition Log, record your observations. Which pattern was easier to "see" in your mind? This difference reflects the depth of your generative model for different stitch patterns.
  4. Before crocheting, write a prediction: How will Pattern B's texture differ from Pattern A's? This is your generative model making a prediction.

Part 2: Dual-Task Test — Automatic vs. Controlled Processing (20 minutes)

  1. Start crocheting Pattern A while listening to a podcast or audiobook (or having a conversation with a circle partner).
  2. Time yourself for 5 minutes. At the end, note in your log: Were you able to follow the audio content while crocheting? Did you lose your stitch count? Did the crochet feel automatic?
  3. Now switch to Pattern B with the same audio content.
  4. Time yourself for 5 minutes. Note: Did you have to pause the audio or stop the conversation to focus on the pattern? Did you lose track of which stitch came next? Did the crochet demand more conscious attention?
  5. Record the differences in your log. The shift you experienced — from easy dual-tasking with Pattern A to difficult dual-tasking with Pattern B — illustrates the transition from automatic to controlled processing.

Part 3: Mental Simulation and Counting (10 minutes)

  1. Set aside the physical swatch. Without crocheting, mentally simulate working one row of Pattern B. Walk through it in your mind: "Sc in dc, dc in sc, sc in dc..." Count how many stitches you simulate before you lose track or your mind wanders.
  2. Record this number. It is a rough measure of your internal simulation capacity for this pattern.
  3. Now physically crochet one row of Pattern B while counting each stitch aloud. Compare: was the physical version easier or harder than the mental simulation? Why?

Part 4: Prediction Testing (10 minutes)

  1. Look at your Pattern A swatch and your Pattern B swatch side by side.
  2. Compare them to the prediction you wrote in Step 4. Did Pattern B's texture match your expectation? Where was your generative model accurate and where was it off?
  3. Feel both swatches with your eyes closed. Can you tell them apart by texture alone? Record your observations.

Part 5: Reflection and Discussion (10 minutes)

  1. Review your Cognition Log. Where did cognition feel effortless (automatic) and where did it require focused attention (controlled)? What triggered the transitions?

Discussion Questions

  • When you read Pattern B for the first time, how much of the finished texture could you predict? What did you need to actually crochet before you could "see" it?
  • During the dual-task test, what happened to your crochet when your attention was captured by the audio content? Did you make more errors? Did your tension change?
  • When simulating stitches mentally, did you "feel" phantom hand movements? What does this suggest about the relationship between cognitive simulation and motor planning?
  • How does this lab change your understanding of what "thinking" means during crochet?

Wrap-Up

You have explored crochet cognition through pattern reading (model loading), dual-tasking (automatic vs. controlled processing), mental simulation (internal counting), and prediction testing (model accuracy). These cognitive operations are at the heart of Active Inference — they are how your generative model engages with the world before, during, and between stitches.

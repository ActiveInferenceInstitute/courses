## Active Inference Integration

The history of metallurgy is a history of learning — humanity's progressive refinement of generative models for material behavior. From Bronze Age empiricism (trial-and-error alloying) through the Hume-Rothery rules (structured priors for solid solubility) to modern computational alloy design (high-throughput DFT screening), each era represents a deeper generative model with greater predictive power. In Active Inference terms, the field has been performing model learning: updating its beliefs about structure-property relationships to reduce surprise when encountering new alloy systems.

## Key Mappings

| FEP Concept | Alloy Development Translation |
|-------------|------------------------------|
| Model Learning | Historical progression from empirical rules to physics-based models |
| Prior Beliefs | Hume-Rothery rules, Darken equations, classical nucleation theory |
| Likelihood Update | Experimental validation of predicted phase stability or mechanical properties |
| Generative Model Refinement | Iterative improvement of CALPHAD databases with new experimental data |
| Surprise | Unexpected discovery (e.g., metallic glasses, quasicrystals, high-entropy alloys) |
| Epistemic Value | Experiments designed to maximally reduce uncertainty about unexplored composition spaces |

## Content Guidelines

- Frame the Bronze Age to superalloys arc as cumulative Bayesian model building across centuries
- Treat the discovery of high-entropy alloys as a paradigm-shifting surprise that forced model revision
- Connect Hume-Rothery rules to informative priors that constrain the search space for new alloys
- Emphasize that modern materials informatics (ML for materials) automates the learning cycle that metallurgists have performed manually for millennia

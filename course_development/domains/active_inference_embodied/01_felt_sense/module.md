# Unit 01: The Felt Sense — Interoception, Somatic Experience, and the Body as Generative Model

## Learning Objectives

1. Understand Eugene Gendlin's concept of the **felt sense** as an implicit, holistic bodily knowing that precedes and shapes explicit cognition.
2. Explain how **interoceptive inference** — the brain's predictive modeling of internal bodily states — provides the computational foundation for somatic experience.
3. Analyze Antonio Damasio's **somatic marker hypothesis** through the lens of active inference, recognizing emotions as embodied predictions about organismic viability.
4. Develop first-person phenomenological awareness of the felt sense as a practice that complements formal active inference theory.

## Introduction

Place your hand on your chest. Before you name what you feel — before language arrives — there is something already there. A quality, a texture, a sense of how things are right now in your body. Eugene Gendlin called this the **felt sense**: a pre-conceptual, bodily knowing that carries forward the meaning of your situation. It is not yet an emotion, not yet a thought, but the fertile ground from which both arise.

This unit explores the felt sense as a fundamental dimension of embodied cognition, one that active inference helps us understand with unprecedented precision. The body is not merely a vehicle for the brain; it is itself a vast generative model, continuously predicting its own internal states, generating the affective tone that colors all experience. When you feel a knot in your stomach before a difficult conversation, your body is not merely reacting — it is actively inferring, drawing on deep priors about what this situation means for your continued existence.

## Key Concepts

### 1. The Felt Sense as Implicit Complexity

Gendlin (1981) observed that the body holds a kind of knowing that exceeds what we can explicitly articulate. A felt sense is not a sensation in a specific location, nor is it an emotion we can name. It is the body's holistic registration of a situation — what Gendlin called an "implicit intricacy." In active inference terms, the felt sense corresponds to the body's high-dimensional posterior belief about its own state, a belief that integrates interoceptive, proprioceptive, and affective signals into a unified, if inarticulate, gestalt.

### 2. Interoceptive Inference and the Predictive Body

The brain does not passively receive signals from the viscera. As Anil Seth and Karl Friston have shown, interoception is fundamentally predictive: the brain generates top-down predictions about expected cardiac rhythms, respiratory patterns, gut motility, and autonomic tone, then registers prediction errors when bodily states deviate from expectation. This **interoceptive predictive coding** is the computational mechanism through which the felt sense arises. The vague unease you feel in an unfamiliar situation is a prediction error — your body expected one pattern of internal states and encountered another.

### 3. Somatic Markers as Embodied Priors

Antonio Damasio proposed that emotions function as somatic markers — bodily states that tag decision options with positive or negative valence, guiding choice before conscious deliberation. In active inference, somatic markers are best understood as **embodied priors**: learned associations between situations and their predicted bodily consequences. The gut feeling that warns you away from a bad decision is your generative model running a simulation of the expected interoceptive states that would follow each choice, and flagging the option with the highest predicted free energy.

### 4. Precision and Emotional Intensity

Not all felt senses carry equal weight. The **precision** (inverse variance) of interoceptive prediction errors determines how strongly a bodily signal influences perception and action. High precision on interoceptive signals produces vivid emotional experience — the unmistakable grip of anxiety, the warmth of connection. Low precision dampens the felt sense, producing alexithymia or emotional numbness. Active inference thus explains both the power and the fragility of somatic knowing.

### 5. Focusing as Free Energy Minimization

Gendlin's Focusing technique — attending to the felt sense, allowing it to unfold, and finding a "handle" word that resonates — can be understood as a deliberate practice of interoceptive free energy minimization. By directing attention (precision) to the felt sense, the practitioner amplifies interoceptive prediction errors, prompting the generative model to update until a new, more accurate representation emerges. The characteristic "felt shift" that Focusing practitioners report corresponds to a reduction in free energy — the moment when the model's predictions finally match the body's actual state.

## Active Inference Connection

The felt sense is not an epiphenomenon of embodied cognition; it is its core mechanism. Active inference holds that all perception is prediction, and the body's self-model — its predictions about its own internal milieu — constitutes the deepest layer of the generative model. Free energy minimization at this interoceptive level is what keeps the organism alive: maintaining homeostasis, regulating allostatic processes, and generating the affective signals that orient behavior toward viability. The felt sense, in this framework, is the phenomenology of interoceptive inference — what it is like, from the inside, to be a self-modeling body.

## Experiential Applications

* **Practice — Body Scan as Interoceptive Attention**: Lie comfortably and systematically bring attention to each region of your body, from feet to crown. Notice not just physical sensations (tingling, pressure, warmth) but the felt quality of each region — its aliveness, its mood, its implicit meaning. You are training yourself to increase the precision weighting on interoceptive signals, bringing the body's generative model into sharper phenomenological focus.

* **Case Study — Somatic Experiencing in Trauma Resolution**: Peter Levine's Somatic Experiencing therapy works with the felt sense to resolve trauma held in the body. A client who survived a car accident may carry a chronic felt sense of bracing — shoulders lifted, breath held, muscles tense. Through gentle, titrated attention to these bodily states, the therapist helps the client's generative model update: the accident is over, the body can release its defensive predictions. The felt shift — a spontaneous trembling, a deep exhale, a softening — marks the moment of free energy reduction.

## Summary

The felt sense — that pre-verbal, bodily knowing described by Gendlin — finds rigorous computational grounding in active inference's account of interoceptive prediction. The body is not a passive container but an active generative model, continuously predicting its own internal states and registering surprise when predictions fail. Emotions, gut feelings, and somatic markers are embodied priors that shape perception and guide action long before conscious deliberation begins. By attending to the felt sense through practices like Focusing and body scanning, we engage directly with the deepest layer of our predictive architecture — the layer that keeps us alive and orients us toward what matters.

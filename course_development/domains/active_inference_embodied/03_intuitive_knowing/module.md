# Unit 03: Intuitive Knowing — Tacit Knowledge, Embodied Expertise, and Pre-Reflective Intelligence

## Learning Objectives

1. Explain Michael Polanyi's concept of **tacit knowledge** — "we know more than we can tell" — as a form of embodied generative modeling that operates below the threshold of explicit awareness.
2. Analyze Hubert Dreyfus's **skill acquisition model** through active inference, understanding how expertise transforms deliberate rule-following into fluid, pre-reflective performance.
3. Describe how **gut feelings** and **intuitive judgments** emerge from highly compressed, precision-weighted interoceptive priors built through extensive experience.
4. Appreciate the relationship between embodied expertise and free energy minimization as the progressive reduction of prediction error through deep practice.

## Introduction

A master potter centers clay on the wheel. Her hands know exactly how much pressure to apply, when to draw upward, when to let the form emerge. If you ask her to explain what she is doing, she will struggle — not because she lacks intelligence, but because the intelligence lives in her hands, in the micro-adjustments of pressure and speed that no verbal description can capture. This is intuitive knowing: the vast reservoir of embodied competence that exceeds explicit articulation.

Michael Polanyi famously observed that "we know more than we can tell." Hubert Dreyfus spent decades arguing that this tacit, embodied knowing is not a deficient form of cognition awaiting translation into rules, but the highest form of intelligence — the kind that distinguishes genuine expertise from mere competence. Active inference provides the formal framework for understanding why: intuitive knowing is the result of a generative model so deeply trained by experience that it can predict outcomes with minimal free energy, generating fluid, skilful action without the computational overhead of deliberate reasoning.

## Key Concepts

### 1. Tacit Knowledge and the Subsidiary-Focal Structure

Polanyi (1966) distinguished between **focal awareness** — what we attend to explicitly — and **subsidiary awareness** — the background knowledge that enables focal attention but cannot itself be fully articulated. A pianist focally attends to the musical phrase while subsidiarily relying on years of embodied finger-keyboard knowledge. In active inference terms, subsidiary awareness corresponds to deeply entrenched priors in the generative model — parameters that have been optimized to such low free energy states that they no longer generate significant prediction errors and thus remain below the threshold of conscious attention.

### 2. The Dreyfus Model: From Novice to Expert

Hubert Dreyfus and Stuart Dreyfus (1986) proposed five stages of skill acquisition: Novice, Advanced Beginner, Competent, Proficient, and Expert. The novice follows explicit rules; the expert acts from intuitive, situation-specific responsiveness. This trajectory maps precisely onto active inference's account of model optimization. The novice's generative model is sparse and relies on high-precision, explicit priors (rules). Through practice, the model becomes increasingly rich and contextual, building deep hierarchical priors that allow fluid, context-sensitive prediction. The expert's model has minimized free energy so thoroughly that action flows without deliberative interruption.

### 3. Gut Feelings as Compressed Inference

When an experienced firefighter enters a burning building and suddenly feels that the floor is about to collapse — before any visual or auditory evidence confirms it — she is drawing on what Gary Klein calls **recognition-primed decision making**. In active inference terms, her generative model, trained by hundreds of fire scenes, generates a rapid interoceptive prediction: this situation pattern predicts danger. The gut feeling is the somatic expression of a high-precision, low-entropy posterior belief — a prediction so confident that it bypasses deliberative processing and manifests directly as bodily urgency.

### 4. The Paradox of Expertise and Articulation

Why does expertise resist verbal articulation? Because the knowledge is encoded not in propositional format but in the parameters of the generative model itself — in synaptic weights, in the tuning of sensorimotor contingencies, in the body's learned patterns of response. Attempting to articulate tacit knowledge forces a translation from a high-dimensional, continuous model into a low-dimensional, discrete symbolic format. Information is inevitably lost. This is not a failure of the expert but a structural feature of embodied cognition: the body's knowing exceeds the bandwidth of language.

### 5. Pre-Reflective Self-Awareness

Shaun Gallagher distinguishes between **reflective** self-awareness (thinking about oneself) and **pre-reflective** self-awareness (the implicit sense of being the subject of one's experience). Intuitive knowing operates at this pre-reflective level: the expert does not think about what to do but simply does it, with a tacit awareness that it is she who is acting. In active inference, pre-reflective self-awareness corresponds to the generative model's implicit self-model — the deep prior that "I am the agent generating these predictions and executing these actions."

## Active Inference Connection

Intuitive knowing represents the long-term fruit of active inference. Every episode of practice, every encounter with a domain, every correction of a prediction error contributes to the progressive optimization of the generative model. Over thousands of hours, the model achieves such deep attunement to the domain's statistical structure that it can generate accurate predictions with minimal computational effort. Free energy is chronically low; surprise is rare. The subjective experience of this state is flow, mastery, and the uncanny sense of "just knowing" — the body and world so thoroughly coupled that the boundary between them becomes phenomenologically transparent.

## Experiential Applications

* **Practice — Attending to Tacit Knowing in Daily Action**: Choose a routine skilled activity — cooking, driving, typing, playing an instrument. Perform the activity normally, then pause and try to articulate exactly what your body is doing. Notice the gap between your fluent performance and your ability to describe it. This gap is the territory of tacit knowledge — the vast submerged portion of your generative model's iceberg.

* **Case Study — Chess Grandmasters and Pattern Recognition**: Herbert Simon and William Chase (1973) showed that chess grandmasters recognize approximately 50,000 board patterns, allowing them to perceive meaningful structures in positions that novices see as random arrangements. This massive pattern library constitutes a deep generative model of chess positions. The grandmaster's intuitive sense that "this position feels dangerous" or "there must be a tactic here" is a precision-weighted prediction error — the model detecting a mismatch between the current position and its expected trajectory, even before the specific tactical sequence has been consciously identified.

## Summary

Intuitive knowing — the tacit, pre-reflective intelligence that distinguishes the expert from the novice — is the deepest expression of embodied active inference. Through extended practice, the generative model becomes so finely tuned to a domain's structure that prediction flows effortlessly, action arises without deliberation, and knowledge lives not in propositions but in the body's learned patterns of engagement. Polanyi's tacit dimension, Dreyfus's phenomenology of expertise, and Klein's recognition-primed decision making all converge on the same insight that active inference formalizes: the body knows more than the mind can say, and this knowing is not a deficiency but the pinnacle of cognitive achievement.

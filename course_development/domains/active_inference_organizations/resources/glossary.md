# Glossary: Active Inference for Organizations

> Definitions of key terms used throughout the curriculum.
> Designed for business leaders, managers, and organizational scientists.
> Each entry maps the formal Active Inference / FEP concept to its organizational translation.

---

### Active Inference

The theory that all adaptive systems — including organizations — act to confirm their predictions about the world while simultaneously updating those predictions when surprised. In business terms: organizations execute strategy (action) while learning from market feedback (perception), continuously reducing the gap between expectations and reality.

### Adaptive Strategy

A strategy that is explicitly designed to be revised as new information arrives, rather than treated as a fixed plan. Under Active Inference, adaptive strategy corresponds to policy selection under expected free energy — choosing actions that simultaneously exploit known advantages and explore uncertain opportunities.

### Allostasis

The process of achieving stability through change. Unlike homeostasis (maintaining a fixed setpoint), allostasis means the organization actively recalibrates its targets. Example: a retailer shifting from brick-and-mortar to omnichannel is not returning to a prior state but transforming its steady state.

### Competitive Intelligence

The organizational analogue of sensory inference — gathering, filtering, and interpreting signals about competitors, markets, and regulatory environments. Under Active Inference, this corresponds to the likelihood mapping (how observations relate to hidden states of the competitive landscape).

### Culture as Shared Model

The idea that organizational culture is, formally, a shared generative model — a set of collective beliefs, expectations, and norms that team members use to predict each other's behavior and coordinate action. Cultural misalignment = divergent generative models → high collective prediction error.

### Double-Loop Learning

A concept from Chris Argyris: **single-loop learning** updates actions within existing assumptions; **double-loop learning** revises the assumptions themselves. In Active Inference terms, single-loop corresponds to updating beliefs (posterior inference), while double-loop corresponds to updating the generative model's structure (model selection / structure learning).

### Epistemic Value (Information Value)

The expected reduction in uncertainty that a strategic action will produce. When a firm runs a pilot program or market test, the primary value is not immediate revenue but the information gained — reducing uncertainty about customer preferences, technology readiness, or competitive response.

### Execution Risk

The prediction error associated with strategic implementation — the gap between the plan's expected outcomes and the actual results. High execution risk corresponds to high expected free energy along a particular policy, suggesting the organization should either gather more information (epistemic action) or choose a more robust strategy.

### Expected Free Energy (EFE)

The quantity that guides policy selection in Active Inference. It combines **pragmatic value** (will this action achieve our goals?) and **epistemic value** (will this action reduce our uncertainty?). For organizations, EFE provides a principled framework for balancing exploitation of known markets with exploration of new opportunities.

### Free Energy (Variational)

A tractable upper bound on surprise. For organizations, free energy represents the total "tension" or misalignment between the organization's model of the world and reality. Strategic initiatives, market research, and organizational learning all serve to minimize this quantity — either by changing actions (active inference) or updating beliefs (perceptual inference).

### Generative Model (Organizational Model)

The organization's implicit or explicit model of how its environment works — including assumptions about customer behavior, competitor strategy, regulatory trends, and internal capabilities. It generates predictions about what will happen next. A flawed generative model leads to persistent strategic surprise.

### Homeostasis

The process of maintaining critical variables within viable bounds. For organizations, this includes maintaining cash flow, employee engagement, customer satisfaction, and regulatory compliance within acceptable ranges. Active Inference extends this concept by showing how organizations actively work to keep themselves in preferred states.

### Incentives

Mechanisms that shape the precision (confidence weighting) of signals within an organization. Well-designed incentives increase precision on strategically important signals; poorly designed incentives create "noisy" or misleading internal information flows, leading to organizational prediction error.

### Information Flow

The patterns of communication, data movement, and signal propagation within and across organizational boundaries. In Active Inference terms, information flows constitute the sensory and active states of the organizational Markov blanket — what comes in (market data, customer feedback) and what goes out (products, communications, strategies).

### Internal States

The hidden variables that characterize an organization's identity, capabilities, and beliefs — including tacit knowledge, culture, routines, and strategic assumptions. These are the states "inside the Markov blanket" that the organization updates through learning and restructuring.

### Knowledge Management

The organizational practice of creating, sharing, using, and curating knowledge. Under Active Inference, knowledge management is the mechanism by which the organization maintains and updates its generative model — ensuring that lessons learned are incorporated into future predictions and that institutional knowledge survives personnel changes.

### Markov Blanket (Organizational Boundary)

The statistical boundary that separates an organization's internal states from its external environment. It consists of **sensory states** (what the org receives: market data, customer feedback, regulatory signals) and **active states** (what the org emits: products, communications, lobbying). The blanket defines what is "inside" and "outside" the organization.

### Mental Models

The internal representations that individuals and teams use to understand how things work. In Active Inference, these correspond to the parameters of the generative model. When team members have incompatible mental models, coordination breaks down because their predictions about each other's behavior diverge.

### Organizational Boundaries

The formal and informal limits that define what is inside versus outside an organization. Boundaries can be legal (corporate entity), functional (department walls), informational (what data stays inside), or cultural (who shares the same norms). Active Inference frames these as Markov blankets at different scales.

### Organizational Cognition

The distributed process by which an organization forms beliefs, makes sense of ambiguous situations, and updates its worldview. This goes beyond any individual's cognition — it emerges from the interaction of information systems, decision processes, meetings, and cultural norms. Under Active Inference, organizational cognition is the collective process of posterior inference.

### Organizational Learning

The process by which an organization improves its generative model over time — reducing the gap between predictions and outcomes. This includes both incremental improvement (parameter learning: refining existing mental models) and transformative change (structure learning: adopting entirely new frameworks for understanding the business environment).

### Precision (Signal Confidence)

The inverse variance (reliability) of a signal or belief. In organizations, precision determines which information gets weighted heavily in decisions. A CEO who "trusts the data" is assigning high precision to quantitative signals; one who "goes with gut feeling" is assigning high precision to prior beliefs. Precision weighting errors are a major source of organizational dysfunction.

### Prediction Error (Strategic Surprise)

The difference between what the organization expected and what actually happened — e.g., a missed sales forecast, an unexpected competitor move, or an unforeseen regulation. Active Inference frames all organizational adaptation as driven by the imperative to minimize prediction error, either by changing the world (active inference) or changing beliefs (perceptual inference).

### Resilience

An organization's capacity to maintain its identity and functioning when confronted with unexpected perturbations. Under Active Inference, resilience corresponds to having a generative model that is both accurate (low prediction error under normal conditions) and flexible (able to rapidly reweight beliefs and actions when the environment changes).

### Resource Allocation

The process of distributing organizational resources (capital, talent, attention) across competing priorities. Under Active Inference, resource allocation is analogous to precision allocation — deciding which signals, projects, and opportunities deserve the most attention and investment.

### Roles

Functional positions within an organization that partition the overall inference task. Each role specializes in a subset of the sensing, modeling, or acting that the organization must perform. Well-defined roles reduce the computational burden on any single agent; poorly defined roles create redundancy, gaps, or conflicts in the organizational inference process.

### Scenario Planning

A strategic practice of constructing multiple plausible future states and evaluating strategies against each. Under Active Inference, scenario planning corresponds to evaluating expected free energy across multiple possible world-models — selecting strategies that perform well not just under the most likely scenario but also under plausible alternatives.

### Sense-Making

The process by which individuals and organizations give meaning to ambiguous or equivocal signals (Karl Weick's foundational concept). In Active Inference terms, sense-making is the process of posterior inference — updating beliefs to best explain the observed sensory data. Organizational sense-making is collective posterior inference.

### Signal Detection

The organizational capacity to distinguish meaningful signals from noise in the business environment. Corresponds to the likelihood function in the generative model — the mapping from hidden states (true market conditions) to observations (reports, metrics, customer feedback). Failures in signal detection lead to either false alarms or missed threats.

### Strategic Assumptions

The typically unexamined beliefs that underpin an organization's strategy — e.g., "Our customers value price over quality" or "This technology will not disrupt our industry for 5 years." Under Active Inference, these are the priors of the generative model. When strategic assumptions are wrong, all downstream predictions and actions will be systematically biased.

### Strategic Planning

The organizational process of selecting long-term goals and the policies (action sequences) to achieve them. Under Active Inference, strategic planning corresponds to policy selection — choosing sequences of actions that minimize expected free energy over an extended time horizon, balancing pragmatic goal achievement with epistemic uncertainty reduction.

### Team Dynamics

The patterns of interaction, communication, and mutual prediction among team members. Under Active Inference, effective teams are those whose members have aligned generative models — they can predict each other's behavior, reducing coordination costs. Team dysfunction often stems from divergent mental models or misaligned precision weighting.

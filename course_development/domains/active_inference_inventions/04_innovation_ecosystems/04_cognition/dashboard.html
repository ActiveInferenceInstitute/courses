Business Model as Generative Model

A business model predicts how value flows between your invention and its ecosystem. Each element of the Business Model Canvas is a hypothesis with an associated confidence level. The Active Inference approach treats business model design as model construction, emphasizing uncertainty quantification over assertion.

Example: Stripe's model evolved from "simple payments for developers" to a comprehensive developer services platform as evidence updated each canvas element.

Competitive Positioning Through Model Comparison

Porter's three strategies (cost leadership, differentiation, focus) represent different approaches to reducing customer free energy. Competitive advantage comes from maintaining a more accurate generative model of market needs than competitors. Your actions reveal your model to competitors, creating recursive modeling dynamics.

Example: Tesla positioned on software-defined vehicles — a dimension where incumbent automakers' generative models could not easily adapt.

Technology Forecasting as Probabilistic Inference

S-curves describe typical technology performance trajectories. Forecasting errors arise from misidentifying which S-curve you are on or failing to anticipate transitions. Active Inference maintains probability distributions over possible trajectories rather than single-point predictions.

Example: Moore's Law was a remarkably accurate generative model of semiconductor progress maintained for decades, whose recent slowing signals a model structure change.

Scenario Planning as Model Averaging

Rather than betting on a single future, scenario planning maintains multiple generative models and selects strategies that perform well across several of them. This is Bayesian model averaging applied to strategic decisions, naturally handling deep uncertainty where the correct model structure is unknown.

Example: Shell's dual-scenario planning in the 1970s prepared it for the oil price shock that competitors' single-model strategies did not anticipate.

Metacognition and Strategic Blind Spots

Metacognition means recognizing the limitations of your own generative model — confirmation bias, anchoring, overconfidence, and untested assumptions. Andy Grove's "strategic inflection points" and Jeff Bezos's "Day 1" philosophy are metacognitive strategies for maintaining model flexibility.

Example: Grove's question — "What would a new CEO do?" — deliberately circumvents the sunk-cost bias that prevents model restructuring.

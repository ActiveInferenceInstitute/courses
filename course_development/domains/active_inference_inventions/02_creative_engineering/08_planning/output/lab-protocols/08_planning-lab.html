Objective

By the end of this lab, you will have a structured but adaptive plan for advancing your invention from its current stage to its next major milestone.

Part 2: Exploration-Exploitation Assessment (10 minutes)

The exploration-exploitation trade-off is the most important strategic decision in creative work. Explore too long and you never finish anything — you become a perpetual ideator with a drawer full of concepts. Converge too early and you miss better solutions — you become an efficient developer of mediocre ideas. The Active Inference framework provides guidance: explore when the expected information gain from generating more alternatives exceeds the expected value of developing the current best option. Converge when further exploration is unlikely to discover a concept significantly better than the best one you already have. This assessment helps you determine which phase your project should currently be in.

Part 3: Design Your Stage-Gate Plan (15 minutes)

A stage-gate plan divides your inventive project into discrete stages separated by decision points (gates). Each stage involves creative work — building, testing, learning. Each gate involves evaluation — do we have enough evidence to proceed, or should we pivot or stop? The power of stage-gate planning is that it makes implicit decisions explicit. Without gates, projects drift — continuing out of momentum rather than evidence, or pivoting out of frustration rather than analysis. With gates, every continuation is a conscious choice supported by evidence against defined criteria.

Part 4: Information-Maximizing Milestones (10 minutes)

Traditional project planning orders activities by logical dependency — you cannot test before you build, you cannot build before you design. Information-maximizing planning adds a second ordering criterion: test the most dangerous assumptions first. A "dangerous" assumption is one that is both high-impact (if wrong, the project fails) and testable (you can design an experiment to check it). By testing these assumptions early, you either gain confidence that the project is viable or discover deal-breakers before investing significant resources. This is the "fail fast" principle applied at the planning level — structuring the project so that the most likely failure modes are encountered as early as possible.

Part 5: Multi-Horizon Planning (10 minutes)

Most planning fails not because the plans are wrong but because they apply the wrong level of detail to the wrong time horizon. A common mistake is to create a detailed 12-month plan — specifying exact activities, deliverables, and dates for every week of the next year. This plan will be wrong by month three because the structural uncertainty in creative work makes long-range prediction unreliable. An equally common mistake is to have no plan at all for the near term — living in vague aspirations without concrete next actions. Multi-horizon planning solves both problems by matching the level of detail to the reliability of prediction: precise for next week, directional for next quarter, visionary for next year.

Discussion / Debrief Questions

  1. Was it difficult to classify your uncertainties as parametric versus structural? What does this difficulty tell you about the maturity of your generative model?

  2. The exploration-exploitation assessment may have revealed that you are exploring too much or too little. Which tendency do you naturally exhibit, and how does it relate to your inventor archetype from Module 02?

  3. Stage-gate processes force explicit go/no-go decisions. Is there a decision you have been avoiding making about your invention project? What evidence would help you make it?

  4. Information-maximizing milestone ordering often produces a plan that is very different from the intuitive linear sequence. How did your restructured plan differ from your original, and which order makes more sense?

  5. The multi-horizon planning approach asks you to be very detailed about the near future and very vague about the far future. Does this feel uncomfortable? How does matching planning depth to prediction reliability differ from how you normally plan?

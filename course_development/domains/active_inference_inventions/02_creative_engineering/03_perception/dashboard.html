Perception as Prediction Error Detection

In Active Inference, you perceive what your model fails to predict. Creative perception means detecting prediction errors that others' models suppress. Henry Ford saw the assembly line principle in a slaughterhouse because his manufacturing model predicted stationary products — the moving carcasses were a prediction error he treated as signal.

Functional Fixedness

When categorical priors about objects have excessively high precision, alternative uses become invisible. Duncker's candle problem shows this: the box of tacks cannot be perceived as a shelf when it is firmly categorized as "container." The Generic Parts Technique — describing objects by physical properties only — reduces categorical precision and unlocks novel perceptions.

Cross-Domain Pattern Recognition

Generative models encode abstract structural patterns that transfer across domains. The Shinkansen bullet train nose was redesigned based on kingfisher beak morphology because engineer Eiji Nakatsu's dual model (engineering + ornithology) could perceive the structural similarity between media-transition problems. Polymaths carry more transferable models.

Latent Need Detection

Latent needs exist as prediction errors in users' models but are not consciously registered because users have adapted their preferences to available options. Sony's Walkman addressed a need no consumer had articulated. Ethnographic observation and detecting "thoughtless acts" (unconscious workarounds) reveal these hidden opportunities.

Perceptual Reframing Techniques

Systematic methods for altering your generative model to change what prediction errors become visible. Inversion (make it worse, then reverse), scale shifting (component vs. system vs. society), stakeholder rotation (see through different eyes), and temporal reframing (past, present, future) each modify different model parameters to reveal hidden features.

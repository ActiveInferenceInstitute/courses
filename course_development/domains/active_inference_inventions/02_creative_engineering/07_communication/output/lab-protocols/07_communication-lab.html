Lab: Crafting Your Inventive Pitch

Objective

Develop a complete communication package for your invention idea: a structured narrative, visual communication artifacts, an elevator pitch, and a practice patent claim. By the end of this lab, you will have a multi-format communication toolkit and the experience of adapting your message for different audiences.

Materials / Prerequisites

  • Your own invention idea or project
  • Paper and pen/markers for sketching
  • A timer
  • A willing listener (in person or remote) for the pitch practice
  • Completed reading of Module 07: The Inventive Pitch

Part 1: Audience Model Analysis (10 minutes)

Before communicating, model your audience. The single most common mistake in invention communication is talking about what you know rather than addressing what the audience needs to know. Effective communication begins with empathy — building an explicit model of what the audience currently believes, what they care about, and what would need to change in their understanding for them to appreciate your invention. This is Active Inference applied to social cognition: you are constructing a generative model of another person's generative model, then designing actions (words, images, demonstrations) to update it.

Part 2: Build the Invention Narrative (15 minutes)

The six-part invention narrative (setup, tension, insight, solution, evidence, vision) is not just a presentation format — it is a model-alignment protocol. Each part performs a specific cognitive operation on the audience: the setup activates their existing model, the tension creates a prediction error that their model cannot resolve, the insight provides a model update that resolves the error, the solution demonstrates the updated model's predictions, the evidence confirms those predictions, and the vision extends the model into the future. By following this structure, you manage the audience's learning process rather than simply dumping information.

Part 3: Visual Communication (12 minutes)

Visual communication is not optional — it is essential. Research on cognition consistently shows that visual information is processed faster, remembered longer, and understood more accurately than verbal information. For inventions, which often involve spatial relationships, temporal sequences, and causal mechanisms, visual communication provides information channels that words cannot access. You do not need to be an artist. Rough sketches with clear labels communicate more effectively than polished prose. The goal is to make the invisible visible — to give your audience perceptual evidence that their visual system can process directly.

Create three visual artifacts that communicate different aspects of your invention.

Part 4: The Elevator Pitch (10 minutes)

The elevator pitch is the most compressed form of invention communication — a 30-second to 2-minute summary that conveys the core value proposition under extreme time constraint. Its importance extends far beyond actual elevator encounters: the ability to articulate your invention concisely and compellingly is a test of how well you understand it. If you cannot explain your invention in 60 seconds, it usually means your own generative model contains ambiguities, redundancies, or unnecessary complexity. The pitch-writing process often reveals these issues, making it a valuable self-diagnostic tool as well as a communication device.

Step 1 (5 min): Write a 60-second elevator pitch for your invention. It should include: the problem (1-2 sentences), the insight (1 sentence), the solution (2-3 sentences), and the ask or vision (1 sentence). Time yourself reading it aloud — edit until it fits in 60 seconds.

Part 5: Practice Patent Claim (13 minutes)

Patent claims are the most precise form of invention communication. While narratives and pitches are designed to persuade, patent claims are designed to define — they specify exactly what the invention is, no more and no less. Writing a patent claim forces a level of clarity about your invention that no other communication format demands. You must specify the essential components (what cannot be removed without destroying the invention), the relationships between them (how they interact), and the result (what the combination achieves that the components alone do not). This exercise is not about creating a legally valid patent claim but about experiencing how formal specification sharpens your understanding of your own invention.

Step 1 (5 min): Write a simplified independent claim for your invention. Use this structure: "A [type of thing] for [function], comprising: [component A]; [component B]; wherein [component A] is [related to] [component B] such that [result]."

Discussion / Debrief Questions

  1. When you delivered your elevator pitch, what was the biggest gap between what you intended to communicate and what the listener understood? How would you modify the pitch to close that gap?

  2. The module describes the "curse of knowledge" — the inventor's difficulty in predicting what the audience does not know. Did you experience this during the lab? What assumptions did you make about your audience's knowledge that turned out to be wrong?

  3. Which communication format (narrative, visual, pitch, patent claim) was most difficult for you? What does this tell you about which aspects of your generative model are hardest to externalize?

  4. The before/after visual comparison is often the most effective single communication artifact. Was your before/after compelling? What could make the contrast sharper?

  5. Patent claims force extreme precision about what your invention IS and IS NOT. Did the claim-writing exercise change how you think about the boundaries of your invention? Did it reveal ambiguities you had not noticed?

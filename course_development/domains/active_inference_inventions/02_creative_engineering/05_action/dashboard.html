Making as Active Inference

Every prototype serves dual functions: pragmatically changing the world (building the invention) and epistemically generating observations (learning what works). Dyson's 5,127 prototypes were 5,127 questions posed to reality, each answer updating his generative model of cyclonic separation. A prototype that "fails" is an informative observation, not a waste.

Rapid Prototyping and Fidelity Matching

Maximize information gained per unit of time and cost. Match prototype fidelity to the question being asked: paper prototypes for layout, foam models for ergonomics, functional breadboards for electronics. Building too much fidelity wastes resources and introduces confounding observations (users react to finish quality rather than function).

Sketch Modeling and Embodied Cognition

Rough 3D models made from cardboard, foam, and tape engage spatial reasoning, motor planning, and proprioceptive feedback unavailable during mental simulation. Frank Gehry begins every project with crumpled cardboard models whose physical properties co-create the design. Manipulation generates perceptual evidence that imagination cannot.

Design Sprints

Five-day structured process compressing the full perception-action-cognition cycle: Map (define model), Sketch (generate policies), Decide (evaluate expected free energy), Prototype (execute policy), Test (observe and update). Time constraint forces action over analysis, resolving uncertainties in days that would persist for months under conventional development.

Bricolage: Invention With What Is at Hand

Building with available materials rather than acquiring specific materials for a predetermined design. The bricoleur lets material affordances guide the generative model — materials become co-authors. Apollo 13's CO2 scrubber repair (plastic bags, cardboard, tape, a sock) is the most famous bricolage achievement in engineering history.

# Lab: Building Your Inventive Learning Plan

## Objective

Assess your current inventive skill profile across perception, cognition, action, and learning. Then design a personalized deliberate practice plan, conduct a mini patent analysis exercise, and explore one biomimicry connection to your invention idea. By the end of this lab, you will have a concrete, actionable plan for systematically improving your inventive capacity.

- Internet access (for patent search)

## Part 3: Biomimicry Exploration (12 minutes)

Nature has been solving engineering problems for 3.8 billion years through the iterative process of natural selection — a process that is, at its core, a form of Active Inference operating at evolutionary timescales. Every organism represents a refined generative model for surviving in its environment. Biomimicry taps into this vast library of tested solutions by identifying structural analogies between biological and engineering challenges. The key is to think in functional terms: do not ask "What animal looks like my invention?" but rather "What organism faces the same functional challenge as my invention?"

## Part 4: Design Your Learning Plan (15 minutes)

A learning plan for invention is fundamentally different from a learning plan for academic study. Academic learning optimizes for knowledge acquisition — absorbing and retaining information. Inventive learning optimizes for capability expansion — being able to do things you could not do before. This requires all three modes: parameter learning (getting better at what you already do), structure learning (developing new ways of understanding problems), and policy learning (acquiring new hands-on skills). The most common learning imbalance among inventors is over-emphasis on parameter learning (refinining existing expertise) at the expense of structure learning (developing new frameworks) and policy learning (acquiring new practical skills).

## Part 5: The Learning Spiral Check (6 minutes)

The learning spiral — perception, cognition, action, learning — must cycle continuously for inventive skill to develop. Most inventors have a preferred phase where they spend disproportionate time: some love reading and researching (perception), others love analyzing and planning (cognition), others love building (action), and some love reflecting and writing (learning). The phase you neglect is often the one that would contribute most to your growth, precisely because it exercises cognitive capacities your preferred phase does not engage. This brief self-assessment identifies your imbalance and prescribes a corrective action.

## Discussion / Debrief Questions

1. Which of the three learning modes (parameter, structure, policy) is most important for your current inventive work? Why? Is it the same mode you naturally practice, or do you need to deliberately shift?

2. The patent analysis exercise asks you to read patents as Active Inference artifacts. Did this framing change how you read the patent? What did you learn about the inventor's thinking process that a conventional reading would have missed?

3. Biomimicry requires translating biological principles to engineering. Did you find it easy or difficult to identify biological analogies for your invention challenge? What does this tell you about the breadth of your current generative model?

4. Deliberate practice requires targeting weaknesses, which is inherently uncomfortable. How will you motivate yourself to practice skills you are bad at rather than skills you enjoy?

5. Edison maintained daily experiment quotas. What is the equivalent for your inventive practice? How many learning cycles (perception-cognition-action-learning) per week would constitute adequate practice?

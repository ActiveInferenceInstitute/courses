# Lab: Mapping Your Invention as an Active Inference System

## Objective

Apply the foundational Active Inference concepts from all eight modules in this section to produce an integrated system analysis of your own invention project. By the end of this lab, you will have a Markov blanket map, an agent profile, a prediction error inventory, and a preliminary development roadmap.

## Prerequisites

- Familiarity with the key concepts from Modules 01-08 of Invention Foundations
- An invention idea (at any stage of development, from initial concept to working prototype)
- Paper, whiteboard, or digital drawing tool for diagramming

## Materials

- Your invention idea or project
- Drawing materials (paper and markers, whiteboard, or a diagramming tool like Miro or draw.io)
- A timer for timed activities
- The Summary Tables from Modules 01-08 for reference

## Procedure

### Part 1: System Boundary Mapping (15 minutes)

Draw a diagram of your invention as a system.

1. **Identify internal states**: What are the core components, mechanisms, or processes that define your invention? List at least five.
2. **Identify external states**: What aspects of the environment does your invention interact with? Consider users, physical conditions, competing solutions, and regulatory contexts.
3. **Draw the Markov blanket**: Identify the sensory states (how your invention receives information from the world) and the active states (how your invention changes the world). Draw these as the boundary between internal and external.
4. **Identify nested subsystems**: Does your invention contain subsystems with their own Markov blankets? Draw at least one level of nesting.

### Part 2: Inventor Agent Profile (10 minutes)

Analyze yourself as the inventive agent.

1. **Generative model inventory**: What knowledge, skills, and assumptions do you bring to this invention? List your three strongest relevant competencies and your three biggest knowledge gaps.
2. **Prior preferences**: What outcome are you hoping for? Be specific -- not just "a successful invention" but the specific states you prefer (e.g., "users report saving 30 minutes per day").
3. **Blind spot audit**: What aspects of the problem might you be systematically underestimating or ignoring because of your background? Identify at least two potential blind spots.

### Part 3: Prediction Error Hunt (15 minutes)

Identify the key uncertainties and prediction errors relevant to your invention.

1. **Problem validation errors**: What evidence would show that the problem you are solving is not actually a problem (or not as severe as you believe)?
2. **Mechanism errors**: What could go wrong with your proposed mechanism or approach? Where is your mental model most uncertain?
3. **User errors**: What might users do with your invention that you have not predicted? What assumptions about user behavior might be wrong?
4. **Rank by impact**: Order your prediction errors from most to least critical. Which one, if it turned out to be wrong, would most fundamentally threaten your invention?

### Part 4: Development Roadmap Sketch (20 minutes)

Create a preliminary staged development plan using expected free energy reasoning.

1. **Stage identification**: Define 3-5 development stages for your invention, from current state to a working, adopted solution. For each stage, name the primary uncertainty it resolves.
2. **Epistemic vs. pragmatic balance**: For each stage, indicate whether the primary value is epistemic (learning something critical) or pragmatic (advancing toward the goal). Early stages should be heavily epistemic.
3. **Decision tree**: Identify at least two branch points where different evidence would lead to different next steps. Write explicit criteria for each branch (e.g., "If user testing shows >70% task completion, proceed to manufacturing; if <40%, redesign the interface").
4. **First action**: Based on your roadmap, what is the single most valuable action you could take in the next week? This should be the action that most reduces the expected free energy of your project.

## Discussion

1. How did drawing the Markov blanket change your understanding of what your invention is? Did the boundary end up where you expected?
2. What was the most surprising blind spot you identified in Part 2? How might you address it?
3. Looking at your ranked prediction errors, what is the single cheapest experiment you could run to test the highest-ranked one?
4. Compare your roadmap with another student's (if available). How do the epistemic-pragmatic balances differ, and what does that reveal about the different stages of your projects?

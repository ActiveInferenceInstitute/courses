# Section 1: Invention Foundations -- Section Overview

## Learning Objectives

1. Understand inventions as adaptive systems with identifiable Markov blankets, sensory states, active states, and nested subsystems.
2. Profile the inventor as an Active Inference agent with generative models, prior preferences, and identity priors.
3. Apply the eight-topic Active Inference spine (Systems, Agents, Perception, Cognition, Action, Learning, Communication, Planning) to the practice of invention.
4. Connect foundational Active Inference concepts to real invention case studies spanning diverse domains.
5. Build a personal Invention Foundations Portfolio through cumulative lab exercises.

## Introduction

Every invention begins with a gap -- a difference between the world as it is and the world as the inventor believes it could be. Active Inference provides a rigorous framework for understanding this gap and the entire process by which inventors close it. This section establishes the foundational vocabulary, concepts, and tools that will carry through the entire course.

The Free Energy Principle tells us that any system that persists must minimize the difference between its model of the world and the world itself. An invention is precisely such a system: it embodies a model of how things work, senses its environment, acts upon it, and must continually adapt to remain viable. The inventor, too, is an adaptive agent -- sensing problems, constructing mental models, building prototypes, learning from failure, communicating discoveries, and planning under uncertainty.

Across eight modules, this section builds a complete Active Inference account of what inventions are and how inventors work. We begin with the ontological question -- what makes an invention a system? -- and proceed through the agent, perception, cognition, action, learning, communication, and planning dimensions. Each module connects formal Active Inference concepts to concrete invention practice through real-world case studies, from Edison's electric lighting system to SpaceX's rocket development program.

## Key Concepts

### 1. Inventions as Adaptive Systems (Module 01: Systems)

The first module establishes that inventions are not static objects but dynamic systems. Every invention maintains a Markov blanket -- a boundary between its internal mechanisms and its external environment. The mousetrap's trigger plate is a sensory state; its snapping bar is an active state. A smartphone senses through its touchscreen and acts through its display. Understanding where to draw this boundary is the first design decision in any inventive project. The module introduces nested system architecture (subsystems within systems) and the accuracy-complexity trade-off that governs how robust an invention will be across varied conditions.

### 2. The Inventor as Agent (Module 02: Agents)

With the system defined, the second module turns to the inventor. In Active Inference, an agent is any entity that maintains a generative model and acts to minimize surprise. The inventor's generative model encompasses their knowledge, skills, assumptions, and blind spots. The module explores how prior beliefs shape inventive capacity, how identity priors constrain what an inventor considers possible, and how multi-agent collaboration -- as exemplified by the Wright Brothers' complementary skills -- enables inventions that no single agent could produce.

### 3. Perception and Evidence Gathering (Modules 03-04: Perception and Cognition)

Modules 03 and 04 develop the cognitive foundations of invention. Perception is the inventor's ability to detect prediction errors -- gaps between what the world offers and what people need. Great inventors see what others miss, not because they have better eyes but because they have different generative models that make different predictions, producing different prediction errors. Cognition extends this into causal reasoning: once a problem is perceived, the inventor must reason about hidden causes, construct mental models of mechanisms, and reframe problems when direct approaches fail. The Dyson vacuum story illustrates reframing -- shifting from "the motor is too weak" to "the bag is the problem."

### 4. Action, Learning, and Communication (Modules 05-07: Action, Learning, Communication)

Modules 05 through 07 cover the outer loop of inventive practice. Action is prototyping -- making the mental model physical and testable. Every prototype is a hypothesis about how the world works, and every test is an experiment that generates evidence. Learning is the systematic updating of the generative model based on that evidence, distinguishing parameter learning (tuning what you have) from structure learning (fundamentally changing your approach). Communication extends the individual inventor's model to other agents -- pitching to investors, documenting for collaborators, and instructing users. The polio vaccine story shows how Salk's communication strategy was as important as the science itself.

### 5. Planning Under Uncertainty (Module 08: Planning)

The final module addresses the temporal dimension. Invention projects unfold over time under deep uncertainty. Active Inference provides expected free energy as a principled framework for planning: each candidate action is evaluated by both its pragmatic value (does it advance the goal?) and its epistemic value (does it reduce uncertainty?). The SpaceX case study demonstrates staged development -- deferring irreversible decisions until critical uncertainties are resolved through evidence.

## Applications

### Application 1: The "Bring Your Own Invention" Framework

This section's lab exercises use an inclusive pedagogical design where every student applies Active Inference concepts to their own invention project, regardless of domain. A baker inventing a new kitchen tool, an engineer designing a sensor, an artist creating an interactive installation, and a social entrepreneur designing a community program all use the same framework. The Markov blanket analysis (Module 01 lab) works for any invention because every invention has boundaries, sensory states, and active states. The inventor agent profile (Module 02 lab) works for any inventor because every inventor has a generative model with priors and blind spots. This universality is not accidental -- it follows directly from the generality of the Free Energy Principle.

### Application 2: The Cumulative Portfolio

The eight labs in this section produce a cumulative Invention Foundations Portfolio: a system map, an agent profile, a prediction error hunt, a causal model, a minimum viable prototype, an iteration log, a communication package, and an adaptive roadmap. Together, these outputs constitute a complete Active Inference analysis of the student's invention and a concrete plan for developing it further. This portfolio serves as the foundation for Section 2 (Creative Engineering), where students apply creative process techniques to refine and extend their inventions.

## Conclusion

Invention Foundations establishes the conceptual bedrock for the entire course. By understanding inventions as adaptive systems, inventors as inference agents, and the inventive process as a perception-cognition-action-learning cycle, students gain a principled methodology that applies across every domain of invention. The section moves from ontology (what is an invention?) through agency (who is the inventor?) to the full cycle of perception, cognition, action, learning, communication, and planning. The next section -- Creative Engineering -- builds on this foundation by examining how the creative process itself operates through Active Inference.

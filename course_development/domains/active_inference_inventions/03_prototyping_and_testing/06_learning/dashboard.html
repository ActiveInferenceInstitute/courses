Core Concepts

1. Parameter vs. Structure Learning

Parameter learning: tuning values within a fixed model (adjust spring constant from 5 to 4.2 N/m). Structure learning: changing the model's causal architecture (discovering that aeroelastic flutter exists and adding it to the bridge model). Structure learning is rarer but more transformative — it adds entirely new understanding.

2. Post-Mortem Analysis

Five steps: (1) establish facts without interpretation, (2) compare to predictions, (3) attribute causes using competing hypotheses, (4) extract parameter and structure updates, (5) assess transferability. The Army's After Action Review asks: what was supposed to happen, what actually happened, why the difference, and what to learn.

3. Failure Mode Cataloging

Systematic records of how prototypes fail: date, failure description, failure mode, root cause, severity, detectability, lesson learned, and countermeasure. FMEA methodology originated in the 1940s military. Over time, the catalog reveals patterns that prevent repeated mistakes and enable failure anticipation in new designs.

4. Success Analysis

Positive outcomes also carry information: why did it work? Were the right factors responsible, or was success due to luck? 3M's Post-it Note adhesive was classified as a failure (too weak) until Art Fry recognized it as a success for a different application. Success analysis confirms model parameters and reveals untested assumptions.

5. Transfer Learning via Abstraction

The highest form of learning is transfer: applying lessons from one context to another. The abstraction ladder climbs from specific observation to proximate cause to general mechanism to design principle to meta-principle. "ABS breaks at -10C" is specific; "test materials at environmental extremes" transfers to every project. Experienced inventors carry richer models from accumulated transfers.

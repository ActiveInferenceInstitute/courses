# Lab: The Prototype Test Cycle

## Objective

Execute a complete prototype-test-iterate cycle for your invention project. You will build a targeted prototype, conduct a structured test, analyze the results using Bayesian reasoning, and produce a documented iteration plan.

## Prerequisites

- Completion of Sections 1 and 2 labs, including your system map, prediction error inventory, and creative engineering outputs
- An invention project with at least one testable hypothesis
- Basic prototyping materials appropriate to your invention domain

## Materials

- Your ranked prediction error list from Section 1
- Prototyping materials: paper, cardboard, tape, markers, modeling clay, foam core, or digital prototyping tools
- A test participant (another student, friend, or colleague) who is not familiar with your invention
- Timer, notebook or digital document for recording observations
- A3 or large sheet of paper for the documentation exercise

## Procedure

### Part 1: Hypothesis Selection and Prototype Design (15 minutes)

1. **Select the critical hypothesis**: From your prediction error inventory, choose the single most critical uncertainty -- the one whose resolution would most change your development path.
2. **State the hypothesis**: Write it as a testable prediction. (Example: "An untrained user can assemble this device in under 3 minutes using only the included instructions.")
3. **Define success and failure criteria**: What specific observation would confirm the hypothesis? What would disconfirm it? Write explicit criteria before testing.
4. **Choose prototype fidelity**: What is the minimum prototype that would generate useful evidence? Define what is included in the prototype's Markov blanket (being tested) and what is excluded (assumed).
5. **Build the prototype**: Construct it using available materials. Prioritize testability over aesthetics. Time limit: 10 minutes.

### Part 2: Structured Testing (15 minutes)

1. **Pre-test prediction**: Before the test, write down exactly what you expect to happen. Be specific about user behavior, performance metrics, and potential failure modes.
2. **Conduct the test**: Have your test participant interact with the prototype. Provide only the information a real user would have. Do not guide, prompt, or help unless they are completely stuck.
3. **Silent observation**: During the test, record everything you observe without interpreting it. Use the following signal categories:
   - **Quantitative**: Measurable values (time to complete, number of errors, measurements)
   - **Behavioral**: What the user actually did (sequence of actions, hesitations, workarounds)
   - **Verbal**: What the user said, especially unprompted comments
   - **Failure modes**: Any point where the prototype or the user's interaction broke down
4. **Post-test interview**: Ask three questions: (a) What was easy? (b) What was confusing? (c) What would you change?

### Part 3: Analysis and Bayesian Updating (15 minutes)

1. **Compare predictions to observations**: Go through your pre-test predictions one by one. For each, note whether the observation matched, partially matched, or contradicted the prediction.
2. **Identify key prediction errors**: Which observations most strongly contradicted your expectations? These are the highest-information signals.
3. **Update your model**: Based on the evidence, what specific aspects of your generative model need updating? Classify each update as:
   - **Parameter update**: Adjusting a value within the current approach (e.g., "make the handle 2 cm longer")
   - **Structural update**: Changing a fundamental assumption (e.g., "users do not read instructions, so the design must be self-explanatory")
4. **Pivot-or-persevere assessment**: Does the overall evidence support continuing with your current approach (persevere with parameter updates) or suggest a fundamentally different approach (pivot with structural updates)?

### Part 4: A3 Documentation (15 minutes)

Document your complete test cycle on a single A3 sheet (or equivalent large page) following this structure:

1. **Background**: One-sentence description of your invention and the problem it solves
2. **Hypothesis tested**: The specific prediction you tested
3. **Prototype description**: What you built and what it did/did not include
4. **Key observations**: The 3-5 most important observations from the test, organized by signal type
5. **Analysis**: What the evidence tells you about your generative model -- what was confirmed, what was disconfirmed
6. **Next actions**: The specific changes you will make based on this evidence, classified as parameter or structural updates
7. **Next test**: The hypothesis you will test next, and why it is now the highest-priority uncertainty

## Discussion

1. How did your pre-test predictions compare to the actual observations? What surprised you most, and what does that surprise reveal about your generative model's blind spots?
2. Was the prototype fidelity appropriate for the hypothesis, or did you find yourself wanting more or less detail? How would you adjust fidelity for the next test?
3. Did the test participant interact with the prototype in ways you did not anticipate? What does unexpected user behavior teach that no amount of mental simulation could?
4. Look at your A3 documentation. Could another inventor understand your reasoning and continue your work based solely on this document? If not, what is missing?

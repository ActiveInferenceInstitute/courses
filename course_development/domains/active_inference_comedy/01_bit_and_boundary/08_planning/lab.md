# Lab: Planning a Comedy Routine

## Objective

Plan, structure, and refine a short comedy routine from scratch, applying Active Inference planning principles.

## Materials

* Pen and paper (or text editor)
* Timer
* A partner or small audience for testing
* Optional: recording device

## Prerequisites

* Module 08: Planning lecture (module.md)
* Recommended: completion of prior labs in this course

## Steps

### Part 1: Choose Your Ambiguity (10 minutes)

1. Choose a word or phrase that has two meanings — one that works as a name or title and one that is an ordinary word. Examples:
   - "Patience" (a name / a virtue)
   - "Justice" (a name or title / a concept)
   - "Coach" (a name / a vehicle or trainer)
   - "Swift" (a name / fast)
2. Build a scenario around this word: who uses it as a name, who uses it as the ordinary meaning, and what context brings them together?

### Part 2: Planning the Architecture (15 minutes)

3. Plan your routine's temporal structure:
   - **Setup** (exchanges 1-3): Introduce the ambiguity naturally. The audience should begin to sense the double meaning. The confusion should feel accidental.
   - **Development** (exchanges 4-8): Escalate the confusion. Introduce variations, tags, and complications. Consider adding a second ambiguous word.
   - **Blow** (exchange 9-10): Design the climactic moment — the exchange where the accumulated confusion reaches maximum prediction error.
4. For each exchange, note:
   - What does Speaker A (the straight man) intend?
   - What does Speaker B (the comic) understand?
   - What does the audience know?
5. Explicitly plan one moment of **near-resolution** — a point where the comic almost understands — followed by a plunge back into confusion.

### Part 3: Expected Free Energy Analysis (10 minutes)

6. For each of the straight man's planned utterances, evaluate:
   - Could this utterance be phrased differently to resolve the ambiguity? If so, what is the EFE cost of the alternative phrasing? (Low EFE = low comedy.)
   - Is this the utterance that maximizes the comic's expected confusion?
7. Identify any exchanges where the straight man's planned utterance is suboptimal — where a different phrasing would be funnier. Revise.

### Part 4: Performance Test (15 minutes)

8. Perform your routine with a partner. The straight man follows the plan; the comic improvises responses based on their (wrong) interpretation.
9. Record the performance. Note where the plan worked and where it didn't.
10. After the first performance, revise the plan: adjust timing, rephrase key utterances, add or remove exchanges.
11. Perform a second time with the revisions. Compare.

### Part 5: Reflection (10 minutes)

12. Write a brief analysis:
   - How did the planned structure (setup → development → blow) affect the comedy?
   - Did the moment of near-resolution (Step 5) amplify the subsequent confusion?
   - How did the second performance differ from the first? What did you learn from the first audience test?
   - Compare your planning process to Abbott's implied planning in "Who's on First?"

## Discussion Questions

* How much of your routine's comedy came from the plan vs. the improvised responses of the comic? Is planning necessary for comedy, or can it emerge spontaneously?
* If you were to perform this routine 100 times, how would you expect it to change? What would converge (become fixed) and what would vary?
* Abbott planned for maximum confusion; you planned for maximum prediction error. Are these the same thing?

## Wrap-Up

You have planned a comedy routine from scratch using Active Inference principles. The process — choosing an ambiguity, structuring the temporal arc, optimizing for expected free energy, testing with an audience, and revising — mirrors the real-world process by which comedy routines are developed. This concludes the Bit & Boundary labs. In Course 2 (Straight & Setup), you will examine these same dynamics from the performer's embodied perspective.

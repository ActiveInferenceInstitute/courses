# Module 08: Planning in Comedy

1. Model Abbott's sequencing of names and questions as an **expected free energy** optimization.

## Introduction

Abbott is not just a straight man. He is a director — a planner who orchestrates the course of the routine with the strategic precision of a chess master. He decides *when* to introduce each player name, *how* to phrase each assertion, and *where* to allow Costello moments of near-understanding before plunging him back into confusion.

In Active Inference, planning is the selection of action policies that minimize expected free energy over a temporal horizon. The agent evaluates possible futures and chooses the sequence of actions most likely to achieve its goals. Abbott's goal (within the routine) is to maximize Costello's confusion while maintaining his own composure. His planning is exquisite: each move is designed to build tension, set up future confusion, and guide the routine toward its climactic blow.

## Key Concepts

### Expected Free Energy in Comedy Direction

**Expected free energy** (EFE) is the anticipated surprise and information gain associated with a future action policy. An agent planning its next action evaluates multiple possible policies and selects the one with the lowest expected free energy (or, in Abbott's case, the one that generates the highest expected free energy *for the other agent*).

When Abbott chooses to say 'Who' rather than 'The first baseman's name is Who spelled W-H-O,' he is selecting the policy with maximum EFE for Costello. The shorter utterance preserves the ambiguity that the longer one would resolve. Abbott is not being lazy or obtuse — he is executing a precision-optimized policy that keeps the comedic system running at maximum prediction error.

This is comedy direction as **adversarial planning**: the straight man's planning horizon extends across the entire routine, and each utterance is selected for its downstream effects on the comic's model and the audience's experience.

### Temporal Structure: Setup, Development, Blow

Every well-planned comedy routine has a temporal architecture:

1. **Setup phase**: The initial conditions are established. Abbott introduces the topic of the team. The audience and Costello enter the conversation with expectations.
2. **Development phase**: The confusion builds. Each exchange escalates the prediction error without resolving it. Abbott introduces Who, then What, then I Don't Know, layering ambiguities.
3. **Blow phase**: The routine reaches its climax — the moment of maximum prediction error, typically Costello's explosion of frustration or a catastrophic multi-name collision.

This temporal structure is not accidental. It is a planned trajectory through the state space of the comedic system. Abbott's planning determines the pacing: how fast to escalate, when to introduce new names, when to allow moments of near-resolution (I Don't Know on third — 'Third base!') that give the audience a breath before the next escalation.

The blow line is the routine's planned endpoint — the action that produces maximum prediction error given all the accumulated tension. A great blow is not just a final joke; it is the detonation of carefully planted information across the entire development phase.

### Planning for Repetition

The routine was performed thousands of times. Each performance is a re-execution of a planned policy — but planning for repetition introduces new challenges:

- **Audience adaptation**: Regular audiences (repeat viewers) have different generative models than first-time viewers. The planned policy must work for both.
- **Performance variation**: Within the planned structure, the performers vary timing, emphasis, and specific word choices. This variation is itself planned — it is the policy's built-in exploration parameter.
- **Self-monitoring**: The performers must plan their own emotional states. Abbott must plan to appear patient; Costello must plan to appear confused. The emotion is genuine (good performers feel their characters) but it is also managed by a higher-level planning process that monitors the routine's trajectory.

A mature comedy routine is like a flight plan: the destination is known, the waypoints are set, but the specific altitude and speed vary with conditions. The planning is never complete — it is always responsive to the audience's real-time reactions, making it a form of **adaptive planning** or **model predictive control**.

## Applications

- **Set Construction as Policy Design**: A comedian building a set for a 20-minute performance is designing an action policy: which jokes go first (warmup, low risk), which go in the middle (escalation, audience trust established), and which go last (closer, maximum impact). This set construction is expected free energy optimization over a 20-minute horizon.

- **Career Planning as Long-Horizon Decision Making**: A comedian's career is a planning problem at the scale of years: developing material, building an audience, choosing performance contexts, and adapting to cultural change. The 'voice' of a mature comedian is the converged policy of a long-horizon planning process.

## Conclusion

Abbott doesn't just perform — he plans. Every utterance is the output of a policy selected for its downstream effects on Costello, the audience, and the routine's trajectory. The temporal structure of the routine (setup, development, blow) is a planned architecture that maximizes cumulative prediction error. Planning in comedy is planning for surprise — and Abbott is the master planner, guiding the routine through a state space of escalating confusion toward a climax that feels both inevitable and unexpected.

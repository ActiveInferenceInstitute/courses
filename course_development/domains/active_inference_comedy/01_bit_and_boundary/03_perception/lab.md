# Lab: Hearing "Who"

## Objective

Conduct a perceptual experiment to test how prior beliefs shape the parsing of ambiguous words, using your own auditory system as the laboratory.

## Materials

* A recording of "Who's on First?" (audio only preferred for Part 1)
* A partner
* Headphones (optional but recommended)
* Pen and paper
* Timer / stopwatch

## Prerequisites

* Reading of Module 03: Perception lecture (module.md)
* Familiarity with the terms: likelihood, prior, posterior, likelihood degeneracy

## Steps

### Part 1: The Prior-Priming Experiment (15 minutes)

1. **Before listening**, have your partner read you the following priming sentence: "I'm going to tell you the names of the baseball players. The first baseman's name is a common English word you wouldn't expect as a name." Now listen to the first 30 seconds of "Who's on First?"
2. Note how quickly you resolve the ambiguity. Write down the moment you "got it."
3. Reset. Have your partner read you a different priming sentence: "I'm going to play you a conversation where someone is trying to find out the names of the players, but the manager is being evasive." Listen to the first 30 seconds again.
4. Note how your perception differs. Under this second prime, does "Who's on first" sound like a question or a fact?
5. Record your observations: how did the prior (the priming sentence) change your perceptual inference of the identical auditory stimulus?

### Part 2: The Ambiguity Inventory (15 minutes)

1. Listen to the routine from 0:00 to 2:00. Each time a word appears that is simultaneously a name and an ordinary word, mark it on your transcript:
   * "Who" — name / question word
   * "What" — name / question word
   * "I Don't Know" — name / expression of ignorance
   * Any others you can identify
2. For each ambiguous word, rate the **degree of degeneracy** on a 1–5 scale: how easily could the word be mistaken for the ordinary meaning? (5 = extremely easy, 1 = unlikely)
3. Rank the ambiguous words by comedic effectiveness. Is the most degenerate word the funniest? Why or why not?

### Part 3: Prosody Analysis (10 minutes)

1. Listen carefully to Abbott's delivery of "Who's on first" in three different instances in the recording. Note differences in:
   * Pitch contour (rising = question, flat/falling = statement)
   * Stress pattern (emphasis on "Who" vs. "first")
   * Speed (deliberate vs. casual)
2. For each instance, assess: is Abbott's prosody more consistent with a statement or a question? How does his prosody help maintain the ambiguity without resolving it?

### Part 4: Cross-Modal Perception (10 minutes)

1. Watch a *video* version of the routine (with Abbott's and Costello's faces visible). Note how facial expressions, gestures, and body language provide additional perceptual evidence:
    * Does Abbott's face look like someone asking a question or stating a fact?
    * Does Costello's face look like someone hearing a name or hearing a question?
2. Compare this to the audio-only experience. Does the visual channel resolve any of the ambiguity, or does it add a new layer?

### Part 5: Designing Your Own Degenerate Observation (10 minutes)

1. With your partner, identify three English words that are simultaneously common names and common words (e.g., "Penny," "Clay," "Sage").
2. Improvise a 1-minute exchange where one partner uses the word as a name and the other interprets it as the common meaning.
3. Reflect: how hard was it to maintain the ambiguity? What makes "Who" a better candidate than most words?

## Discussion Questions

* Did the priming sentence in Part 1 change your perception of the *same* auditory stimulus? What does this tell you about the role of priors in perception?
* Is "I Don't Know" more or less ambiguous than "Who"? Why does the routine place it on third base rather than first?
* Could Abbott resolve the ambiguity through prosody alone (e.g., saying "WHO — it's his name — is on first")? Why is this resolution not in the routine?
* How does this lab change your understanding of everyday misunderstandings?

## Wrap-Up

You have tested the perceptual engine of "Who's on First?" on yourself and found that prior beliefs shape perception even when the acoustic input is identical. This is Active Inference in action: perception is not hearing — it is inferring the hidden state that best explains the observation, given your model of the world.

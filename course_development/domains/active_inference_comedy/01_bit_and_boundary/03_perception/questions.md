# Study Questions: Perception

1. Explain **likelihood degeneracy** using the word "Who" in the routine. Why does a single phoneme sequence support two incompatible perceptual inferences?

2. Abbott and Costello hear the same words but perceive different meanings. Is either of them perceiving "incorrectly"? Justify your answer using Bayesian inference.

3. How does the **prior** determine which parse of "Who" wins in each agent's perception? Write out the Bayesian equation informally for both Abbott's and Costello's cases.

4. The routine also uses "What" (second base) and "I Don't Know" (third base). Analyze the likelihood degeneracy of each. Is "I Don't Know" more or less degenerate than "Who"? Why?

5. When Abbott says "Who" and Costello hears a question, there is no *acoustic* difference between the name and the question word. What does this tell us about the role of context in perception?

6. Comedic timing involves inserting pauses at strategic moments. Explain how a pause functions as a **precision accumulation window** for the audience's perceptual inference.

7. A deaf audience member watching the routine with sign language interpretation would perceive the words through a different modality. Would the likelihood degeneracy survive translation into sign language? Why or why not?

8. In Active Inference, perception is described as "inverting the generative model." Explain what this means using Costello's perception of "Who's on first" as the example.

9. The audience perceives *both* parses of "Who" simultaneously. In Active Inference terms, the audience maintains a **bimodal posterior** — two competing hypotheses with roughly equal probability. How is this different from what Abbott and Costello experience?

10. Consider the moment when Costello first hears "Who's on first." His initial perception is presumably the question parse. Could a different opening phrasing by Abbott have biased Costello's prior toward the name parse? What would that phrasing look like?

11. Mishearing gags are a staple of comedy ("I said 'dock the boat,' not 'rock the boat!'"). Compare the perceptual mechanism of a mishearing gag to the mechanism in "Who's on First?" Which relies more on acoustic similarity vs. semantic ambiguity?

12. Some comedy works across languages; some does not. "Who's on First?" depends on specific English-language ambiguities. Identify the perceptual features that would need to be preserved in a translation for the routine to work.

13. How does **prosody** (intonation, stress, rhythm) affect the perception of "Who's on first"? Could Abbott change his prosody to resolve the ambiguity? Why doesn't he?

14. A baby hearing "Who's on first" for the first time has no prior for either parse. What would the baby's perceptual inference look like? No prior = maximally uncertain posterior.

15. In the routine, Abbott sometimes says "Who" with emphasis ("WHO is on first!"). Does this emphasis change the likelihood, the prior, or both? How does it affect Costello's perception?

16. Perception in Active Inference generates **prediction errors** when observations don't match predictions. In the routine, who experiences the most prediction error: Abbott, Costello, or the audience?

17. Visual perception can be manipulated by comedians through gesture, facial expression, and body language. How might Abbott's body language reinforce his verbal perception of "Who" as a name?

18. Consider a comedy routine that relies on a visual ambiguity (e.g., a duck/rabbit figure). Map its perceptual structure onto the "Who's on First?" auditory ambiguity. What is the analogous degenerate observation?

19. If you heard "Who's on First?" for the first time in a noisy environment where every other word was obscured, how would your perceptual inference change? Would the comedy survive partial information?

20. Reflect on your own perception when you first encountered "Who's on First?" How quickly did you resolve the name/question ambiguity? What does the speed of your resolution tell you about your prior?

# Practice Quiz: Perception

## Part A: Multiple Choice

1. The word "Who" in "Who's on First?" is an example of:
A) An observation with a unique likelihood mapping to one hidden state
B) A degenerate observation consistent with two incompatible hidden states
C) An external state that cannot be perceived
D) A blanket state with no informational content

2. Costello's perception of "Who" as a question word is:
A) Irrational and poorly reasoned
B) Bayes-optimal given his prior, which assigns high probability to "Who" being a question word
C) Evidence that his Markov blanket is broken
D) A deliberate misinterpretation

3. The audience's perception of "Who" in the routine is best characterized as:
A) Identical to Abbott's perception
B) Identical to Costello's perception
C) A bimodal inference tracking both the name parse and the question parse simultaneously
D) Confused and unable to resolve the ambiguity

4. A comedic pause after a setup functions perceptually as:
A) Dead air that kills the joke
B) A precision accumulation window that increases the amplitude of subsequent prediction error
C) An internal state of the performer
D) A Markov blanket disruption

5. If "Who's on First?" were translated into a language where the question word for "who" sounds nothing like any possible name, the routine would fail because:
A) The audience would be smarter
B) The likelihood degeneracy would be eliminated — the observation could only map to one hidden state
C) The performers would refuse to do it
D) Comedy cannot survive translation

6. Mishearing gags and "Who's on First?" both rely on:
A) Acoustic noise
B) Ambiguous observations that support multiple perceptual inferences
C) The audience being unable to hear
D) Visual ambiguity

7. Abbott's prosody (tone, emphasis, rhythm) when saying "Who's on first" is:
A) Random and irrelevant to perception
B) Calibrated to maintain the ambiguity — declarative enough to express a fact, but not explicit enough to resolve the name/question parse
C) Always identical across performances
D) Designed to confuse the audience

## Part B: Short Answer

1. Write out, informally, the Bayesian inference that Costello performs when he hears "Who's on first." Include the prior P(question word), the likelihood P("Who" | question word), and the posterior. Then do the same for Abbott's inference. Why do they reach different conclusions from the same observation?

2. The audience holds both parses of "Who" simultaneously — a bimodal posterior. Explain why this dual-tracking is essential for comedy, and what would happen if the audience only tracked one parse.

3. Design a simple comedy bit that exploits a *visual* likelihood degeneracy (where the same visual observation supports two incompatible interpretations). Describe the setup, the degenerate observation, and the punchline.

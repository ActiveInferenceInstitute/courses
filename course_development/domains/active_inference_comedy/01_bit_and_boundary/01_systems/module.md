# Module 01: Systems in Comedy

## Learning Objectives

1. Identify the boundary between the internal logic of a comedy routine and the external world using the concept of a **Markov blanket**.
2. Recognize how performer, words, and audience form an interacting system with internal and external states.
3. Map the components of "Who's on First?" onto the formal structure of a system in Active Inference.

## Introduction

Press play on any recording of "Who's on First?" — the 1945 film version, the radio broadcasts, the television appearances. Within seconds, a system snaps into existence. Abbott speaks. Costello responds. The audience listens. And something holds together that is more than the sum of these parts: a bounded, self-sustaining, dynamical system that generates escalating confusion and laughter for as long as the performers choose to maintain it.

In Active Inference, a **system** is anything that maintains itself as distinct from its surroundings. A comedy routine does exactly this. It has an inside — the internal logic of the bit, the performers' beliefs, the accumulated history of exchanges. It has an outside — the audience's prior knowledge, the venue, the cultural moment. And it has a boundary — the dialogue itself, the words spoken aloud — where inside and outside meet.

This module invites you to look at "Who's on First?" not as a collection of jokes, but as a living system. Where does the routine end and the world begin? What role does the spoken word play at the boundary? And how does the simple act of saying "Who's on first" create and maintain a system that has persisted for nearly a century?

## Key Concepts

### 1. The Dialogue as Markov Blanket

In Active Inference, a **Markov blanket** is the statistical boundary that separates an agent's internal states from the external world. It consists of **sensory states** (how the outside affects the inside) and **active states** (how the inside affects the outside).

In "Who's on First?", this boundary is the dialogue. Every word Abbott speaks is an active state — an output from his internal model that impacts the external world (Costello, the audience). Every word Costello hears is a sensory state — an input from the external world that impacts his internal beliefs. The audience perceives the routine *only through* this boundary. They cannot directly read Abbott's intention or Costello's confusion — they infer these internal states from the words crossing the Markov blanket.

Consider the opening exchange:

> **Abbott**: "Who's on first."  
> **Costello**: "That's what I want to find out."

Two sentences. Two states crossing the blanket. Abbott's internal state (the certain knowledge that a player named "Who" plays first base) produces an active state (the utterance "Who's on first"). Costello's sensory state (hearing the phoneme sequence /huːz ɒn fɜːrst/) collides with his internal model (which parses "Who" as a question word), producing a response that reveals his model's state. The audience detects the mismatch *through* these public boundary states and generates a prediction error that will eventually become laughter.

### 2. Internal States: The Logic Inside the Routine

The **internal states** of the "Who's on First?" system are everything shielded from the external world by the Markov blanket. These include:

- **Abbott's generative model**: He knows the players' names (Who, What, I Don't Know). His model has high precision — he is certain of the mapping between names and positions. This certainty is never visible to Costello directly; it only manifests through Abbott's utterances at the blanket boundary.

- **Costello's generative model**: He believes "Who," "What," and "I Don't Know" are ordinary English words — pronouns and phrases, not proper nouns. His model is structurally reasonable but referentially wrong. This wrong model is also hidden; it manifests as confused questions at the boundary.

- **The accumulated exchange history**: As the routine progresses, the system accumulates internal states — the prior exchanges have happened, and both performers carry forward the memory (or, in Costello's case, the mounting frustration) of failed communication.

These internal states are the hidden generative models that drive the system. They are never directly observed by the audience — only their consequences, expressed at the boundary as dialogue, are perceptible.

### 3. External States: Audience, Culture, and Context

The **external states** of the "Who's on First?" system include everything outside the Markov blanket:

- **The audience**: The most important external state. The audience maintains its own generative model — one that is typically superior to both performers'. The audience knows (or quickly figures out) that "Who" is a name. They hold the comic irony: they can see that Costello's model is wrong, that Abbott's model is right, and that the mismatch will never resolve. The audience's laughter is an external state that feeds back into the system, modulating the performers' timing and energy.

- **The cultural context**: The routine depends on the audience sharing certain linguistic conventions (English syntax, the cultural role of baseball, the possibility of unusual names). In a culture without baseball or without the question-word "Who," the routine's Markov blanket would fail — the system couldn't form.

- **The venue**: The acoustics, the lighting, the size of the room, the warmth of the audience — all are external states that affect the system without being part of its internal logic.

## Applications

- **The Rule of Three as System Mechanics**: Many comedy structures create mini-systems with three exchanges: two to establish internal states (the pattern), and one to disrupt the boundary (the violation). "Who's on First?" is a hyper-extended version: the "rule of three" repeats *recursively*, with each player name creating a new mini-system of confusion nested inside the larger routine.

- **Bombing as System Dissolution**: When a routine fails — when the jokes don't land, when the audience is silent — the Markov blanket weakens. The system's internal logic persists (the performer still delivers the lines), but the external world stops responding. The system collapses because its boundary is no longer coupled to its environment. A bomb is the comedy equivalent of a crochet project with the live loop slipping off the hook.

## Conclusion

"Who's on First?" is a system in the Active Inference sense: it has internal states (the performers' competing generative models), external states (the audience, the culture, the venue), and a Markov blanket (the dialogue, where words cross between hidden beliefs and public behavior). Understanding this structure helps us see why the routine *holds together* — why it is bounded, self-sustaining, and resistant to dissolution across decades of performance.

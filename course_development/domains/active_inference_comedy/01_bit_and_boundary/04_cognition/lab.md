# Lab: Cognitive Traps in Comedy

## Objective

Experience belief perseveration firsthand through an ambiguity game, then analyze the cognitive dynamics using Active Inference concepts.

## Materials

* 3–4 participants
* Index cards or slips of paper
* Timer
* Recording device (optional)

## Prerequisites

* Module 04: Cognition lecture (module.md)

## Steps

### Part 1: The Name Game (20 minutes)

1. One participant secretly writes four words on index cards: common English words that could plausibly be names (e.g., "Chance," "Joy," "Stone," "Faith"). These are "player names."
2. The card-holder (playing Abbott) introduces the scenario: "Let me tell you about our new team. Chance is the pitcher, Joy is the catcher, Stone is shortstop, and Faith is outfield."
3. Another participant (playing Costello) must try to learn the names — but they don't know the words are names. They ask questions naturally.
4. The remaining participants are the audience. They observe and note when they detect the cognitive mismatch.
5. Run the exchange for 3–5 minutes. Don't break character.

### Part 2: Cognitive Analysis (15 minutes)

6. After the exercise, each participant writes answers to:
   - **Abbott**: How hard was it to maintain your model? Did you ever want to clarify?
   - **Costello**: At what point did you realize the words were names? If you never realized, what do you think was happening?
   - **Audience**: When did you first detect the cognitive gap? Was it immediately or did it take time?
7. Compare these experiences to the module's concepts: belief perseveration, precision maintenance, and meta-cognition.

### Part 3: Model Evidence Analysis (10 minutes)

8. For each ambiguous word used in Part 1, evaluate:
   - How compatible was the word with the "ordinary meaning" model? (High = hard to detect as a name)
   - How compatible was the word with the "name" model?
   - Is higher compatibility with both models (higher degeneracy) associated with longer perseveration?

### Part 4: Breaking the Trap (10 minutes)

9. Replay the exercise, but this time Abbott deliberately introduces one piece of disambiguating evidence midway through (e.g., "Chance — that's his actual name, C-H-A-N-C-E"). Note:
   - Does Costello's model update?
   - Does the comedy survive the disambiguation?
   - What does this tell you about the role of irresolvable ambiguity in the routine's comedy?

## Discussion Questions

* Was belief perseveration in your exercise rational or irrational? Could Costello (your version) have done better with the same evidence?
* How does the curse of knowledge feel from Abbott's perspective? Did you experience frustration at Costello's inability to understand?
* The audience detected the mismatch before Costello did. Is this always the case in cognitive comedy? What advantages does the third-party observer have?
* How does this exercise change your understanding of everyday miscommunication?

## Wrap-Up

You have experienced the cognitive trap at the heart of "Who's on First?" from the inside. Belief perseveration is not a bug — it is rational inference under ambiguous evidence. The comedy emerges from the gap between agents' models, and the audience's meta-cognitive perspective makes the gap legible and laughable.

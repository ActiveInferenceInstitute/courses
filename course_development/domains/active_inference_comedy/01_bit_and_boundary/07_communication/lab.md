# Lab: Communication Breakdown Workshop

## Objective

Create, perform, and analyze a communication-failure comedy exercise, applying Gricean maxim analysis and Active Inference concepts.

## Materials

* A partner (required)
* Paper and pen
* Recording device (optional)
* Timer

## Prerequisites

* Module 07: Communication lecture (module.md)
* Familiarity with Gricean maxims (Quality, Quantity, Relation, Manner)

## Steps

### Part 1: Identifying Maxim Violations (10 minutes)

1. With your partner, read through the first 10 exchanges of "Who's on First?" transcript.
2. For each exchange, label which Gricean maxim(s) are being violated and by whom:
   - Q = Quality (truthfulness)
   - Qn = Quantity (informativeness)
   - R = Relation (relevance)
   - M = Manner (clarity)
3. Note: Abbott rarely violates Q — he tells the truth. His primary violation is M (ambiguity).

### Part 2: Designing Your Own Communication Failure (15 minutes)

4. Choose a context where name-word ambiguity could create confusion. Examples:
   - A restaurant where the dishes are named after emotions ("I'll have the Confusion." "You're already confused." "No, I want the dish called Confusion.")
   - A tech company where the products are named after common actions ("Can I try Run?" "Sure, run where?" "No, the product called Run.")
5. Write a 10-exchange script with your partner, where:
   - Speaker A uses the word as a name (following Abbott's pattern)
   - Speaker B uses the word in its ordinary meaning (following Costello's pattern)
   - The communication fails at Level 1 but succeeds at Level 2 (a hypothetical audience would understand the situation)

### Part 3: Multi-Channel Analysis (15 minutes)

6. Perform your script aloud with your partner. Record it if possible.
7. After performing, analyze the communicative channels:
   - **Verbal**: What words created the ambiguity?
   - **Prosodic**: Did Speaker A use declarative tone? Did Speaker B use interrogative tone?
   - **Gestural**: What body language accompanied the confusion?
   - **Timing**: Where did pauses fall? Did pauses amplify the comedy?
8. Identify any moments of **cross-channel prediction error** (where one channel contradicted another).

### Part 4: The Resolution Test (10 minutes)

9. Perform the script again, but this time Speaker A resolves the ambiguity at exchange 5 (e.g., "That's the name of the dish, not a description of your feelings.").
10. Note what happens to the comedy after resolution. Does it:
   - End immediately?
   - Continue with diminished energy?
   - Generate a new kind of comedy (the comedy of "oh, I see")?
11. Discuss: is irresolvable ambiguity essential for the routine's comedy, or can resolved ambiguity also be funny?

### Part 5: Audience Communication Test (10 minutes)

12. Show your recorded performance (or perform live) for a third person.
13. Ask them: "When did you understand the situation? Did you understand before the characters did?" 
14. Their answer should confirm that Level 2 communication (performer-to-audience) is working even while Level 1 communication (character-to-character) fails.

## Discussion Questions

* In your exercise, which Gricean maxim did Speaker A primarily violate? Was it the same as Abbott's violation (Manner)?
* Does your routine work as well as "Who's on First?" If not, what does the original have that yours lacks? (Consider: repetition, escalation, the specific quality of the name-words.)
* If you performed your routine for someone who does not speak English, would Level 2 communication still succeed? What minimum shared knowledge is required?

## Wrap-Up

You have designed, performed, and analyzed a communication-failure comedy routine. The exercise demonstrates that comedy often works *because* communication fails at one level while succeeding at another — and that this dual-level structure requires both Gricean awareness and Active Inference principles.

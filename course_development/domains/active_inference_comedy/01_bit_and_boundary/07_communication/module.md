# Module 07: Communication in Comedy

## Learning Objectives

1. Analyze 'Who's on First?' as a systematic **violation of Gricean maxims** of communication.
2. Distinguish between communication failure (Costello and Abbott) and communicative success (performers and audience).
3. Apply the concept of **pragmatic inference** to comedic exchange.

## Introduction

'Who's on First?' is a communication disaster. Two people speak the same language, hear each other perfectly, and understand every word — yet they completely fail to communicate. Abbott cannot convey the names; Costello cannot extract them. By the end, both are exasperated, and the audience is in tears of laughter.

But at the meta-level, the routine is a communication *masterpiece*. Abbott and Costello (the performers, not the characters) communicate brilliantly with the audience. Every failed exchange between the characters is a successful exchange between the performers and the listeners. The communication failure is the communication.

This module examines how comedy exploits the machinery of human communication — the rules, expectations, and inferences that make language work — and what happens when that machinery is deliberately jammed.

## Key Concepts

### Gricean Maxims and Their Violation

Philosopher Paul Grice identified four maxims that govern cooperative conversation:

1. **Quality**: Be truthful. Don't say what you believe to be false.
2. **Quantity**: Be as informative as required. No more, no less.
3. **Relation**: Be relevant.
4. **Manner**: Be clear. Avoid ambiguity.

Abbott violates maxim 4 (manner) spectacularly: he is maximally ambiguous, using words that have two meanings without clarifying which one he intends. But he does *not* violate maxim 1 (quality): everything he says is true. Who *is* on first. What *is* on second. I Don't Know *is* on third. The lies are all in the communication channel, not in the content.

Costello, meanwhile, follows all four maxims impeccably. He is truthful (he really doesn't know), informative (he is telling Abbott what he needs), relevant (he is staying on topic), and clear (his questions are unambiguous from his perspective). The irony is that one agent's perfect cooperation cannot overcome the other agent's structural ambiguity.

The audience recognizes the maxim violation — they can see that Abbott's manner violates Grice's fourth maxim. This recognition is itself a form of pragmatic inference: the audience infers that the communication is failing *because* one agent is being systematically ambiguous, and this inference generates the prediction error that produces laughter.

### Two Levels of Communication

'Who's on First?' operates on two simultaneous communication levels:

**Level 1 (Character-to-Character)**: Abbott tries to tell Costello the names; Costello tries to extract them. This communication *fails*. Information does not transfer. Uncertainty does not reduce. The channel is jammed by the degenerate likelihood of the names.

**Level 2 (Performers-to-Audience)**: Abbott and Costello (as performers) communicate the *situation* to the audience. This communication *succeeds*. The audience understands perfectly: there is a man who knows the names, a man who doesn't, and names that sound like questions. The performers convey the structure of the confusion with exquisite clarity.

The comedy arises from the juxtaposition of these two levels: Level 1 fails while Level 2 succeeds. The audience experiences successful communication about failed communication. This is a recursive structure that depends on theory of mind at multiple levels.

### Comedic Communication Channels

Communication in comedy uses multiple channels simultaneously:

- **Verbal**: The words themselves (the primary source of ambiguity in 'Who's on First?')
- **Prosodic**: Intonation, stress, and rhythm (Abbott's patient declarative vs. Costello's rising interrogative)
- **Gestural**: Pointing, shrugging, throwing hands up (Costello's physical expressions of frustration)
- **Timing**: Pauses, rapid-fire exchanges, pregnant silences (the temporal modulation of information flow)
- **Facial**: Expressions of certainty (Abbott), confusion (Costello), and delight (audience)

Each channel carries information across the Markov blanket, and each channel can either reinforce or contradict the others. In 'Who's on First?', Abbott's verbal channel (ambiguous) contradicts his prosodic channel (declarative certainty), creating a cross-channel prediction error that the audience detects and enjoys.

## Applications

- **Inside Jokes as Compressed Communication**: An inside joke between friends is a maximally compressed communication — the setup is shared context, and the punchline is a minimal signal that activates the shared model. Inside jokes are high-precision, low-bandwidth communication.

- **Sarcasm as Deliberate Maxim Violation**: Sarcasm violates Grice's maxim of quality (the speaker says the opposite of what they mean), but the audience is expected to detect the violation and infer the true meaning. Like 'Who's on First?', the comedy depends on the listener successfully detecting the violation.

## Conclusion

Comedy is communication engineering. 'Who's on First?' is a masterclass in failed communication that succeeds at a higher level: the audience receives — perfectly — a demonstration of how communication breaks down when the fundamental assumption of shared meaning is violated. The routine shows us, with painful clarity, that communication depends not just on shared language but on shared priors. When the priors diverge, the words mean nothing — and that nothing is extremely funny.

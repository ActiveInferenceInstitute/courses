# Study Questions: Learning

1. "Who's on First?" has been funny for nearly 90 years. If comedy depends on prediction error, how can a routine still generate prediction error when the audience knows every word?

2. Describe the three levels of learning in the routine: (a) Costello's non-learning, (b) the audience's re-learning, (c) the performers' cross-performance learning.

3. Why can't Costello learn that "Who" is a name? Use the concept of **model evidence** — Costello's model has high evidence (the observations are compatible with his model), so there is no pressure to revise.

4. When you re-watch a routine you already know, you voluntarily **suppress your top-level knowledge** and re-engage with the ambiguity. What Active Inference mechanism is this? (Hint: it relates to precision adjustment on prior beliefs.)

5. Abbott and Costello refined the routine across thousands of performances. Describe this refinement as a **gradient descent** on the landscape of comedic free energy.

6. An open mic is described as "high exploration, low stakes." Map open mic performance onto the **exploration-exploitation tradeoff** in Active Inference.

7. A joke that was hilarious in 1990 may not be funny today. What has changed: the joke, the audience's generative model, or both?

8. Compare the learning trajectory of a comedian (from open mic to headliner) to the learning trajectory of a child acquiring language. What structural similarities exist in their free energy landscapes?

9. Why does physical comedy (slapstick) tend to age better than verbal comedy? Is it because physical prediction errors are less culturally dependent?

10. The "tight five" is described as the endpoint of a comedian's learning process. In Active Inference terms, a tight five is a **converged policy**. What does convergence mean in this context?

11. "Hack" comedy is defined as material that generates zero prediction error — the audience already knows the punchline. How does a joke transition from "fresh" to "hack"? Is this a learning process?

12. Costello's non-learning is the engine of the routine. If Costello learned (figured out that "Who" is a name), the routine would end. What does this tell us about the relationship between learning and comedy?

13. How do callbacks exploit the audience's **temporal model** — their memory of earlier parts of the set? A callback generates prediction error by reactivating a previously resolved belief.

14. When a comedian "works out" new material on stage, they are observing audience reactions and updating their model of what works. Describe this as a **Bayesian model selection** process.

15. Child audiences and adult audiences laugh at different things. How do the generative models of children and adults differ, and how do these differences change what counts as prediction error?

16. A parody works by exploiting the audience's prior knowledge of the original. If the audience hasn't seen the original, the parody fails. Analyze parody as a learning-dependent form of comedy.

17. Some comedians (like Mitch Hedberg) use the same material for their entire career. Others (like Chris Rock) constantly retire old material and develop new sets. Compare these strategies using Active Inference learning dynamics.

18. The concept of "too soon" — a joke about a recent tragedy that is funny in principle but offensive in practice — relates to the **benign** component of benign violation theory. How does temporal distance function as a learning variable?

19. If you watched "Who's on First?" backwards (last exchange first, first exchange last), would it be funny? What does this tell us about the role of sequential learning in comedy?

20. Design a comedy routine that specifically exploits the audience's *failure* to learn. How would you structure the routine so that the audience's model persistently fails to update?

# Lab: The Learning Experiment

## Objective

Test the relationship between prior knowledge and comedy by exposing participants to "Who's on First?" under different learning conditions and measuring the effects.

## Materials

* A recording of "Who's on First?" (audio or video)
* At least 3 participants, ideally 6+
* Paper and pen for each participant
* Timer

## Prerequisites

* Module 06: Learning lecture (module.md)

## Steps

### Part 1: The Learning Conditions (15 minutes)

1. Divide participants into three groups:
   - **Group A**: Has never heard "Who's on First?" before (or pretends not to have)
   - **Group B**: Has heard the routine 1-3 times
   - **Group C**: Has heard the routine many times and can quote parts of it
2. Before playing the recording, each participant rates their anticipated enjoyment on a 1-10 scale.
3. Play the routine (first 3 minutes) for all groups simultaneously, but seat them apart so they cannot hear each other's reactions.

### Part 2: Response Measurement (10 minutes)

4. After the recording, each participant answers:
   - When did you first laugh? (Approximate timestamp)
   - What was the funniest moment? (Describe it)
   - Rate your actual enjoyment on a 1-10 scale
   - Were you surprised at any point? If so, when?
5. Compare responses across groups. Look for:
   - Does Group A laugh first? (More prediction error from novelty)
   - Does Group C rate enjoyment lower? (Less surprise) Or higher? (Appreciation of craft)
   - Do the groups identify different "funniest moments"?

### Part 3: Repeated Exposure (15 minutes)

6. Play the recording a second time for all groups.
7. Each participant notes: what was different the second time? Did they laugh at different moments? Did they notice new features (timing, word choice, delivery quality)?
8. Rate enjoyment again on 1-10. Compare first-viewing and second-viewing ratings.

### Part 4: Learning Transfer (10 minutes)

9. After the second viewing, play a different comedy routine that uses name-confusion humor (if available) or describe a hypothetical one.
10. Does prior exposure to "Who's on First?" change participants' responses to the new routine? Are they quicker to "get it"? Are they less surprised?

### Part 5: The Key Question (10 minutes)

11. Group discussion: Why does the routine work for Group C — the people who already know everything? What is generating prediction error for them?
12. Each participant writes their answer using at least two Active Inference concepts (e.g., precision adjustment, micro-level prediction error, social inference, craft appreciation).

## Discussion Questions

* Was Group A's experience qualitatively different from Group C's? If so, in what ways?
* Did repeat exposure (second viewing) increase or decrease enjoyment? What mechanism explains the trend?
* How does the concept of "voluntary precision reduction" (choosing to re-engage with the ambiguity) explain Group C's continued enjoyment?

## Wrap-Up

You have tested the learning dynamics of comedy empirically. The results should show that comedy persists beyond novelty — that prediction error can be generated even from known material, through timing, social context, and voluntary re-engagement. Learning and comedy have a paradoxical relationship: comedy exploits surprise, but it survives learning.

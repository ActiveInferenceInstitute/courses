# Lab: The Dual-Track Mind

## Objective

Experience and measure dual-track cognition by performing a comedy exchange while simultaneously tracking a meta-level task, then analyze the cognitive load patterns and model-switching costs.

## Materials

* A partner (required)
* The exchange from the Module 02 Lab (or any short comedy exchange)
* A deck of playing cards or a printed number grid
* A stopwatch
* Blank paper and pen

## Prerequisites

* Reading of Module 04: Cognition in Straight & Setup (module.md)
* Completion of Modules 01-03 Labs
* Familiarity with: dual-track cognition, cognitive load, theory of mind

## Steps

### Part 1: Single-Track Baseline (10 minutes)

1. Perform your comedy exchange normally. Rate the cognitive effort on a 1-10 scale. This is your single-track baseline.
2. Now perform a counting task alone: count backwards from 100 by 7s (100, 93, 86...) for 30 seconds. Rate the cognitive effort on a 1-10 scale.
3. These two ratings establish your baselines for single-track performance.

### Part 2: Dual-Track Challenge (15 minutes)

1. Now perform the comedy exchange while ALSO keeping a mental running count of how many times your partner says a specific word (choose a common word like "I" or "you"). Perform the exchange and report your count at the end.
2. Rate the cognitive effort. Compare to your single-track baseline. This is dual-track cognition: running the character model AND a monitoring task simultaneously.
3. Repeat, but this time keep a running count of your OWN hesitations (any moment you pause, stumble, or slow down). This is meta-monitoring — the performer's meta-model tracking the character model's execution.
4. Compare the difficulty of monitoring your partner vs. monitoring yourself. Which meta-model task was harder?

### Part 3: Theory of Mind Exercise (10 minutes)

1. Before performing the next exchange, write a brief "audience prediction" — three specific things you predict the audience (or your partner) will find funny. Be specific: "They will laugh at the word 'garden' in line 7."
2. Perform the exchange. Check your predictions. How accurate was your theory of mind?
3. Write three things the audience found funny that you did NOT predict. These are theory-of-mind failures — moments where the audience's model diverged from your model of their model.

### Part 4: Model Switching (10 minutes)

1. Prepare a short "act-out" sequence: tell a brief story in third person (straight man mode), then switch to performing a character (comic mode), then switch back. For example: "So my neighbor comes over and says —" [switch to character voice/posture] "— 'I think your dog ate my newspaper!'" [switch back to narrator] "And I just looked at him."
2. Perform this three times. Each time, notice the moment of switching: what changes? Voice? Posture? Facial expression? Internal sense?
3. Time the switch. How long does it take to fully inhabit the new model? Does it get faster with practice?

### Part 5: Intentional Corpsing (5 minutes)

1. Perform the comedy exchange one more time. This time, try to make your partner break character (corpse). Use micro-signals: unexpected eye contact, subtle facial expressions, small timing changes.
2. If your partner corpses, note what triggered it. This is a meta-model breach — your meta-level signal broke through their character boundary.
3. Discuss: what was happening cognitively the moment you (or your partner) broke? Was the meta-model thought that triggered the break funny, or was it the SURPRISE of the meta-model intruding?

## Discussion Questions

* How did the dual-track challenge change the quality of your comedy performance? Did the character suffer when you were monitoring?
* The module claims rehearsal reduces lower-level cognitive load. Based on your experience across labs (increasing familiarity with the exchanges), do you agree?
* When you performed the theory-of-mind prediction exercise, how accurate were you? What would make you more accurate?
* During model switching (the act-out), did you experience a cognitive cost? Was it like switching languages, switching tasks, or something else?

## Wrap-Up

You have directly experienced dual-track cognition, measured its costs, practiced theory of mind prediction, and performed model switching. These cognitive operations — running in parallel, largely invisible to the audience — are the hidden engine of comedy performance.

# Lab: The Embodied Performer

## Objective

Explore the action repertoire of comedy performance through vocal parameter manipulation, physical comedy experiments, and timing exercises, experiencing how embodied actions cross the performance boundary.

## Materials

* A partner (recommended)
* A smartphone for recording (audio and video)
* A stopwatch
* A simple one-liner joke (provided below)
* Blank paper and pen

## Prerequisites

* Reading of Module 05: Action in Straight & Setup (module.md)
* Completion of Modules 01-04 Labs
* Familiarity with: vocal action, physical action, timing, precision weighting

## The Joke

Use this one-liner for all exercises:

**"I told my wife she was drawing her eyebrows too high. She looked surprised."**

## Steps

### Part 1: Vocal Parameters (15 minutes)

1. Record yourself delivering the joke in your normal voice. Play it back. This is your baseline delivery.
2. Now deliver the joke five different ways, recording each:
   * **Low and slow**: Deep voice, slow pace, falling intonation
   * **High and fast**: Higher voice, rapid pace, rising intonation
   * **Whispered**: Near-whisper, intimate, as if sharing a secret
   * **Deadpan**: Flat affect, no tonal variation, no emphasis
   * **Animated**: Exaggerated pitch variation, dramatic pauses, emphatic emphasis on key words
3. Play back all five recordings. For each, answer: What precision weight does this delivery encode? Does it make the joke funnier, less funny, or differently funny?
4. Which delivery works best for this specific joke? Why? What about the joke's structure rewards one vocal profile over another?

### Part 2: Physical Action Spectrum (15 minutes)

1. Deliver the joke while standing completely still — no gestures, no facial expression changes, minimal body movement. This is Abbott Mode.
2. Deliver the joke with full physical comedy — act out "drawing eyebrows," mime the wife's "surprised" look, use your whole body. This is Costello Mode.
3. Record both on video. Watch the playback. Which is funnier for THIS joke? Is there a physical approach in between that works even better?
4. Experiment with just ONE physical action: raising your own eyebrows at the word "surprised." Does this single gesture change the joke's impact? Why?

### Part 3: Timing Laboratory (15 minutes)

1. Deliver the joke with NO pause between setup and punchline: "ItoldmywifeshewaSdrawinghereyebrowstoohighshelookedsurprised."
2. Deliver with a SHORT pause (1 second): "I told my wife she was drawing her eyebrows too high. [1 sec] She looked surprised."
3. Deliver with a LONG pause (3 seconds): "I told my wife she was drawing her eyebrows too high. [3 sec] She looked surprised."
4. Deliver with an EXTRA-LONG pause (5 seconds): "I told my wife she was drawing her eyebrows too high. [5 sec] She looked surprised."
5. Which pause length produced the strongest audience reaction (or, if alone, which FELT most effective)? At what point does the pause become too long? What happens when the prediction window exceeds the audience's patience?

### Part 4: Channel Isolation (10 minutes)

1. Tell the joke SILENTLY — using only facial expressions and gestures, no words. Can the joke survive without the verbal channel?
2. Tell the joke with your BACK to your partner/audience — voice only, no visual. How does removing the visual channel change the impact?
3. Write the joke and have your partner read it silently. Is it still funny? What is lost compared to live delivery?
4. Reflect: which channel is most essential for this particular joke? Is the answer different for different types of jokes?

### Part 5: Action Combination Workshop (5 minutes)

1. Create your ideal delivery by combining the best elements from Parts 1-3: choose the vocal profile, the physical approach, and the timing that you found most effective.
2. Record this "optimized" delivery. Compare to your baseline from Part 1. How much better is the optimized version?

## Discussion Questions

* After experimenting with vocal parameters, do you believe that HOW a joke is told is more important than WHAT the joke says? What ratio of content to delivery determines a joke's success?
* Did the timing exercise reveal a "sweet spot" for pause length? How does this relate to the concept of prediction windows in Active Inference?
* Which medium preserves the most of the joke's impact: live, audio, video, or text? What does each medium lose?
* The module describes comedy as "clever bodies." After this lab, do you agree? Can you separate the words from the delivery?

## Wrap-Up

You have explored the full action repertoire of a single joke — varying voice, body, timing, and channel availability. The embodied nature of comedy performance is now concretely established through your own experience.

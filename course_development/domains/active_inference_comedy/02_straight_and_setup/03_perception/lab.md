# Lab: Reading the Room

## Objective

Develop and test your perceptual skills by systematically observing comedy audiences, measuring reaction latencies, and mapping the multi-channel sensory world of the performer.

## Materials

* Three video recordings of stand-up comedy (one killing, one bombing, one mixed — YouTube or Netflix)
* A stopwatch or timer
* Blank paper and pen for recording observations
* Headphones (for audio-only phase)

## Prerequisites

* Reading of Module 03: Perception in Straight & Setup (module.md)
* Completion of Modules 01-02 Labs
* Familiarity with: precision-weighted perception, perceptual channels, audience hidden states

## Steps

### Part 1: Multi-Channel Observation (15 minutes)

1. Watch the first 3 minutes of a stand-up comedian who is doing well (killing). As you watch, tally audience reactions across channels:
   * **Auditory**: Mark each burst of laughter with an L, each silence with an S, each audible reaction (groans, gasps, applause) with its type.
   * **Visual** (if audience is visible): Note body language — leaning forward (engaged), leaning back (disengaged), looking at phones (disconnected).
   * **Temporal**: For each punchline, estimate the delay between delivery and audience response (< 1 second = fast, 1-2 seconds = normal, > 2 seconds = slow).
2. Create a simple chart: Punchline # | Auditory Response | Visual Response | Temporal Delay.

### Part 2: Audio-Only Perception (10 minutes)

1. Now listen to the same 3-minute clip with your eyes closed (audio only). You have lost the visual channel entirely.
2. Note which audience reactions you can still detect and which you cannot. How much information did you lose?
3. Repeat with a different clip. Listen to the performer's voice ONLY — try to infer: Are they killing or bombing? Are they confident or nervous? How do you know?

### Part 3: The Bombing Set (10 minutes)

1. Watch 3 minutes of a comedian bombing (YouTube "comedian bombs" compilations). Apply the same observation chart as Part 1.
2. Note the silence after punchlines. What does the silence tell you about the audience's hidden state? Is the silence uniform or does it have texture (shuffling, coughing, murmuring)?
3. Track the comedian's response to the silence: Do they speed up? Slow down? Change material? Make eye contact with the audience? Each response is a perceptual-action adjustment.

### Part 4: Perceptual Commentary (10 minutes)

1. Watch the mixed-reaction clip. Pause after each punchline and write a brief "perceptual report" as if you were the performer:
   * "I heard nervous laughter from the front. The back is quiet. Estimated 60% engagement. The energy is medium-low. I should try a bigger joke to lift the room."
2. This exercise practices verbalizing the perceptual inference that experienced comedians perform automatically. The goal is to make the implicit explicit.

### Part 5: Precision Weighting Exercise (10 minutes)

1. Watch any comedy clip and deliberately practice two attention strategies:
   * **Abbott Mode**: Focus ONLY on the comedian. Ignore audience reactions entirely. How does this change your experience?
   * **Costello Mode**: Focus ONLY on the audience. Watch their faces, listen to their reactions, ignore the comedian's words. What do you notice?
2. Write a brief reflection: Which attention strategy gave you more information about the system? Which strategy does an experienced performer use? Is it one or the other, or a blend?

## Discussion Questions

* After mapping multi-channel perception, do you think "reading the room" is an acquired skill or a natural talent? What evidence from this lab supports your view?
* How much information is lost when a visual channel is removed (audio-only)? Is it possible to compensate entirely through other channels?
* When the comedian bombs, what perceptual signals indicate the system is failing? At what point should the performer abandon their current material?
* How does the "perceptual commentary" exercise in Part 4 compare to how you normally watch comedy? Did it change your experience?

## Wrap-Up

You have practiced systematic perceptual observation of comedy audiences across multiple channels, latencies, and conditions. These skills — multi-channel perception, latency tracking, and precision-weighted attention — are the foundation of what experienced performers call "stage sense."

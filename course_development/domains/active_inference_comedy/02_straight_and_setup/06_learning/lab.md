# Lab: The Rehearsal Laboratory

## Objective

Experience the learning dynamics of comedy performance by rehearsing a joke through multiple iterations, tracking improvement metrics, and analyzing the transition from effortful to automatic delivery.

## Materials

* A joke to rehearse (provided below, or choose your own)
* A smartphone for recording (audio and/or video)
* A stopwatch
* A small audience of 2-5 people for final test
* Blank paper and pen for tracking data

## Prerequisites

* Reading of Module 06: Learning in Straight & Setup (module.md)
* Completion of Modules 01-05 Labs
* Familiarity with: rehearsal, audience feedback, exploration vs. exploitation, automaticity

## The Joke

Use this short story joke for all rehearsal iterations:

**"My neighbor knocked on my door at 3 AM. 3 AM! Luckily, I was still up playing my drums."**

## Steps

### Part 1: First Iteration — The Baseline (10 minutes)

1. Read the joke once. Close the paper. Deliver it from memory. Record this delivery (audio or video).
2. Rate yourself on: (a) word accuracy (did you get the words right?), (b) timing (did you pause before the punchline?), (c) vocal delivery (was your voice engaging?), (d) physical presence (were you confident?), and (e) overall funniness (your honest assessment). Rate each 1-5.
3. Write down one specific thing you want to improve on the next iteration.

### Part 2: Focused Rehearsal — Five Iterations (15 minutes)

1. Deliver the joke five more times (iterations 2-6). Each time, focus on improving ONE specific element:
   * Iteration 2: Focus on word accuracy — nail the exact wording
   * Iteration 3: Focus on timing — experiment with the pause before "Luckily"
   * Iteration 4: Focus on vocal delivery — try different emphasis patterns
   * Iteration 5: Focus on physical delivery — experiment with gestures, posture
   * Iteration 6: Integrate all elements — aim for the best overall delivery
2. Record each iteration. After all six recordings exist, play them in sequence.
3. Rate iteration 6 on the same 5 criteria from Part 1. Compare to iteration 1.

### Part 3: Tracking Automaticity (10 minutes)

1. Deliver the joke while simultaneously counting backwards from 50 by 3s (50, 47, 44...). If you can deliver the joke smoothly while counting, the lower-level features (words, basic timing) have reached automaticity.
2. Rate the cognitive effort of the dual task. Compare to your experience in the Module 04 Lab.
3. Now deliver the joke while walking around the room and making eye contact with objects or people. Can you maintain quality while moving? If so, you have freed cognitive resources from basic delivery.

### Part 4: Audience Test — The Training Signal (10 minutes)

1. Deliver the joke to your small audience. Note their reaction: timing of laughter, intensity, any visual cues.
2. Based on the audience's reaction, identify one specific adjustment to make. This is using the audience as a training signal.
3. Deliver the joke again to the same audience with the adjustment. Did the reaction improve?
4. If possible, deliver to a DIFFERENT small group. Note: does the adjustment that worked for audience 1 also work for audience 2?

### Part 5: Exploration vs. Exploitation (5 minutes)

1. You now have an "optimized" version of the joke — exploitation mode. Write it down as your final version.
2. Now try a radical variation — exploration mode. Change the order ("Luckily I was still up playing my drums when my neighbor knocked at 3 AM"). Change the details ("My WIFE knocked on my door at 3 AM"). Change the tone (whispered, angry, confused).
3. Did any exploration produce a version that is BETTER than your optimized version? If so, you have discovered the value of continuing to explore even after finding a good exploit.

## Discussion Questions

* How quickly did the joke reach "automaticity" — the point at which you could deliver it without conscious effort? How many iterations did this take?
* The module describes audience feedback as a training signal. How specific was the signal you received? Could you extract a clear improvement direction from the audience's reaction?
* Did the exploration in Part 5 yield any useful variants? How does a comedian decide when to stop exploring and commit to an optimized version?
* Compare your first recording to your last. What changed? Can you hear the learning?

## Wrap-Up

You have experienced the learning dynamics of comedy in miniature: from raw first delivery through focused rehearsal, automaticity development, audience-guided refinement, and the exploration-exploitation tension. The same learning process, scaled to thousands of performances across years, is how professional comedians refine their craft.

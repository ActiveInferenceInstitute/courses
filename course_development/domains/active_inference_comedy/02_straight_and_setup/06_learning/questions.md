# Study Questions: Learning in Straight & Setup

1. The module describes rehearsal as "refining the performer's generative model." At what level does this refinement occur — is it updating the model's parameters, its structure, or both? Provide examples from comedy performance.

2. "Audience feedback as training signal" is described as supervised learning. But the audience doesn't provide labeled feedback ("that joke was funny because X"). Is this really supervised learning, or something closer to reinforcement learning? Argue for your position.

3. The performer's learning trajectory mirrors the "standard Active Inference learning curve — from exploration to exploitation." But comedians also describe returning to exploration later in their careers (reinventing their act). How does this return to exploration fit the framework?

4. Abbott and Costello performed "Who's on First?" thousands of times. Did they learn the same things from each performance? What is the marginal information value of the 1,000th performance compared to the 10th?

5. "A joke that lands consistently is reinforced; a joke that bombs is revised or dropped." Is this the optimal strategy? Can a comedian learn MORE from jokes that bomb than from jokes that land? How?

6. The module describes automaticity as freeing cognitive resources for higher-level monitoring. But can automaticity become a trap? When highly rehearsed material becomes too automatic, what dangers emerge?

7. Deliberate practice in comedy is compared to deliberate practice in music. What is the comedy equivalent of running scales? What specific skill components can be isolated and drilled?

8. The mentor-student dynamic is described as "compressed generative model" transfer. What can be transferred through mentorship and what can only be learned through personal experience? Where does the compression fail?

9. The module describes a comedian's career as "gradient descent toward optimized performance." Is this metaphor accurate? Gradient descent assumes a single loss function. Do comedians optimize for a single objective, or for multiple competing objectives?

10. How does the audience as "teacher" differ from a human teacher? The audience's feedback is: (a) aggregate (not individual), (b) noisy (laughter can mean many things), (c) variable (different every night). How does the comedian extract a clear learning signal from this noise?

11. The transition from "exploration to exploitation" in a comedian's career corresponds to "finding your voice." What does "voice" mean in Active Inference terms? Is it a particular generative model, a particular action policy, or something else?

12. Over-rehearsal can make a performance feel stale. In Active Inference terms, what has happened? Has the model become too precise? Has the action policy lost flexibility?

13. Compare the learning dynamics of an improv comedian to a scripted comedian. The improv comedian never repeats the same material — their "training data" is entirely novel. How does this change the learning process?

14. The module mentions "timing drills" as a form of deliberate practice. Design a specific timing drill for a beginning comedian. What would they practice, how would they measure improvement, and what is the underlying computational skill they are developing?

15. When Abbott and Costello first performed together, they had to learn each other's rhythms. Describe this inter-performer learning as a mutual model-building process. What was each learning about the other?

16. The module describes early-career comedy as "high variance, many failures, broad sampling." This matches the explore phase. But young comedians also face survival pressure (they need to get booked to earn money). How does survival pressure affect the explore/exploit balance?

17. A comedian watches a recording of their own performance and cringes. What is happening computationally? They are comparing their meta-model (what they intended) with the recorded output (what they actually did). What learning does this comparison enable?

18. The module mentions that the seasoned comedian has a "arsenal of proven material." But cultural contexts change — a joke that worked in 2010 may not work in 2025. How does a comedian update their model of what works when the training distribution shifts?

19. Consider a comedian who gets consistently good reviews from critics but mediocre audience reactions. These are contradictory training signals. Which should the comedian weight more heavily? Why?

20. The module concludes that learning in comedy is "a long gradient descent." But some comedians have breakthrough moments — a single performance that transforms their career. How do breakthroughs fit into a gradual learning framework? Are they discontinuities or accelerations?

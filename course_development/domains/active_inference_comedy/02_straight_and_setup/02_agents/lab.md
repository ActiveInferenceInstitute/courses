# Lab: Inhabiting the Agent Archetypes

## Objective

Experience the cognitive demands of the straight man and comic roles by performing both, measuring the precision discipline required, and documenting the meta-model dynamics between performers.

## Materials

* A partner (required)
* The exchange from the Module 01 Lab (or the one provided below)
* A stopwatch or timer
* Blank paper and pen
* A small audience of 2-5 people (optional but recommended)

## Prerequisites

* Reading of Module 02: Agents in Straight & Setup (module.md)
* Completion of Module 01 Lab
* Familiarity with: agent archetypes, precision profiles, meta-model

## The Exchange

Use this extended exchange (or write your own 8-line dialogue with a straight man and comic):

**A (Straight)**: "I'm teaching a cooking class next week."
**B (Comic)**: "Oh great! What are you making?"
**A**: "We'll be making thyme."
**B**: "Yeah, but what are you COOKING?"
**A**: "Thyme. It's very popular."
**B**: "I know time is popular! Everyone wants more of it!"
**A**: "Right, that's why I'm growing it in my garden."
**B**: "You're growing TIME? In a GARDEN?"
**A**: "Yes. Right next to the sage."
**B**: "I don't care how wise it is — you can't grow time!"

## Steps

### Part 1: Straight Man Discipline (15 minutes)

1. Perform the exchange as the straight man (A). Your single instruction: **believe every word you say with absolute certainty.** "Thyme" is the herb, and nothing else.
2. During and after the performance, note: How hard was it to NOT react to your partner's confusion? Did you feel the urge to clarify? To laugh? To break character? Rate the cognitive effort on a 1-5 scale.
3. Perform again. This time, try to actively suppress every natural reaction to your partner's misunderstanding. Notice the mental effort required. This is precision maintenance under social pressure.
4. Write down three moments where you felt the strongest urge to break character. What were the signals your body wanted to send? What did you actually send?

### Part 2: Comic Volatility (15 minutes)

1. Switch roles. Now play the comic (B). Your instruction: **genuinely try to understand what A is saying, and express real confusion when it doesn't make sense.**
2. Notice the cognitive demands: you must simultaneously track the actual meaning (the herb thyme), generate the wrong interpretation (time), express that interpretation convincingly, and escalate emotionally. How many cognitive tracks are you running?
3. After performing, rate the cognitive load on a 1-5 scale. Compare to the straight man's load.
4. Perform again. This time, pay attention to the pacing: the comic must react QUICKLY. Each response should come within 1-2 seconds. Notice how this time pressure affects your cognitive load.

### Part 3: The Meta-Model (10 minutes)

1. After performing both roles, sit with your partner and discuss: What did you both know that the characters didn't? Write down three elements of your shared meta-model (e.g., "We both know 'thyme' is the punchline," "We both know the comic gets more confused," "We both know the audience expects the misunderstanding").
2. During performance, were there moments when you communicated with your partner THROUGH the performance (a glance, a timing adjustment, a breath) without the audience noticing? List them.
3. Diagram the three-model structure: Character A's model, Character B's model, and the shared performer meta-model. Show which information crosses which boundary.

### Part 4: Role Reversal Experiment (10 minutes)

1. Now swap: the person who was the straight man becomes the comic, and vice versa.
2. Perform the exchange again. Note: does the role feel different now that you understand both perspectives?
3. Write a brief reflection: Which role felt more demanding? Which felt more fun? Which required more sustained effort vs. more moment-to-moment flexibility?

## Discussion Questions

* The module claims the straight man's role is "harder." Based on your experience, do you agree? What kind of difficulty does each role present?
* Could you feel the "meta-model" operating during performance? Was it conscious or automatic?
* When you played the comic, were you genuinely confused or performing confusion? What's the difference from the inside?
* How did having an audience change the dynamics compared to performing for just your partner?

## Wrap-Up

You have inhabited both agent archetypes and experienced their cognitive demands firsthand. Your notes on precision discipline, comic volatility, and meta-model dynamics will serve as personal reference for the remaining modules.

# Module 08: Planning in Straight & Setup

## Learning Objectives

1. Understand building a set: action policy design from the performer's perspective.
2. Apply Active Inference concepts to the lived experience of comedy performance.
3. Connect planning theory to practical performance craft.

## Introduction

Before you step on stage, you plan. You choose your opening bit. You decide the order of your material. You plan where to put the callbacks, when to shift energy, and how to build to your closer. You plan contingencies: what to do if the audience is cold, what to do if a heckler interrupts, what to do if a joke bombs.

This is planning in the performer's sense — not abstract theory, but the practical, embodied process of designing an action policy for a specific context. This module examines how performers plan their sets, manage contingencies, and balance the tension between structure and spontaneity.

## Key Concepts

### Set Construction as Policy Architecture

A comedy set is an action policy: a planned sequence of behaviors designed to achieve a goal (maximum laughter) across a temporal horizon (5, 20, or 60 minutes). Set construction involves: selecting material (which bits to include), ordering material (which bits go where), planning transitions (how to move between bits), and designing the energy arc (how the set builds, peaks, and resolves). Each decision is an expected free energy calculation: 'If I put this joke here, what is the downstream effect on the audience's model?'

### Contingency Planning: Adapting to the Unknown

No plan survives contact with the audience. The professional comedian plans contingencies: alternative bits if the opener bombs, crowd work material if the audience needs warming up, recovery lines for hecklers, and shortened versions of sets for tight time slots. Contingency planning is **policy branching** — the comedian pre-computes alternative action sequences and deploys them based on real-time observations. The deeper the contingency tree, the more resilient the performer.

### The Tension Between Script and Spontaneity

Abbott and Costello had a script — a planned structure refined across thousands of performances. But within that structure, there was room for variation: timing adjustments, emphasis changes, audience-responsive pauses. The best comedy lives in the tension between plan and improvisation: the structure provides scaffolding, and the performer's real-time responsiveness fills in the details. This is model predictive control: the plan sets the trajectory, and moment-to-moment adjustments keep the performance on track.

## Applications

- **The Comedy Special as a Planned Product**: A Netflix comedy special is a meticulously planned artifact: the set has been refined over hundreds of performances, the timing is precise, the energy arc is designed for a 60-minute viewing experience. But the best specials still feel spontaneous — the planning is invisible because the performer's real-time responsiveness is seamlessly integrated.

- **Career Planning as Long-Horizon Policy**: A comedian's career is a planning problem at the scale of years: developing material, building an audience, choosing performance contexts, and adapting to cultural change. 'Finding your voice' is the convergence of a long-horizon policy optimization.

## Conclusion

Planning from the performer's perspective is practical, embodied, and adaptive. The set is a planned structure; the performance is a real-time adaptation; the career is a long-horizon policy. Every level of planning involves the same core computation: anticipating the audience's model and selecting actions that maximize the gap between what they expect and what they experience.

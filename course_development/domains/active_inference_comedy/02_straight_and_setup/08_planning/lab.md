# Lab: Building Your Set

## Objective

Design, perform, and iterate on a complete comedy set, experiencing the planning dynamics of material selection, ordering, energy arc design, contingency planning, and real-time adaptation.

## Materials

* 5-8 short jokes or comedic observations (your own, or sourced from public domain)
* A small audience of 3-5 people
* A smartphone for recording
* Index cards (one per joke)
* Blank paper and pen
* A stopwatch

## Prerequisites

* Reading of Module 08: Planning in Straight & Setup (module.md)
* Completion of Modules 01-07 Labs
* Familiarity with: set construction, contingency planning, energy arcs, exploration vs. exploitation

## Steps

### Part 1: Material Assembly (10 minutes)

1. Write 5-8 jokes, comedic observations, or short stories on index cards — one per card. They can be jokes you've heard, one-liners, or personal anecdotes. Don't worry about quality — this is a planning exercise, not a performance audition.
2. Rate each card on two dimensions:
   * **Strength** (1-5): How reliable is this joke? Does it usually get a response?
   * **Energy** (1-5): How much energy does this joke require from the performer? How much does it demand from the audience?

### Part 2: Set Design — Version 1 (10 minutes)

1. Arrange your cards in an order for a 5-minute set. Consider:
   * **Opener**: Which joke establishes the tone and warms up the audience?
   * **Middle**: Which jokes build energy and maintain momentum?
   * **Closer**: Which joke is your strongest material — the one you want the audience to remember?
2. Draw the planned energy arc: sketch a simple line graph where the X-axis is time and the Y-axis is energy. Mark where each joke sits on the arc.
3. Write one contingency plan: "If the opener bombs, I will ___."

### Part 3: First Performance (10 minutes)

1. Perform your 5-minute set for the audience. Follow your planned order.
2. Record the performance.
3. After performing, note:
   * Did the energy arc go as planned?
   * Which jokes landed? Which didn't?
   * Did you use your contingency plan?
   * At what point (if any) did you deviate from the plan?

### Part 4: Adaptation Workshop (10 minutes)

1. Based on the audience's reactions, re-order your cards. Move the strongest material earlier or later as needed. Drop the weakest joke.
2. Add a callback: reference your opener's punchline later in the set. Write it on a new card and insert it.
3. Draw a new energy arc for Version 2. Compare to Version 1.
4. Write two additional contingency plans: "If the audience is cold, I will ___" and "If a specific joke bombs, I will___."

### Part 5: Second Performance (5 minutes)

1. Perform Version 2 for the same audience (or a different one if available).
2. Compare the recordings: Version 1 vs. Version 2. What improved? What still needs work?
3. Write a brief "post-show debrief": What did you learn about set construction? About energy management? About the tension between planning and adaptation?

### Part 6: Time Pressure Challenge (5 minutes)

1. Cut your set from 5 minutes to 2 minutes. You must drop material. Which jokes do you keep? Which do you cut?
2. Write down your decision criteria. What makes a joke "must-keep" vs. "cuttable"?
3. Perform the 2-minute version. Does the shorter set feel tighter, or does it lose something essential?

## Discussion Questions

* How much did your planned set differ from your actual performance? Was the gap large or small?
* Did the energy arc concept help you organize material, or was it too abstract? Would you use it again?
* When you had to cut material (Part 6), what surprised you about the decisions? Were there jokes you expected to keep but ended up cutting?
* After two performances and two redesigns, do you feel you understand your material better? Has the planning process changed how you think about the jokes themselves?

## Wrap-Up

You have experienced the full planning lifecycle of a comedy set: material assembly, ordering, energy arc design, contingency planning, performance, adaptation, and time-pressure cutting. This lab integrates all eight modules of Course 2 — you used systems thinking (reading the room), agent archetypes (your role as performer), perception (audience signals), cognition (dual-track monitoring), action (vocal and physical delivery), learning (iteration), communication (multi-channel engagement), and planning (set construction) in a single exercise.

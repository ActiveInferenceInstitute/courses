# Glossary: Comedy & Active Inference

> Canonical term definitions for the Active Inference & Comedy curriculum. All module content must use these definitions consistently.

---

## A

**Act-out** — A comedian's physical performance or embodied imitation during a routine. In Active Inference terms, an act-out is an embodied generative model: the performer uses their body to generate predictions in the audience's mind about a character, situation, or scenario. The audience's mirror system reads the act-out as a compressed likelihood mapping.

**Action (comedic)** — Any behavior a performer executes to alter the state of the comedic exchange: delivering a line, pausing, gesturing, breaking character. In Active Inference, action is the process by which an agent changes sensory states to reduce expected free energy. In comedy, actions are designed to *increase* the audience's prediction error — the reverse of most biological action.

**Active Inference** — A framework from theoretical neuroscience (Friston, 2010) proposing that all adaptive systems minimize variational free energy through perception (updating beliefs) and action (changing the world). In comedy, performance is a case of *engineered* free energy: the comedian builds generative models in the audience only to violate them.

**Agent** — An entity that maintains a generative model (beliefs about the world) and acts to minimize free energy. In "Who's on First?", there are at minimum three agents: Abbott (the straight man), Costello (the comic), and the audience.

**Ambiguity (engineered)** — The deliberate construction of utterances that activate multiple generative models simultaneously. "Who's on first" is engineered ambiguity par excellence: the same phonemic string activates two incompatible semantic models.

**Audience** — The collective inference agent in any comedic performance. The audience maintains its own generative model, tracks the models of both performers, and generates laughter when prediction error exceeds a threshold. The audience's model is typically superior to both performers' models — they hold the comic irony.

---

## B

**Beat** — A unit of comedic timing; a deliberate pause in delivery. In precision-weighting terms, a beat is a window during which the audience accumulates evidence under their current generative model, increasing precision and thus amplifying the prediction error when the punchline arrives.

**Benign violation** — McGraw & Warren's (2010) theory that humor arises when a situation is simultaneously perceived as a violation (of expectation, norm, or logic) and as benign (non-threatening). In Active Inference, this maps to prediction error that does not trigger threat-related precision escalation.

**Bit** — A self-contained comedic unit; a bounded inference episode. A bit has its own internal structure (setup, development, punchline) and Markov blanket (the frame that separates it from surrounding material). "Who's on First?" is a single, long bit — or a recursive series of nested bits.

**Blow / Blow line** — The final, highest-amplitude punchline in a routine; the terminal prediction error. The blow is where the audience's accumulated model catastrophically fails and the laughter peaks. In "Who's on First?", the blow is often Costello's eventual explosion of frustration.

**Bombing** — When a joke or routine fails to produce laughter. In Active Inference terms, the audience's generative model successfully predicted the punchline: no surprise occurred, no prediction error was generated, and no laughter was triggered. The performer's action policy failed to alter sensory states.

---

## C

**Callback** — A reference to a joke or element from earlier in the set. A callback reactivates a previously resolved posterior belief and generates new prediction error from it. The audience's model had "filed away" the earlier element; the callback forces a Bayesian re-evaluation. Callbacks exploit the temporal depth of the audience's generative model.

**Comic** — The performer who occupies the role of the confused, surprised, or destabilized agent. In "Who's on First?", Costello is the comic. In Active Inference terms, the comic operates with low precision on key parameters — their generative model is volatile, updating rapidly and incorrectly.

**Costume / Costuming** — Visual priors that shape the audience's generative model before a word is spoken. Abbott's sharp suit signals authority and certainty (high precision); Costello's rumpled appearance signals confusion and volatility (low precision).

---

## D

**Dead air** — An unintended silence in performance. Unlike a deliberate beat, dead air is a failure of the performer's action policy — the agent has no available policy to execute. The audience detects this as a model failure in the performer, which generates awkwardness rather than laughter.

**Double entendre** — A phrase with two meanings, one typically risqué. A degenerate observation (single phonemic input mapping to two semantic hypotheses). Unlike "Who's on First?", double entendres typically have a *dominant* and a *subordinate* meaning, creating an asymmetric likelihood mapping.

---

## E

**Epistemic action** — An action taken to gather information rather than achieve a pragmatic goal. In "Who's on First?", Costello's repeated questioning ("Who's on first?") is tragically epistemic — he is genuinely trying to reduce uncertainty. But each question regenerates the same ambiguous observation, trapping him in an epistemic loop.

**Expected free energy** — The anticipated surprise (and information gain) associated with a future action policy. Abbott's comedic genius lies in selecting actions — specific phrasings, particular ordering of player names — that maximize expected free energy *for Costello* while minimizing it for himself.

---

## F

**Free energy** — A quantity from variational Bayesian inference that bounds surprise (negative log-evidence). In Active Inference, agents minimize free energy. In comedy, the audience's free energy is deliberately *increased* by the performer, then discharged as laughter. Laughter is the sound of free energy being released.

**Frogging** — Borrowed from craft terminology (and earlier entries in this curriculum series): undoing work that has gone wrong. In comedy, frogging is the performer's correction when a bit goes off-track — an improvised retreat to an earlier state where the generative model still works.

---

## G

**Generative model** — An internal model that generates predictions about sensory observations. Every participant in comedy maintains a generative model: the performer models the audience, the audience models the performer, and both model the joke's internal logic. In "Who's on First?", Abbott and Costello have structurally identical but referentially incompatible generative models.

---

## H

**Hack** — A joke or comedian that relies on degenerate priors — material so predictable that it generates zero prediction error. A hack's generative model is a subset of the audience's prior beliefs. No surprise, no laughter, no free energy discharged. The audience's model already contains the punchline.

---

## I

**Incongruity theory** — The oldest formal theory of humor (Schopenhauer, Kierkegaard, Kant), proposing that laughter arises from the perception of incongruity between expectation and reality. In Active Inference terms: humor = prediction error under a benign attribution.

---

## K

**KL divergence** — Kullback-Leibler divergence; a measure of the difference between two probability distributions. In comedy, the KL divergence between the audience's prior beliefs and their posterior beliefs after the punchline quantifies the magnitude of the joke. High KL divergence = big laugh (if benign). Zero KL divergence = hack.

---

## L

**Laughter** — The behavioral correlate of rapid free energy discharge in social contexts. Laughter is not random — it occurs at precise moments when prediction error peaks and threat attribution is low. It is a social signal that communicates "my model just broke, but I'm safe."

---

## M

**Markov blanket (comedic)** — The boundary between the internal states of a comedic system (the logic of the bit, the performers' models) and the external states (the audience, the venue, the cultural context). In "Who's on First?", the Markov blanket is the dialogue itself — the exchange of words at which internal semantic models contact the external world. The audience perceives the routine *through* the dialogue, not directly through the performers' internal states.

---

## P

**Pacing** — The rhythm and speed of delivery. Pacing modulates precision accumulation: fast pacing prevents the audience from building strong predictions (keeping them off-balance), while slow pacing allows precision to build, creating conditions for larger prediction errors.

**Punchline** — The moment of maximum prediction error delivery within a joke. The punchline is the observation that the audience's current generative model cannot accommodate. In "Who's on First?", the punchline recurs hundreds of times — every instance of "Who" triggers a fresh prediction error because Costello cannot update his model.

**Precision** — The confidence assigned to a prediction or observation (the inverse variance). High-precision predictions are believed strongly; low-precision predictions are held loosely. The straight man has high precision on names ("I'm *telling* you, Who is on first"). The comic has fluctuating precision, producing rapid, inaccurate model updates.

---

## R

**Rule of three** — A comedic structure: two instances establish a pattern (building the audience's generative model), and the third violates it (delivering prediction error). The rule of three is the minimum viable prior + violation structure.

**Riffing** — Improvised comedic exploration; epistemic foraging through comedy state space. When a comedian riffs, they are sampling actions with high epistemic value, looking for observations (audience reactions) that reveal rich pockets of prediction error.

---

## S

**Setup** — The portion of a joke that establishes the audience's generative model — building expectations that the punchline will violate. A good setup is a lie told with conviction: it installs a high-precision prediction that the punchline will shatter.

**Straight man** — The performer who maintains the stable generative model against which the comic's confusion is measured. In "Who's on First?", Abbott is the straight man. He holds his model with high precision and refuses to accommodate Costello's confusion. The straight man is the fixed point around which the comic orbits — the invariant structure that makes prediction error legible.

**Surprise** — The negative log-probability of an observation under the current model: −log P(o|m). The fundamental currency of comedy. No surprise = no joke. In Active Inference, agents minimize surprise; in comedy, performers *maximize* surprise (for the audience, not for themselves).

---

## T

**Tag / Topper** — A secondary punchline that immediately follows the primary one, generating additional prediction error from the same setup. Tags exploit the audience's post-punchline belief state — they have just updated their model, and the tag shows that the new model is also wrong.

**Tight five** — A comedian's most refined, lowest-free-energy five-minute set. Every word has been optimized through hundreds of performances. In Active Inference terms, a tight five is a precision-maximized action policy: maximum prediction error per unit time, minimum wasted effort.

**Timing** — The temporal structure of comedic delivery; the art of when to speak, when to pause, and when to strike. Timing is temporal precision dynamics: the comedian manipulates the audience's precision accumulation rate to control the amplitude and location of prediction error.

---

## V

**Violation** — The component of incongruity that makes something humorous (per McGraw & Warren, 2010). A violation is a prediction error that occurs in a context marked as non-threatening. The context marking is crucial: the same prediction error in a threatening context produces fear, not laughter.

---

## W

**"Who's on First?"** — A comedy routine first performed by Bud Abbott and Lou Costello in the 1930s, and widely regarded as one of the greatest comedy routines in history. The routine exploits the accidental homophony between question words ("Who?", "What?") and proper nouns (player names). It is the central case study of this curriculum: a system of two agents with incompatible generative models producing non-convergent, escalating free energy.

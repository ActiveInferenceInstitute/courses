# Section 01: Stitch & Structure

## The Individual Crocheter as an Active Inference Agent

## Learning Objectives

1. Recognize the crochet project as a system with a Markov Blanket — the working edge of the fabric where internal states (finished rows) meet external states (unworked yarn) through sensory and active states (hook, tension, live loops).
2. Identify yourself as an Active Inference agent whose generative model (the pattern in your mind) drives every stitch through the minimization of prediction error.
3. Connect the tactile, embodied experience of crocheting — tension, rhythm, muscle memory, frogging — to the formal concepts of precision, action, learning, and prediction error correction.
4. Develop awareness of how planning, communication, and learning operate at the level of individual stitch mechanics.

## Introduction

Pick up your hook. Wrap the yarn around your finger. Feel the tension settle. Before you have made a single chain, you are already an Active Inference agent embedded in a system.

Stitch & Structure is the foundation of the entire crochet-circles curriculum. It explores Active Inference at the most intimate scale: the individual crocheter, the single stitch, the relationship between hook and yarn. Every concept in the framework — systems, agents, perception, cognition, action, learning, communication, planning — has a concrete, tactile counterpart in the act of crocheting. This is not metaphor. The mathematics of Active Inference describes exactly what your hands are doing when you pull yarn through a loop, feel for the right tension, notice a mistake, and decide whether to rip back or carry on.

Across eight modules, you will move from the broadest question (what makes my crochet project a system?) through the components of that system (who am I as the crocheter? what do I sense? what do I think? what do I do?) to the processes that develop over time (how do I learn? how do I share? how do I plan?). Each module builds on the last, and each grounds abstract science in the specific, rhythmic, meditative reality of crochet.

## Key Concepts

### 1. The Hook-Yarn Boundary (Markov Blanket)

The working edge of your fabric — the live loops on your hook, the row you are currently stitching — is the Markov Blanket of your crochet project. Through it, you sense the tension of the yarn (sensory state) and act on the fabric by pulling new loops through (active state). Everything behind the working edge is internal state: the finished structure. Everything ahead is external state: the unworked yarn, the pattern instructions, the eventual shape. If the live loop slips off the hook, the boundary is compromised and the fabric can unravel.

### 2. The Crocheter as Embodied Agent

You are not a passive instruction-follower. You are an active inference agent with a generative model — the pattern as you understand it — that generates predictions about what each stitch should feel like, look like, and produce. When reality matches prediction (the stitch sits right, the count is correct, the fabric drapes as expected), free energy is low. When it does not (a missed stitch, uneven tension, a shape that cups when it should lie flat), prediction error rises and you act to correct it.

### 3. Tension as Precision

How tightly you hold the yarn determines the precision of your sensory information. Tight tension produces dense, defined stitches where every detail is crisp. Loose tension produces softer, more ambiguous fabric. In Active Inference, precision is the confidence assigned to prediction errors. A crocheter with well-calibrated tension — not too tight, not too loose — is an agent with well-calibrated precision: responsive to genuine errors without overreacting to noise.

### 4. Frogging as Prediction Error Correction

Every crocheter knows the sound: rip it, rip it. Frogging — undoing rows of work — is the quintessential prediction error response. When the fabric diverges too far from the pattern (the generative model), the accumulated error becomes too large to ignore. The crocheter rips back to a point where predictions and reality align, then rebuilds from there. This is not failure; it is the inference process working exactly as it should.

## Applications

* **Gauge Swatches as Prior Calibration**: Before starting a project, a crocheter makes a gauge swatch — a small test piece to check whether their tension, hook size, and yarn produce the expected stitch dimensions. In Active Inference terms, the gauge swatch calibrates the agent's precision: it aligns the generative model (pattern's stated gauge) with the generative process (the actual fabric produced by this particular crocheter with this particular yarn). Without this calibration, the finished object will not match its intended size.

* **Stitch Markers as Epistemic Scaffolding**: Placing a stitch marker at the beginning of a round or at a pattern repeat offloads counting from memory to the environment. This is an epistemic action — an action taken not to change the fabric but to reduce uncertainty about the agent's position in the pattern. The marker becomes part of the extended Markov Blanket, reducing the cognitive free energy of tracking complex stitch patterns.

Lecture Content

Module 01: Systems in Crochet Circles

Learning Objectives

  1. Identify the boundary between your crochet work and its environment using the concept of a Markov blanket.
  2. Recognize how hook, yarn, and hands form an interacting system with internal and external states.
  3. Map the components of a crochet project onto the formal structure of a system in Active Inference.

Introduction

Pick up a crochet hook and a ball of yarn. Before you make a single stitch, you already have a system: the hook in your hand, the yarn trailing from the skein, and the space where fabric will soon appear. In Active Inference, a system is anything that maintains itself as distinct from its surroundings. Your crochet work-in-progress does exactly this — it has an inside (the stitches already made), an outside (the yarn not yet worked, the air around the fabric, the table beneath it), and a boundary where the two meet.

This module invites you to look at your crochet practice as a living example of systems thinking. Where does the project end and the world begin? What role does the edge of your fabric play? And how does the simple act of hooking yarn through a loop create and maintain a boundary that defines the whole work?

Key Concepts

1. The Hook-Yarn Boundary as Markov Blanket

In Active Inference, a Markov blanket is the statistical boundary that separates an agent's internal states from the external world. It consists of sensory states (how the outside world affects the inside) and active states (how the inside affects the outside).

In crochet, this boundary is tangible. The edge of your fabric — the live loops sitting on your hook, the working row you are currently stitching — is exactly the Markov blanket of your project. Everything behind the working row is internal state: the finished rows, the structure you have built, the pattern that has taken shape. Everything ahead of the working row is external state: the unworked yarn, the pattern instructions you haven't reached yet, the eventual shape the project will take.

The live loop on your hook is special. It is the point of contact, the sensory-active boundary. Through it, you sense the tension of the yarn (sensory state) and you act on the fabric by pulling new loops through (active state). If you set your work down and the live loop slips off, the boundary is compromised — the fabric can unravel. The Markov blanket dissolves.

This is not just a metaphor. In the formal mathematics of Active Inference, the Markov blanket is defined by conditional independence: internal states and external states only communicate through blanket states. In your crochet, the finished rows (internal) and the ball of yarn (external) only interact through the working edge — the hook, the live loop, and your hands.

2. Internal States: The Work-in-Progress

The internal states of a system are everything that is shielded from the environment by the Markov blanket. In crochet, this is the fabric you have already created: the foundation chain, the completed rows, the emerging pattern of single crochets, double crochets, or whatever stitches you have chosen.

These internal states carry information. The stitch count tells you where you are. The height of the rows tells you how far along you are. The texture and drape of the fabric tell you whether your tension has been consistent. In Active Inference terms, internal states encode the system's beliefs about itself — they are the accumulated result of all the actions (stitches) the system has taken.

Notice that once a row is completed and you have turned your work, those stitches become relatively fixed. They are no longer directly in contact with the external world. They influence the current row (because you crochet into them), but they do so through the boundary — through the top loops of the previous row, which are the points of insertion for your hook. This layered structure, where past actions become the substrate for future actions, is a hallmark of how internal states function in Active Inference.

3. External States: Yarn Supply and Environment

The external states are everything outside the Markov blanket. In crochet, this includes the yarn still in the skein, the pattern you are following, the lighting in the room, the other members of your crochet circle, and even the intended recipient of the finished project.

The yarn supply is the most immediate external state. It feeds into the system through the working yarn — the strand that runs from the skein to your hook. The crocheter does not directly perceive the entire ball of yarn at once; you feel it through the tension on the strand, the weight of the skein on your lap, the way the yarn feeds smoothly or catches.

The pattern instructions are another kind of external state. They exist outside your project, but they influence what you do at the boundary. When you read "ch 3, dc in next st," you are receiving information from the external world that shapes your actions at the Markov blanket.

This is why crochet in a group is so rich: the external states expand to include the projects of others, their advice, their shared patterns. The boundary of your system is clear — your hook, your yarn, your fabric — but it exists within a much larger environment.

Applications

In crochet, we see Systems manifest in:

  • Gauge Swatches as System Calibration: When you crochet a gauge swatch before starting a project, you are essentially calibrating your system. You establish how your internal states (tension, hook size, stitch height) interact with external states (yarn weight, pattern requirements) at the boundary. The swatch lets you check whether the Markov blanket of your system — the edge where stitches form — is producing the expected results.

  • Frogging as System Boundary Repair: When you discover an error and rip back your work ("frogging," from "rip it, rip it"), you are deliberately dissolving internal states back through the Markov blanket. Completed stitches return to unworked yarn — internal states become external states again. This is a dramatic illustration of how the system boundary is maintained by active work, and how it can be reversed when the internal states no longer match expectations.

Conclusion

Every crochet project is a system in the Active Inference sense: it has internal states (the fabric you have made), external states (the yarn and world around it), and a Markov blanket (the live edge where hook meets yarn). Understanding this structure helps us see why the working row feels so alive, why a dropped stitch is so alarming, and why the simple act of pulling yarn through a loop is an act of system maintenance. In the next module, we turn our attention to the agent at the center of this system: you, the crocheter.

Lab Protocol

Lab: The Hook-Yarn Boundary

Objective

Crochet a small swatch and physically identify the internal states, external states, and Markov blanket of your crochet system in real time.

Materials

  • Worsted weight yarn (any color), approximately 20 yards
  • Crochet hook, size H/8 (5.0mm)
  • Stitch marker (or a scrap of contrasting yarn)
  • Pen and paper (or sticky notes) for labeling
  • Scissors

Prerequisites

  • Ability to make a foundation chain and single crochet stitches (or willingness to learn during the lab)
  • Reading of Module 01: Systems lecture (module.md)

Steps

Part 1: Setting Up the System (10 minutes)

  1. Before you begin crocheting, lay out your materials on the table: yarn, hook, scissors, stitch marker. On a piece of paper, write three labels: "INTERNAL," "EXTERNAL," and "BLANKET."
  2. Place the labels near the corresponding materials. At this point, everything is external — no system exists yet. Note this on your paper: "Before the first stitch, there are no internal states."
  3. Make a slip knot on your hook. Pause. You now have a single live loop. Ask yourself: has a system come into existence? Where is the Markov blanket?
  4. Write down your answer before continuing.

Part 2: Building Internal States (15 minutes)

  1. Chain 15. As you chain, notice how each new chain stitch transitions from external state (unworked yarn) to internal state (completed chain) through the blanket (your hook and the live loop).
  2. Turn your work. Single crochet in the second chain from the hook and in each chain across (14 sc total).
  3. Pause at the end of the row. Place a sticky note labeled "INTERNAL" on the completed row. Place "EXTERNAL" on the ball of yarn. Place "BLANKET" on the hook and live loop area.
  4. Crochet 4 more rows of single crochet (5 rows total). After each row, pause briefly and notice: the internal region has grown, the external region (yarn ball) has shrunk, and the blanket has moved.

Part 3: Observing the Boundary (10 minutes)

  1. Hold your swatch up and look at the working edge — the row you are currently on. This is the Markov blanket. Notice:
  2. The live loop on your hook (active state — you can act through it)
  3. The top loops of the previous row (sensory state — they tell you where to insert your hook)
  4. The yarn coming from the skein (external input crossing into the blanket)
  5. Gently tug the working yarn. Feel how the tension travels from the external state (skein) through the blanket (your fingers and hook) to affect the internal states (the fabric tightens or loosens). This is sensory information crossing the Markov blanket.
  6. Now deliberately insert your hook into the next stitch but do not yarn over yet. You are poised at the boundary. The hook tip is inside the fabric (touching internal states) while the working yarn is still external. The moment you yarn over and pull through, you convert external yarn into internal fabric through the blanket.

Part 4: Boundary Disruption (10 minutes)

  1. Carefully remove your hook from the live loop (do not pull the yarn — just slide the hook out). Observe what happens. The live loop is still there, but it is unsecured. The Markov blanket is weakened. If you tugged the yarn, the fabric could unravel.
  2. Reinsert your hook into the live loop. The blanket is restored.
  3. Now frog (rip back) one full row. As you pull the yarn, watch each stitch dissolve: internal states become external states again. The boundary moves backward.
  4. Stop after one row is frogged. Crochet that row again. The boundary moves forward once more.

Part 5: Reflection and Discussion (15 minutes)

  1. On your paper, draw a simple diagram of your crochet system showing:
    • Internal states (completed fabric)
    • External states (yarn, environment)
    • Blanket states (working edge, hook, live loop)
    • Arrows showing the flow of yarn from external through blanket to internal

Discussion Questions

  • At what exact moment did the system come into existence — with the slip knot, the first chain, or the first row?
  • When you frogged a row, did the system shrink, or did the boundary simply move?
  • If you set your work down and left the room, does the system still exist? What has changed about the Markov blanket?
  • How does this hands-on experience change your understanding of what a "system boundary" means in Active Inference?

Wrap-Up

Fasten off your swatch (or leave the live loop on the hook secured with a stitch marker). You have physically enacted the creation, maintenance, and partial dissolution of a system's Markov blanket. Bring your labeled swatch to the next crochet circle meeting for group discussion.

Practice Quiz

Practice Quiz: Systems

Part A: Multiple Choice

  1. In a crochet project viewed as an Active Inference system, what represents the Markov blanket? A) The completed rows of fabric B) The yarn still in the skein C) The live working edge — hook, live loop, and working row D) The pattern instructions on paper

  2. When you frog (rip back) several rows of crochet, what happens from a systems perspective? A) External states become internal states B) Internal states are converted back to external states through the blanket C) The Markov blanket becomes stronger D) Nothing changes — the system remains the same

  3. The yarn tension you feel through your fingers while crocheting is best described as: A) An internal state of the fabric B) A sensory state at the Markov blanket C) An external state unrelated to the system D) A planning variable

  4. Which of the following is an internal state of a crochet system? A) The ball of yarn in the skein B) The pattern written on paper C) Three completed rows of double crochet D) The lighting in the room

  5. A gauge swatch helps the crocheter calibrate the system. In Active Inference terms, the swatch primarily helps establish: A) The color of the yarn B) The relationship between agent actions and system outcomes at the boundary C) How many projects to make D) The social dynamics of the crochet circle

  6. When crocheting in the round, the Markov blanket of the working edge: A) Disappears entirely B) Forms a closed continuous loop rather than an open row C) Doubles in size D) Becomes identical to flat-row crochet

  7. If the live loop slips off the hook and the fabric begins to unravel, this is analogous to: A) The system successfully minimizing free energy B) The Markov blanket dissolving, threatening system integrity C) The agent choosing a new policy D) The external states becoming more organized

Part B: Short Answer

  1. Explain how the working yarn — the strand running from the skein to your hook — functions as a channel connecting external states to internal states through the Markov blanket. Use specific crochet actions to illustrate.

  2. You are crocheting a scarf and you reach the end of your first ball of yarn. Describe this moment in terms of systems: what external state has changed, how does the crocheter detect it at the boundary, and what action at the blanket is required to continue the system?

  3. Two crocheters share a single ball of yarn, each pulling from one end to work on separate projects. Describe the system boundaries. How many Markov blankets are there, and how do the two systems interact through shared external states?

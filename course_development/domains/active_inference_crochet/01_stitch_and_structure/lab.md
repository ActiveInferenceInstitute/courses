# Lab: Discovering Active Inference at the Hook

## Introduction

This hands-on lab invites you to experience Active Inference through the act of crocheting. You will not just read about prediction, precision, and inference — you will feel them in your fingers, notice them in your attention, and observe them in your stitches.

> **Intention:** By the end of this lab, you will have identified the Markov Blanket of a crochet project, experienced precision through deliberate tension changes, practiced prediction error correction through intentional frogging, and reflected on how your hands already know what Active Inference describes.

## Materials

* Crochet hook (size appropriate for your yarn — 5.0mm/H-8 is a good default)
* Medium-weight yarn (worsted/aran weight, light color preferred so stitches are visible)
* Stitch markers (2-3)
* Scissors
* Notebook and pen for reflections

## Procedure

### Part 1: Finding the Boundary (15 minutes)

> **Intention:** Identify the Markov Blanket of your crochet project.

1. Chain 20. Do not turn or add any more stitches yet. Hold your chain out in front of you.
2. With your finger, trace the working edge: the live loop on your hook, the last chain made, the hook itself. This is your Markov Blanket — the boundary between inside and outside.
3. Point to the internal states: the completed chain stitches behind the live loop. These are the accumulated results of your actions.
4. Point to the external states: the ball of yarn, the pattern you might follow, the table, the air.
5. Now, deliberately let the live loop slip off the hook. Watch what happens. Pull the yarn end — the chain unravels. The boundary has been broken.
6. Re-hook the loop. The boundary is restored.

### Part 2: Tension as Precision (15 minutes)

> **Intention:** Experience precision through deliberate tension changes.

1. Using your chain from Part 1, single crochet across the row with your normal tension. Count your stitches — you should have 20.
2. Turn. For the next row, crochet with deliberately tight tension — grip the yarn harder, pull loops smaller. Complete the row.
3. Turn again. For this row, crochet with deliberately loose tension — relax your hand, let the loops be large and floppy. Complete the row.
4. Turn one more time and return to your normal tension.
5. Hold up your swatch and look at the three rows. Notice the differences in stitch size, fabric density, and overall appearance.

**Reflection**: How did tight tension change your sensory experience? What about loose tension? Which felt more "precise" — and in what sense? How does tension affect the information you receive from each stitch?

### Part 3: The Prediction Error Experiment (20 minutes)

> **Intention:** Deliberately create and correct prediction errors.

1. Chain 21. Single crochet across (20 sc). Turn.
2. Row 2: Single crochet 10 stitches. Then deliberately skip one stitch (do not crochet into it). Continue to the end. You should have 19 stitches instead of 20.
3. Turn and begin Row 3 with your normal rhythm. As you crochet, pay attention to the moment you reach the skipped stitch from the previous row. Notice: the fabric pulls, the gap is visible, your hook may hesitate.
4. Continue to the end of Row 3. Count your stitches.
5. Now frog Rows 2 and 3. Pull out the yarn, stitch by stitch, back to Row 1.
6. Re-crochet Row 2 correctly — 20 single crochets, no skips.

**Reflection**: Describe the moment you noticed the prediction error (the skipped stitch). What sensory signals told you something was wrong? What was the prediction your hands expected, and what was the reality they encountered? How did frogging feel — was it frustrating, satisfying, or both?

### Part 4: Epistemic Action with Stitch Markers (10 minutes)

> **Intention:** Experience how offloading information to the environment reduces cognitive load.

1. Crochet 3 more rows of 20 sc without any stitch markers. At the end of each row, count your stitches.
2. Now place a stitch marker every 5 stitches (at stitches 5, 10, and 15).
3. Crochet 3 more rows with the markers in place. Notice whether you still need to count every stitch, or whether the markers let you count in groups of 5.

{fill:textarea}
**Reflection**: How did the stitch markers change your experience? Did you think less, notice more, or feel more confident? In Active Inference terms, the markers reduced your cognitive free energy by offloading state estimation to the environment. Describe this in your own words.

### Part 5: Reading Your Work (10 minutes)

> **Intention:** Practice state estimation by reading the fabric.

1. Set your work down and look at it without counting. Based on the height, the number of rows, and the overall appearance, estimate: How many rows have you completed? How many stitches are in the current row? Is your tension consistent across the piece?
2. Now count to verify. How close were your estimates?
3. Turn the fabric over and examine the back. Can you identify where the tension changed? Where the skipped stitch was? Where the frogging happened?

**Reflection**: Reading your work is state estimation — using sensory evidence (looking at the fabric) to update your beliefs about the hidden states (row count, stitch count, tension consistency). How accurate was your initial estimate, and what cues did you use?

## Discussion Questions

1. After this lab, do you experience crocheting differently? Has the language of Active Inference changed how you notice what your hands are doing?
2. Which part of the lab produced the strongest feeling of prediction error? What does this tell you about where your generative model is most precise?
3. If you were teaching someone to crochet for the first time, how would you help them calibrate their precision (tension) without overwhelming them with prediction errors?

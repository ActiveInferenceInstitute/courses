# Glossary: Active Inference: Crochet Circles

> Definitions of key terms used throughout the curriculum.
> Each entry connects crochet practice to Active Inference concepts.

---

### Blocking

Wetting and pinning a finished piece to its intended dimensions so it holds the correct shape. In Active Inference terms, blocking is a post-hoc correction that forces the realized object to conform to the generative model's predictions about final geometry — reducing residual prediction error after the active stitching phase is complete.

### Chain (ch)

The foundational stitch that forms the starting row of most crochet work. The chain is the initial prior — a structural commitment that constrains all subsequent inference. Every stitch built on the chain inherits its assumptions about width, tension, and spacing.

### Colorwork

Working with multiple yarn colors to create patterns in the fabric. Colorwork requires maintaining parallel generative models — one for stitch structure, another for color placement — and coordinating predictions across both. Errors in one model (dropped stitch) may cascade into the other (color misalignment).

### Counting

The act of counting stitches at the end of a row to verify the current state matches the expected state. This is explicit state estimation — comparing observed stitch count to the prediction derived from the pattern. A mismatch triggers either frogging (active error correction) or creative adaptation (model revision).

### Decreasing (dec)

Combining two or more stitches into one, reducing the stitch count and narrowing the fabric. Decreasing is a controlled reduction in model complexity — the generative model predicts convergence and the crocheter executes it, producing a fabric that literally narrows its state space.

### Dye Lot

A batch of yarn dyed together, ensuring color consistency. Working across dye lots introduces unexpected sensory prediction errors — subtle color shifts that the crocheter may not detect until the piece is finished. Matching dye lots is a form of prior precision control, ensuring the material will behave as the model predicts.

### Ease

The difference between body measurements and garment measurements, built into a pattern to ensure fit and comfort. Ease is the tolerance band in the generative model — the acceptable range of prediction error between the ideal form and the lived, moving body that will wear the garment.

### Frogging

Undoing completed stitches by pulling the yarn to unravel the work — named for "rip it, rip it" (ribbit). Frogging is the crocheter's response to accumulated prediction error exceeding a tolerance threshold. When the divergence between the pattern's predictions and the realized fabric becomes unacceptable, the crocheter resets to a known-good state and re-executes inference from there.

### Gauge

The number of stitches and rows per unit of measurement (typically per 4 inches / 10 cm), determined by yarn weight, hook size, and individual tension. Gauge is the crocheter's likelihood function — the mapping between actions (stitches) and outcomes (fabric dimensions). Getting gauge right means your generative model's predictions will match reality at scale.

### Gauge Swatch

A test piece crocheted before starting a project to verify that the crocheter's gauge matches the pattern's specifications. The gauge swatch is prior precision tuning — a deliberate, low-cost experiment to calibrate the generative model before committing resources to a full project.

### Hook Size

The diameter of the crochet hook, which directly affects stitch size and fabric density. Changing hook size is a control parameter adjustment — modifying the instrument to bring the action-outcome mapping (gauge) in line with the generative model's requirements.

### Increasing (inc)

Adding extra stitches to widen the fabric, typically by working two or more stitches into a single stitch from the previous row. Increasing is controlled expansion of the state space — the generative model predicts divergence and the crocheter executes it, producing fabric that grows according to plan.

### Joining

Connecting the end of a row to its beginning (as in working in the round) or attaching separate pieces together. Joining is a topological prediction — the generative model expects closure, and the crocheter verifies alignment before committing. A misaligned join is a high-cost prediction error.

### Magic Ring

An adjustable loop used to start crocheting in the round, allowing the center hole to be pulled tight. The magic ring is an elegant example of deferred commitment — the crocheter keeps the initial state adjustable until enough evidence (surrounding stitches) constrains it, then tightens to lock in the final configuration.

### Muscle Memory

The deeply habituated motor patterns that allow experienced crocheters to stitch without conscious attention to each movement. In Active Inference, muscle memory represents priors with extremely high precision — the generative model's predictions about hand movements are so confident that prediction errors are minimized automatically, freeing attentional resources for higher-level planning.

### Pattern

A written set of instructions that specifies the sequence of stitches needed to produce a particular object. The pattern is a generative model in its most explicit form — a hierarchical set of predictions about what the finished object will look like, decomposed row by row into executable actions.

### Pattern Repeat

A recurring sequence of stitches that tiles across a row or round, creating visual rhythm in the fabric. A pattern repeat is hierarchical structure in the generative model — a chunk of predictions that can be iterated, reducing the complexity of the full model to a compact, repeatable unit.

### Reading the Work

Examining the fabric itself (rather than the written pattern) to understand what has been done and what comes next. Reading the work is perceptual inference — extracting state information directly from the sensory evidence of the fabric rather than relying solely on the top-down predictions of the written pattern.

### Row Counter

A mechanical or digital device used to track the current row number. Like a stitch marker, the row counter is an epistemic artifact — an external memory device that offloads state tracking from working memory to the environment, reducing the cognitive free energy cost of maintaining position in a complex pattern.

### Stash

A crocheter's personal collection of yarn, accumulated over time. The stash is a resource model — the crocheter's beliefs about what materials are available for future projects. Stash management involves planning under uncertainty: which yarns will be needed, what quantities remain, and whether current stock supports intended projects.

### Stitch Marker

A small clip or ring placed in the work to mark a specific position — the beginning of a round, the location of an increase, or a pattern boundary. A stitch marker is an epistemic action that offloads state tracking to the environment. Rather than holding position information in working memory (increasing cognitive load), the crocheter delegates it to a physical marker, reducing free energy.

### Swatching

The general practice of making test pieces to explore how a yarn, hook, or stitch pattern behaves. Swatching is hypothesis testing — generating small-scale predictions and comparing them to outcomes before scaling up. It reduces the expected free energy of a full project by resolving material uncertainties in advance.

### Tension

The force applied to the working yarn as it flows through the crocheter's fingers during stitching. Tension is precision weighting in the sensorimotor loop — it determines how tightly controlled each stitch is and how much variability the crocheter tolerates. High tension produces tight, precise stitches (high precision); loose tension produces open, variable stitches (low precision).

### Turning Chain

The chain stitches made at the beginning of a new row to bring the hook up to the correct height for the next stitch type. The turning chain is a transition prediction — the generative model must account for the height change between rows and produce the correct number of chains to maintain structural coherence.

### Work in Progress (WIP)

A crochet project that has been started but not yet completed. A WIP is an ongoing inference process — the partially realized object embodies all the predictions made so far and constrains the remaining predictions. The crocheter's relationship to their WIPs reflects their capacity for managing multiple concurrent generative models.

### Working in the Round

Crocheting in a continuous spiral or joined rounds rather than back-and-forth rows, producing tubular or circular fabric. Working in the round is a topological shift in the generative model — predictions must account for closure and spiral structure rather than linear rows. The crocheter must track position differently, often relying on stitch markers to maintain state awareness.

### Yarn Over (yo)

Wrapping the yarn around the hook before pulling it through a loop — the fundamental sub-action of nearly every crochet stitch. The yarn over is the atomic prediction-action unit. It is the smallest meaningful motor sequence, and its fluent execution is the foundation on which all higher-level stitch predictions are built.

### Yarn Weight

The thickness category of yarn (lace, fingering, sport, DK, worsted, bulky, super bulky), which determines hook size, gauge, and fabric characteristics. Yarn weight is a prior constraint on the generative model — selecting a yarn weight commits the crocheter to a range of expected outcomes for stitch size, drape, and warmth, narrowing the space of possible fabrics before the first stitch is made.

## Topology & Neural Network Terms (Course 5: Loop & Lattice)

### Topology

The mathematical study of properties preserved under continuous deformation — stretching, bending, twisting, but not tearing or gluing. Crochet is inherently topological: the fabric's connectivity (which stitch connects to which) is preserved even when the fabric is stretched or draped. A crocheted tube remains a tube whether you squeeze it flat or stretch it wide. In Active Inference, the topology of the generative model determines which states can influence which others.

### Gaussian Curvature

A measure of how a surface curves at a point, defined as the product of the two principal curvatures. Flat fabric has zero Gaussian curvature. A bowl (positive curvature) cups inward. A hyperbolic ruffle (negative curvature) flares outward. In crochet, increases and decreases are the primary operators that control Gaussian curvature — they are how the crocheter sculpts the shape of the manifold.

### Hyperbolic Plane

A surface of constant negative Gaussian curvature — it ruffles and flares in every direction. Famously difficult to visualize in flat media, the hyperbolic plane was first physically modeled by mathematician Daina Taimina using crochet. The exponential increase pattern (doubling stitches every N rows) produces hyperbolic fabric. In Active Inference terms, a hyperbolic surface is one where the state space expands faster than the agent can explore it — a model of unbounded uncertainty.

### Manifold

A mathematical space that locally resembles flat Euclidean space but may have complex global structure. Every piece of crochet fabric is a 2-dimensional manifold — locally flat around each stitch, but potentially curved, twisted, or closed globally (as in a hat or a torus). In neural networks, the data manifold is the surface along which meaningful inputs lie, and learning is the process of discovering this surface.

### Euler Characteristic (χ)

A topological invariant defined as V - E + F (vertices minus edges plus faces) for a polyhedral surface. For a flat disc, χ = 1. For a sphere, χ = 2. For a torus (donut), χ = 0. A crocheter planning a project is implicitly choosing an Euler characteristic — a flat blanket (χ = 1) versus a hat (χ = 2) versus a cowl (χ = 0) have fundamentally different topological requirements.

### Genus

The number of "handles" or "holes" in a surface. A sphere has genus 0. A torus (donut) has genus 1. A pretzel shape has genus 3. In crochet, the genus determines how many times the fabric must be joined or closed — a simple hat (genus 0) versus a torus cowl (genus 1) versus a linked chain (higher genus). Each increase in genus requires a topological joining operation.

### Neural Network

A computational model inspired by biological neural systems, consisting of interconnected nodes (neurons) organized in layers. Each connection has a weight that determines signal strength. In the Loop & Lattice course, crocheted fabric is treated as a physical neural network: each stitch is a node, each connection to an adjacent stitch is a weighted edge, and the fabric's structure determines how information (tension, force, pattern) propagates through the network.

### Hidden Layer

In a neural network, a layer of nodes between the input and output that transforms information through weighted connections and activation functions. In crochet, each row between the foundation chain (input) and the final row (output) functions as a hidden layer — it receives input from the row below, transforms it through stitch operations, and passes its output to the row above. Complex stitch patterns create richer hidden representations.

### Backpropagation

The algorithm by which neural networks learn — error signals flow backward from the output layer through hidden layers to adjust weights. In crochet, frogging (ripping back rows) is the physical analog: error detected in the current fabric propagates backward through the rows as the crocheter undoes stitches, then re-executes with adjusted "weights" (tension, stitch choice). This is literally backward propagation of error through the layered structure.

### Activation Function

In a neural network, the function that determines a node's output given its inputs — introducing nonlinearity that allows the network to learn complex patterns. In crochet, different stitch types serve as activation functions: a single crochet (compact, linear-like) versus a shell stitch (expansive, nonlinear) versus a bobble (threshold-like, producing output only when enough yarn-overs accumulate). The choice of stitch determines the character of the layer.

### Loss Landscape

The surface defined by how wrong a neural network's predictions are across all possible weight configurations. Smooth landscapes are easy to optimize; rugged landscapes with many local minima are challenging. In crochet, the "loss landscape" of a project maps all possible combinations of tension, stitch choice, and placement to how far the result is from the intended pattern. A simple scarf has a smooth landscape; a complex doily has a rugged one.

### Overfitting

In machine learning, when a model learns the training data too specifically and fails to generalize to new examples. In crochet, a crocheter who has only ever made granny squares has "overfit" to that pattern — they execute it perfectly but struggle with new stitch patterns or project types. Generalization (a well-rounded skill set) comes from practicing diverse patterns and techniques.

### Filet Crochet

A mesh-based crochet technique that creates grid patterns using alternating solid blocks and open spaces. Filet crochet is the most explicitly network-like form of crochet — the fabric IS a grid graph where filled and empty cells encode information, much like a binary neural network where connections are either active or inactive.

### Möbius Strip

A surface with only one side and one edge, created by giving a strip a half-twist before joining the ends. Can be crocheted by working a foundation chain, giving it a half-twist, and joining to crochet in the round. The Möbius strip is a favorite of mathematical crocheters because it demonstrates a non-trivial topological property (non-orientability) in a tangible, wearable form.

### Graph (Network)

A mathematical structure consisting of nodes (vertices) connected by edges. Crocheted fabric is a graph: each stitch is a node, and each connection between adjacent stitches is an edge. The degree of a node (how many edges connect to it) depends on the stitch type — a single crochet has fewer connections than a shell stitch. Analyzing crochet as a graph reveals its network topology.

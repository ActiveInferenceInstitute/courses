# FAQ: Active Inference: Crochet Circles

> Frequently asked questions for fiber artists, crafters, crochet circle participants, and the curious.

## General

### What is Active Inference?

Active Inference is a theory from neuroscience and biology that describes how all living systems work: by maintaining an internal model of the world, generating predictions based on that model, and then acting to make those predictions come true — or updating the model when predictions fail. Every crocheter does this with every stitch. You hold a pattern in mind (the model), predict what the next stitch should look like (the prediction), execute the stitch (the action), and check whether it came out right (comparing prediction to observation). When it does not match — you frog it. That is Active Inference in action.

### What is the Free Energy Principle?

The Free Energy Principle is the mathematical foundation of Active Inference. It says that living systems maintain their organization by minimizing "free energy" — roughly, the mismatch between what they expect and what they experience. In crochet terms: free energy is the gap between what the pattern says and what your fabric looks like. You minimize it by stitching correctly (action), noticing errors early (perception), or accepting a creative variation (model update).

### Do I need to know math to use this curriculum?

No. This curriculum uses crochet as the primary language. Active Inference concepts are introduced through craft metaphors and hands-on experience, not equations. If you can follow a crochet pattern, you can follow this curriculum. The [Notation Table](./notation_table.md) maps familiar crochet abbreviations to Active Inference ideas.

### Do I need to know how to crochet?

Basic crochet skills are helpful, especially for the labs and hands-on exercises. If you are a complete beginner, start with Course 1 (Stitch & Structure), which introduces stitch mechanics alongside Active Inference concepts. You will learn both at the same time.

## About the Courses

### Why are there 5 courses instead of 1?

Each course examines crochet — and Active Inference — from a different vantage point. Course 1 looks at individual stitch mechanics. Course 2 explores materials and flow states. Course 3 focuses on patterns as symbolic models. Course 4 examines the social dynamics of crafting groups. Course 5 (Loop & Lattice) reveals the topological and neural network structure hidden in every piece of crochet. Together, they show how the same principles operate at every scale, from a single yarn-over to the geometry of surfaces.

### What are the 8 modules about?

Every course covers the same 8 foundational Active Inference topics, interpreted through crochet:

1. **Systems** — What counts as a system? Where are its boundaries? (The hook-yarn boundary, the pattern, the circle)
2. **Agents** — What makes something an agent? (The crocheter, the hands, the pattern reader, each person in the circle)
3. **Perception** — How do we sense and observe? (Feeling tension, reading stitches, noticing the room)
4. **Cognition** — How do we think and model? (Thinking in loops, mental images of the finished piece, shared understanding)
5. **Action** — How do we act on the world? (Every stitch, every frogging, every demonstration)
6. **Learning** — How do we improve? (Muscle memory, material knowledge, pattern literacy, cultural transmission)
7. **Communication** — How do we share? (Stitch notation, color language, pattern standards, stories)
8. **Planning** — How do we anticipate the future? (Project choice, color palettes, yardage estimates, crochet-alongs)

### Can I start with any course?

Yes. Each course is self-contained. However, if you are new to both crochet and Active Inference, Course 1 (Stitch & Structure) provides the most concrete starting point. See [Learning Pathways](./learning_pathways.md) for recommended routes.

### How are the labs structured?

Each module includes a lab designed for the course's perspective:

- **Stitch & Structure** labs are hands-on stitch exercises — you crochet while observing your own inference process
- **Fiber & Flow** labs are materials explorations — you compare yarns, test textures, and notice your sensory responses
- **Pattern & Prediction** labs are pattern design workshops — you read, modify, and create patterns
- **Circle & Community** labs are group activities — designed for crochet circles to do together
- **Loop & Lattice** labs are topology and network explorations — you crochet hyperbolic planes, map stitch graphs, trace signal paths, and design network-inspired pieces

## Conceptual Questions

### How is a crochet pattern a "generative model"?

A crochet pattern is a compact set of instructions that, when executed, produces a specific object. It predicts — row by row, stitch by stitch — what the finished fabric will look like. This is exactly what a generative model does in Active Inference: it generates predictions about the world. The crocheter uses the pattern to predict what comes next, then checks the result against the prediction. When reality and prediction match, the crocheter moves on. When they diverge, the crocheter corrects (frogs) or adapts (modifies the pattern).

### What is a "Markov blanket" in crochet?

A Markov blanket is the boundary that separates a system from its environment — specifically, the surface through which all interaction must pass. In crochet, the hook-yarn interface is a Markov blanket. Everything the crocheter knows about the fabric comes through the hook and the yarn in their fingers. The hook mediates between the crocheter's intentions (internal states) and the fabric (external world). Similarly, each crocheter in a circle has their own Markov blanket — what they can see, hear, and feel from their seat at the table.

### Why is frogging the "quintessential prediction-error response"?

Frogging (ripping out stitches) happens when the crocheter detects that the accumulated error between pattern and fabric has exceeded their tolerance. This is textbook prediction-error-driven behavior: the agent (crocheter) monitors the divergence between predictions (pattern) and observations (fabric), and when that divergence crosses a threshold, takes a corrective action (frogging) to return to a known-good state. It is costly (lost time and effort) but reduces future prediction error by preventing the error from compounding.

### How does a crochet circle function as a "collective agent"?

A crochet circle shares generative models: common pattern conventions, shared techniques, group aesthetic preferences, and collective knowledge of materials. When a new member joins, they undergo legitimate peripheral participation (sitting, watching, asking questions) before becoming a full participant. The circle maintains its identity over time — its "self-model" — through shared practices, regular meeting times, and transmitted traditions. It minimizes collective free energy by teaching newcomers (reducing their uncertainty) and preserving techniques (maintaining the group's predictive accuracy).

### Is this just a metaphor, or is it real science?

Both. The Active Inference framework is a serious, mathematically grounded theory of biological self-organization. The crochet mappings in this curriculum are genuine structural analogies — the same formal principles (prediction, error, model updating, boundary maintenance) apply to both neural systems and craft practice. A crocheter really does maintain a model, generate predictions, and minimize error. The curriculum uses crochet to make these principles tangible, not to trivialize them.

### How does topology connect to crochet?

The connection is deep and literal. Mathematician Daina Taimina proved in 1997 that crochet is uniquely suited to creating physical models of hyperbolic geometry — something that cannot be done with paper or rigid materials. The Institute For Figuring's Hyperbolic Crochet Coral Reef project has brought this mathematical craft to museums worldwide. Course 5 (Loop & Lattice) explores these connections: every increase controls Gaussian curvature, every join changes the topology, and the stitch network itself is a mathematical graph with the same structure as a neural network.

### Do I need to understand neural networks for Course 5?

No. Loop & Lattice introduces neural network concepts through crochet analogies, just as the other courses introduce Active Inference through craft. If you can visualize a crocheted mesh where each stitch connects to its neighbors, you already understand the basic idea of a neural network. The course builds from there.

## Practical Questions

### What materials do I need?

For most labs: a crochet hook (size H/5mm is a good default), a ball of medium-weight yarn (worsted/aran), a tapestry needle, stitch markers, and a row counter. Course 2 (Fiber & Flow) labs will ask you to work with multiple yarn types. Course 4 (Circle & Community) labs require at least 3-4 people.

### Can knitters use this curriculum too?

Many of the Active Inference concepts transfer directly to knitting and other fiber arts. However, the specific stitch mechanics, notation, and terminology in this curriculum are crochet-specific. A knitting adaptation would be a separate domain course.

### Can I use this curriculum for a crochet club or class?

Absolutely. Course 4 (Circle & Community) is specifically designed for group settings, and Pathway 5 in the [Learning Pathways](./learning_pathways.md) provides an 8-week circle study plan. Any module's lab can be adapted for group use.

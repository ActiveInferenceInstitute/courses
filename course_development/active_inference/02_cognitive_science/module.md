## Introduction

If Philosophy asks what Active Inference means, Cognitive Science asks how it works in practice — in brains, in bodies, in behavior. This section bridges the gap between abstract theory and empirical neuroscience, examining how the brain implements the generative models, prediction errors, and precision weighting that the philosophical section introduced conceptually.

The brain is an approximately 86-billion-neuron prediction machine organized into hierarchical assemblies, from cortical columns to large-scale networks. Each level of this hierarchy generates predictions about the level below and receives prediction errors from it. Sensory cortex and motor cortex form the Markov Blanket of the brain, mediating all interchange between internal neural states and the external world. This architecture, when it works well, produces adaptive perception, flexible cognition, skilled action, and cumulative learning. When it goes wrong — when precision is miscalibrated or models fail to update — the result is the constellation of conditions that psychiatry and neurology study.

## Key Concepts

### 1. Predictive Coding and the Cortical Hierarchy

The brain's cortical hierarchy implements Active Inference through bidirectional message passing. Top-down connections carry predictions; bottom-up connections carry prediction errors. This architecture, first formalized by Rao and Ballard (1999) for the visual cortex, has been generalized to explain cortical processing across all modalities. The empirical evidence includes mismatch negativity in auditory cortex, repetition suppression in visual cortex, and the predictive modulation of neural oscillations.

### 2. Precision, Neuromodulation, and Attention

Precision — the confidence assigned to prediction errors — is implemented through neuromodulatory systems. Dopamine, acetylcholine, norepinephrine, and serotonin modulate the gain of neural populations, determining which prediction errors are amplified and which are suppressed. This neuromodulatory architecture explains selective attention, cognitive flexibility, and the effects of drugs and neurological conditions on perception and cognition.

### 3. Clinical Active Inference

Many psychiatric and neurological conditions can be understood as specific failures of the Active Inference machinery. Schizophrenia may involve aberrant precision on sensory prediction errors (producing hallucinations) or on prior beliefs (producing delusions). Autism may reflect uniformly high sensory precision, leading to sensory overload and difficulty with social inference. Anxiety disorders may involve overweighting of threat-related predictions. Depression may involve imprecise or inflexible generative models that resist updating.

### 4. Neural Learning and Consolidation

Synaptic plasticity (Hebbian learning, long-term potentiation) implements the gradient descent on free energy that constitutes learning. Sleep, particularly during REM and slow-wave phases, may implement Bayesian model reduction — pruning unnecessary model complexity while consolidating essential structure. This connects to empirical findings on memory consolidation and the role of dreaming.

## Applications

* **fMRI Resting-State Networks**: The default mode network, salience network, and central executive network can be understood as components of the brain's hierarchical generative model operating at different timescales. Resting-state activity reflects the brain's ongoing priors — the predictions it maintains even in the absence of external stimulation. This interpretation connects Active Inference to one of the most active areas of neuroimaging research.

* **The Rubber Hand Illusion and Body Ownership**: When synchronized visual and tactile stimulation leads a person to experience a rubber hand as their own, the brain is updating its generative model of the body in response to multisensory prediction errors. This paradigm demonstrates that body ownership is not fixed but is actively inferred — a prediction that can be manipulated experimentally.

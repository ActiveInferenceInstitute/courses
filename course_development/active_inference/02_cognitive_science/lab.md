# Lab: Investigating the Predictive Brain

## Objective

Analyze empirical evidence for Active Inference in the brain through case study interpretation, experimental data analysis, and neural correlate mapping.

## Materials

* Access to at least two of the following papers:
  - Rao & Ballard (1999) "Predictive coding in the visual cortex"
  - Seth (2013) "Interoceptive inference, emotion, and the embodied self"
  - Feldman & Friston (2010) "Attention, uncertainty, and free-energy"
  - Adams et al. (2013) "Predictions not commands: active inference in the motor system"
* Notebook or document for written analysis
* Optional: access to a simple visual illusion demonstration (e.g., Kanizsa triangle, binocular rivalry images)

## Procedure

### Part 1: Mapping the Cortical Hierarchy (20 minutes)

Draw or describe the flow of information through the cortical hierarchy for a specific sensory modality (vision, audition, or touch). For each level, specify:

1. What predictions are generated (top-down)
2. What prediction errors are passed upward (bottom-up)
3. What aspect of the stimulus is represented at this level (features, objects, scenes, context)

Example hierarchy for vision: V1 (edges, orientations) -> V2 (textures, contours) -> V4 (shapes, colors) -> IT (objects, faces) -> PFC (context, goals).

**Discussion**: Where in this hierarchy would you expect precision weighting to have the greatest effect on conscious experience? Why?

### Part 2: Clinical Case Analysis (25 minutes)

Analyze the following clinical vignettes using Active Inference concepts. For each, identify the hypothesized aberration (precision too high, too low, model fails to update, etc.) and predict what neural signature you would expect to observe.

**Case A**: A 28-year-old patient reports hearing voices that comment on their actions throughout the day. They are certain the voices are external. Brain imaging shows hyperactivation in auditory cortex even during silence.

**Case B**: A 10-year-old child is overwhelmed by the texture of certain fabrics and the sound of a classroom. They notice details others miss but struggle to integrate information into a coherent social picture.

**Case C**: A 45-year-old patient with generalized anxiety disorder reports constant worry about unlikely catastrophic events. They intellectually know the events are improbable but cannot stop the anxious predictions.

For each case, specify: (1) the Active Inference mechanism involved, (2) the precision aberration, and (3) one testable prediction.

### Part 3: Prediction Error Demonstration (15 minutes)

If available, examine a visual illusion (Kanizsa triangle, bistable figures, or motion aftereffect). Describe your experience using Active Inference language:

1. What prediction does your brain generate before seeing the stimulus?
2. What prediction error is generated by the stimulus?
3. How does your perception change as your generative model updates?

If no illusion is available, describe a recent everyday experience where your expectations were violated (e.g., reaching for a light object that turned out to be heavy). Analyze this using the prediction error framework.

### Part 4: Neural Correlate Mapping (20 minutes)

For three of the following Active Inference concepts, identify the neural structure(s) most associated with their implementation and explain the mapping:

1. Sensory prediction error
2. Precision weighting
3. Prior beliefs / self-model
4. Motor inference
5. Model updating during sleep
6. Social inference / Theory of Mind

Present your mappings in a table with columns for: Active Inference Concept, Neural Structure(s), Evidence (at least one study or finding), and Limitations of the mapping.

## Discussion Questions

1. Which clinical condition do you find most compellingly explained by the Active Inference framework? Which explanation seems weakest?
2. How does mapping Active Inference onto neural structures strengthen or weaken the framework compared to treating it as a purely computational theory?
3. What experiment would you design to test a specific prediction of Active Inference about brain function that has not yet been tested?

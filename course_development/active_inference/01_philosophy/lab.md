# Lab: Philosophical Inquiry into Active Inference

## Objective

Engage with the central philosophical claims of Active Inference through structured thought experiments, close reading of primary sources, and the construction of original philosophical arguments.

## Materials

* Access to at least two of the following texts:
  - Friston (2010) "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?"
  - Clark (2013) "Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents"
  - Kirchhoff & Froese (2017) "Where there is life there is mind"
  - Maturana & Varela (1980) *Autopoiesis and Cognition* (Chapter 2)
  - Merleau-Ponty (1945) *Phenomenology of Perception* (Introduction)
* Notebook or document for written reflection
* 90 minutes of uninterrupted time

## Procedure

### Part 1: The Boundary Thought Experiment (20 minutes)

Consider the following scenarios and identify where the Markov Blanket lies in each case. For each, specify the internal states, external states, sensory states, and active states.

1. A single-celled organism in a pond
2. A human being wearing a winter coat
3. A driver using GPS navigation
4. A person thinking silently in a dark room

For scenario 4, consider: does the absence of external sensory input mean the Markov Blanket has collapsed, or is the agent still performing inference? What does this tell you about the relationship between the blanket and consciousness?

**Discussion**: Which scenarios challenge the idea that the Markov Blanket is a fixed physical boundary? What do they suggest about the dynamic nature of system boundaries?

### Part 2: Close Reading — The Life-Mind Continuity Thesis (25 minutes)

Read Kirchhoff & Froese (2017), Section 2 ("From Life to Mind"), or the equivalent section in Clark (2013) on "prediction machines."

Answer the following:

1. What is the strongest argument the authors present for the continuity between life and mind?
2. What is the most serious objection to this thesis? Do the authors address it adequately?
3. Does this thesis commit the "fallacy of equivocation" — using "inference" in a technical sense for simple organisms and a folk sense for humans? Defend your position.

### Part 3: The Dark Room Debate (20 minutes)

Reconstruct the dark room objection to Active Inference in your own words. Then:

1. Present the standard response (prior preferences make the dark room surprising).
2. Evaluate whether this response is philosophically satisfying or merely a patch on the theory.
3. Connect the dark room problem to Aristotle's concept of *eudaimonia* (flourishing). Does Active Inference imply that agents are constitutively oriented toward flourishing?

### Part 4: Position Paper Draft (25 minutes)

Write a 500-word position paper on one of the following questions:

1. Is the Free Energy Principle a law of nature, a useful fiction, or something else entirely?
2. Does Active Inference successfully bridge the explanatory gap between mechanism and experience?
3. Can an Active Inference agent have genuine free will?

Your paper should include: a clear thesis statement, at least one argument for your position, at least one objection you anticipate, and a response to that objection.

## Discussion Questions

1. After completing this lab, has your understanding of what counts as a "system" changed? In what way?
2. Where do you see the greatest philosophical promise in Active Inference? Where do you see the greatest philosophical vulnerability?
3. How does engaging with these thought experiments differ from reading about Active Inference in a textbook? What does the first-person perspective add?

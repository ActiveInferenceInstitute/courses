## Introduction

This module explores **Systems**. In the **401** curriculum, we approach this topic with a focus on specific applications and theoretical depth appropriate for the audience. Systems is a critical component of the 8-part Active Inference spine, bridging the gap between the course introduction and Neuroscientific Frontiers.

At the advanced PhD level, the philosophical foundations unit interrogates the deepest assumptions underlying systems thinking in Active Inference. Rather than simply accepting the Markov blanket formalism as a given, we examine its philosophical commitments: What ontological status do Markov blankets have? Are they observer-dependent partitions or mind-independent features of reality? How does the free energy principle relate to autopoietic theories of life (Maturana & Varela, 1980) and to enactivist accounts of cognition (Thompson, 2007)? This unit demands that students engage critically with the metaphysics of boundaries, causation, and explanation that the Active Inference framework presupposes.

The eight modules in this unit traverse the Active Inference spine from a philosophical vantage. Systems are examined through the lens of process philosophy and systems ontology; agents are analyzed through debates on minimal cognition and biological autonomy; perception is situated within the long tradition of epistemological inquiry from Kant through Helmholtz to contemporary predictive processing; cognition raises questions about the explanatory scope of the free energy principle; action connects to pragmatist and phenomenological traditions; learning engages with nativist-empiricist debates; communication touches on social ontology and the philosophy of language; and planning raises questions about temporal consciousness and free will.

## Applications

In 401, we see Systems manifest in:
*   **Specific Example 1**: The debate between realist and instrumentalist interpretations of Markov blankets in biological systems. Kirchhoff et al. (2018, "The Markov blankets of life") argue that Markov blankets constitute the ontological boundaries of living systems, grounding a naturalistic account of self-organization. In contrast, Bruineberg et al. (2022, "The Emperor's New Markov Blankets") contend that Markov blankets are statistical constructs that do not straightforwardly map onto physical boundaries, and that conflating the two commits a "reification fallacy." PhD students should critically evaluate both positions, examining whether the free energy principle provides a constitutive explanation of life and mind or merely a descriptive statistical framework, and what this means for the explanatory ambitions of Active Inference as a unified theory of cognition.
*   **Specific Example 2**: The relationship between the free energy principle and the good regulator theorem (Conant & Ashby, 1970), which states that every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system. At the 401 level, this connection is not merely analogical but mathematically precise: the variational free energy bound can be derived from information-theoretic principles that generalize the good regulator theorem to stochastic dynamical systems. Students should trace this lineage through Friston's (2010) seminal paper on the free energy principle, connecting it to Jaynes' maximum entropy formalism, the Helmholtz machine of Dayan et al. (1995), and contemporary work on the Bayesian brain hypothesis. The philosophical question at stake is whether the free energy principle is a tautological restatement of what it means to be a persisting system or a substantive empirical claim about the dynamics of self-organizing systems.

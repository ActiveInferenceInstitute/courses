## Content Guidance

This module must stage the confrontation between two great traditions of understanding perception: the phenomenological tradition (especially Merleau-Ponty's account of embodied, pre-reflective perceptual engagement) and the Helmholtzian tradition (perception as unconscious inference, now formalized as predictive processing). Content must rigorously evaluate the "compatibility thesis" — the claim that predictive processing and phenomenology are not merely consistent but mutually illuminating — advanced by Bruineberg and Rietveld (2014) and contested by Hohwy (2013). The module must also engage Gibson's ecological psychology as a third position that challenges the very framing of the debate (perception as neither inference nor phenomenological constitution, but direct pickup of affordances). The "hard problem of perceptual content" — whether generative models produce representations with genuine phenomenal character or merely functional states that are *about* the environment in a deflationary sense — must be addressed head-on.

## Key Concepts

- **Merleau-Ponty vs. Helmholtz**: The contrast between perception as bodily engagement with a meaningful world (motor intentionality, the primacy of the body schema, the pre-objective "flesh" of experience) and perception as computational inference from noisy sensory data. The FEP appears to side with Helmholtz, but Merleau-Pontian readings are possible if one emphasizes the embodied, enactive character of active inference.
- **The compatibility thesis**: Bruineberg and Rietveld's (2014) argument that the FEP, properly understood through the lens of skilled intentionality and the "skilled body," is compatible with — and even enriched by — phenomenological insights. Hohwy's counter that the Markov blanket creates an "evidentiary boundary" (an inferential veil) that makes the FEP a form of indirect realism incompatible with phenomenological direct contact with the world.
- **Direct perception (Gibson)**: The ecological claim that organisms perceive affordances directly — opportunities for action specified by invariants in the optic array — without the mediation of internal models. The question is whether the FEP's generative model framework can accommodate Gibsonian direct realism or necessarily entails representationalism.
- **The hard problem of perceptual content**: Even if the FEP provides an adequate *functional* account of perception (explaining behavior, prediction error, etc.), does it explain *why there is something it is like* to perceive? Does the minimization of variational free energy entail phenomenal consciousness, or is it neutral on the question?

## Key References

- **Merleau-Ponty, M. (1945/2012)**: *Phenomenology of Perception*. The founding text of embodied phenomenology. Central for this module: the analyses of the phantom limb, the body schema, motor intentionality, and the pre-objective character of perceptual experience. Students must engage with the primary text, not merely summaries.
- **Hohwy, J. (2013)**: *The Predictive Mind*. The most fully developed philosophical defense of predictive processing as a theory of mind. Argues for a strong representationalist reading: the brain is an "inference machine" separated from the world by a Markov blanket that functions as an epistemic boundary. Critical foil for enactivist and phenomenological readings.
- **Bruineberg, J. & Rietveld, E. (2014)**: "Self-organization, free energy minimization, and optimal grip on a field of affordances." Argues that the FEP is best understood not as a representationalist theory but as an account of how organisms maintain "optimal grip" on a landscape of affordances — a reading that draws on Merleau-Ponty and avoids the inferentialist language that Hohwy embraces.
- **Gallagher, S. & Allen, M. (2018)**: "Active inference, enactivism and the hermeneutics of social cognition." Extends the compatibility discussion to social perception, arguing that active inference can be read enactively if one de-emphasizes internal models and emphasizes the dynamics of organism-environment coupling.

## Prerequisite Modules

Module 02 (Agents) must be completed first. The question of what constitutes a perceiving agent — as opposed to a system that merely undergoes state changes correlated with its environment — depends on the analysis of agency developed in Module 02. Without a clear account of what it means for a system to be an agent, one cannot properly ask what it means for that agent to *perceive*.

## Cross-Unit Connections

- **Advanced Theory (Module 03)**: The theory treatment derives predictive coding update equations from the minimization of variational free energy, establishing the formal mechanics of perceptual inference. The philosophical treatment here interrogates the *interpretation* of those equations: whether prediction error minimization constitutes genuine perception (with intentional content and phenomenal character) or merely a mathematical process that we interpret perceptually.
- **Neuroscientific Frontiers (Module 03)**: The neuroscience treatment reviews cortical evidence for predictive coding — laminar specificity, mismatch negativity, precision modulation via neuromodulators. The philosophical module should ask what this evidence *establishes*: does neural evidence for prediction error signals confirm that the brain performs Bayesian inference, or merely that neural computation has a structure that can be *described* in Bayesian terms (the "Bayesian brain" as metaphor vs. mechanism)?
- **Research Methods (Module 03)**: The methods treatment covers neuroimaging paradigms for testing predictive coding. The philosophical module should clarify what experimental confirmation of predictive coding would and would not tell us about the phenomenology of perception — the gap between functional characterization and phenomenal characterization.

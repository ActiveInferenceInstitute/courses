## Content Guidance

This module must address the deepest philosophical tension within active inference: the relationship between inference and action. Active inference claims that action and perception are two sides of the same coin — both minimize variational free energy, perception by updating beliefs, action by changing the world to match predictions. But does this unification genuinely capture the enactive insight that cognition is constituted by sensorimotor engagement, or does it reduce action to a form of inference, thereby committing the intellectualist fallacy that enactivists have long criticized? Content must engage Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's founding enactivist statement, Bruineberg and Rietveld's affordance-based reading of the FEP, and Chemero's radical embodied cognitive science. The module must also examine how the expected free energy (EFE) decomposes into pragmatic and epistemic value and whether this decomposition maps onto meaningful phenomenological distinctions (coping vs. exploring, habitual vs. deliberate action, ready-to-hand vs. present-at-hand engagement).

## Key Concepts

- **Enactive approach (Varela, Thompson, Di Paolo)**: The view that cognition is not the manipulation of internal representations but the enactment of a world through sensorimotor coupling. The five core enactivist theses — autonomy, sense-making, emergence, embodiment, experience — and how active inference relates to each. Does active inference *formalize* enactivism or *betray* it by reintroducing representational intermediaries (generative models)?
- **Affordances as precision-weighted predictions**: Bruineberg and Rietveld's proposal that affordances — Gibson's opportunities for action specified by the environment — can be understood within the FEP as states of affairs that the organism is "geared up" to engage, with precision weighting determining which affordances are salient. Does this genuinely capture Gibson's anti-representationalism, or does it merely dress up a representational theory in ecological clothing?
- **Motor intentionality**: Merleau-Ponty's concept of a form of intentionality that is neither purely intellectual (forming a belief about how to move) nor purely mechanical (reflex arcs), but is the body's pre-reflective capacity to orient toward and engage with meaningful situations. The active inference account of motor control — action as prediction error minimization in proprioceptive channels — must be evaluated against this phenomenological concept.
- **The action-perception cycle**: The continuous, reciprocal relationship between acting and perceiving. In active inference, this cycle is formally captured by the alternation between belief updating (perception) and active state changes (action). But is the formal symmetry between perception and action a philosophical virtue (unifying two seemingly different capacities) or a deficiency (collapsing a genuine phenomenological asymmetry)?

## Key References

- **Varela, F., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991)**: *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*. The founding text of enactivism. Establishes the core claims: cognition as embodied action, the rejection of input-output models, the centrality of lived experience. Essential for evaluating whether active inference honors or subverts the enactivist program.
- **Bruineberg, J. & Rietveld, E. (2014)**: "Self-organization, free energy minimization, and optimal grip on a field of affordances." The most developed attempt to reconcile active inference with ecological-enactive approaches. Argues that free energy minimization is best understood as maintaining "optimal grip" (Merleau-Ponty) on a landscape of affordances, not as updating internal models of hidden causes.
- **Chemero, A. (2009)**: *Radical Embodied Cognitive Science*. Defends a non-representational, dynamical-systems approach to cognition. Provides the strongest challenge to active inference from within the embodied cognition tradition: if cognition is coordination dynamics, not inference, then active inference mischaracterizes the phenomenon it aims to explain.
- **Engel, A., Maye, A., Kurthen, M., & Konig, P. (2013)**: "Where's the action? The pragmatic turn in cognitive science." Argues for a fundamental reorientation of cognitive science around action and pragmatic engagement rather than passive representation. Provides the broader intellectual context for understanding why the emphasis on action in active inference matters philosophically.

## Prerequisite Modules

Module 03 (Perception) must be completed first. The analysis of perception as inference — and the debate between phenomenological, ecological, and Bayesian accounts — is directly presupposed. The action module extends this analysis by asking what happens when the organism does not merely update beliefs but *acts* on the world, and whether this changes the philosophical picture fundamentally.

## Cross-Unit Connections

- **Advanced Theory (Module 05)**: The theory treatment formalizes path integrals and KL control, deriving the EFE and optimal policy selection from first principles. The philosophical treatment here should interrogate whether the mathematical decomposition of EFE into pragmatic and epistemic components corresponds to genuine phenomenological categories, or whether it imposes a decision-theoretic framework onto phenomena (skilled coping, habitual action) that resist such regimentation.
- **Neuroscientific Frontiers (Module 05)**: The neuroscience treatment examines motor control — spinal reflexes as prediction error minimization, cerebellar forward models, active sensing. The philosophical module should ask whether the neural evidence supports the strong active inference claim (action *is* inference) or only the weaker claim (action *can be modeled as* inference), and what turns on this distinction.
- **Research Methods (Module 05)**: The methods treatment covers motor control and decision-making experiments — reaching paradigms, saccade tasks, force field adaptation. The philosophical module should address the limits of behavioral paradigms for adjudicating between genuinely different theoretical interpretations (enactivist vs. inferentialist) that may be empirically equivalent at the level of motor behavior.

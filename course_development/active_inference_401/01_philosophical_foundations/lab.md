# Lab: Philosophical Foundations of Active Inference

## Objective

Conduct a rigorous philosophical analysis of the Free Energy Principle (FEP) by reconstructing its epistemological commitments, evaluating its ontological implications, and situating it within the landscape of contemporary philosophy of mind and philosophy of science.

## Prerequisites

- Graduate-level familiarity with Western epistemology (Descartes, Kant, Quine, Bayesian epistemology)
- Understanding of phenomenology (Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty)
- Working knowledge of philosophy of mind (consciousness, intentionality, the hard problem)
- Competence in formal logic and probability theory sufficient to engage with the FEP's mathematical claims

## Part 1: Epistemological Reconstruction (Analytical)

1. **Formal Argument Mapping**: Reconstruct the FEP as a series of formal arguments. For each step, identify:
   - The premises (mathematical, empirical, or philosophical)
   - The inferential structure (deductive, inductive, abductive)
   - The ontological commitments (what must exist for the argument to hold)
2. **Epistemological Classification**: Classify the FEP along standard philosophical dimensions:
   - Internalism vs. externalism about justification
   - Foundationalism vs. coherentism about epistemic structure
   - Realism vs. anti-realism about the objects of inference
3. **Circularity Analysis**: Formalize the self-evidencing circularity (the model defines what counts as evidence, evidence updates the model). Compare this to Quine's naturalized epistemology and the hermeneutic circle. Argue whether this circularity is vicious or virtuous.

## Part 2: Phenomenological Comparison (Synthetic)

1. Select **one** phenomenological concept (e.g., Heidegger's *Zuhandenheit*, Merleau-Ponty's motor intentionality, Husserl's passive synthesis).
2. Reconstruct the concept in its original philosophical context (minimum 500 words, with textual citations).
3. Map the concept onto the Active Inference formalism: identify the corresponding mathematical structures (generative model, prediction error, precision, expected free energy).
4. Evaluate whether the mapping is *substantive* (reveals genuine structural isomorphism) or *merely metaphorical* (surface-level analogy).

## Part 3: Critical Evaluation (Dialectical)

1. Identify the **strongest objection** to the FEP as a philosophical framework. Candidates include:
   - The triviality objection (the FEP is unfalsifiable / explains everything)
   - The reification objection (Markov blankets are mathematical abstractions mistaken for ontological boundaries)
   - The explanatory gap (the FEP does not address phenomenal consciousness)
2. Develop the objection in its strongest form (principle of charity).
3. Construct the best available response from within the Active Inference framework.
4. Adjudicate: does the response succeed? What residual challenges remain?

## Deliverables

- A formal philosophical essay (3000--5000 words) addressing Parts 1--3
- An appendix with the formal argument map from Part 1
- A bibliography of at least 15 primary philosophical sources

## Discussion Requirements

- Present your analysis in a seminar format (20-minute presentation + 10-minute Q&A)
- Be prepared to defend your epistemological classification and phenomenological mapping under critical questioning
- Identify at least one open philosophical question that your analysis raises for future research

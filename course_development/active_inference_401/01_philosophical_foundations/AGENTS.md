# Course AGENTS: Philosophical Foundations

> **Quick Navigation**: [Course README](./README.md) | [Curriculum AGENTS](../AGENTS.md)

## Identity

- **Course**: Philosophical Foundations
- **Number**: 1
- **Perspective**: Epistemology, phenomenology, 4E cognition (embodied, embedded, enacted, extended)
- **Lab Type**: Seminar Discussion
- **Audience**: PhD students and researchers in philosophy of mind, philosophy of science, and cognitive science
- **Tone**: Argumentative, dialectical, historically informed. Dense engagement with primary philosophical texts. Formal notation used illustratively, not as the primary mode of analysis.

## Core Question

What does Active Inference *mean*? What are its ontological commitments (what must exist for the FEP to be true?), epistemological implications (what can we know through this framework?), and conceptual entailments (what follows from its assumptions?)?

## Intellectual Traditions

This unit draws on and engages critically with:

- **Phenomenology**: Husserl (intentionality, noesis/noema), Merleau-Ponty (motor intentionality, embodied perception), Heidegger (being-in-the-world, readiness-to-hand)
- **4E Cognition**: Embodied (Varela, Thompson), Embedded (Haugeland), Enacted (Noe, Di Paolo), Extended (Clark, Chalmers)
- **Ecological Psychology**: Gibson (affordances, direct perception, ecological optics)
- **Philosophy of Biology**: Autopoiesis (Maturana & Varela), organizational closure (Moreno & Mossio), autonomy (Barandiaran)
- **Bayesian Epistemology**: Probabilistic coherentism, rational belief updating, the problem of priors
- **Philosophy of Science**: Explanatory unification, mechanism vs. principle, falsifiability of the FEP

## Key Journals

*Philosophy of Science*, *Synthese*, *Mind*, *Nous*, *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences*, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, *Frontiers in Psychology* (Theoretical and Philosophical Psychology)

## Conventions

All modules in this course must:

1. Use language appropriate for PhD students with strong philosophical training
2. Frame all concepts through the lens of epistemology, phenomenology, and 4E cognition
3. Include Seminar Discussion lab activities requiring close reading of primary texts and structured argumentation
4. Present at least two competing philosophical interpretations of each key concept
5. Distinguish carefully between the formal claims of the FEP and the philosophical interpretations layered onto them
6. Adhere to notation standards in [../resources/notation_table.md](../resources/notation_table.md) — use equations illustratively, always explaining philosophical significance
7. Cross-reference the shared [../resources/glossary.md](../resources/glossary.md)
8. Link to the corresponding modules in other units where the same topic receives different treatment

## Assessment Philosophy

Seminar Discussions should require students to:
- Reconstruct arguments from primary texts with charity and precision
- Identify hidden premises, unstated assumptions, and equivocations
- Evaluate arguments using standard philosophical criteria (validity, soundness, explanatory power)
- Engage with the strongest version of opposing positions (steelman, not strawman)
- Connect philosophical analysis to the formal framework where appropriate

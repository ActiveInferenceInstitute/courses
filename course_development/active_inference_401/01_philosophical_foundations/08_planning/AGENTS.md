## Content Guidance

This module must address the philosophical implications of the FEP's treatment of planning as deep temporal inference — the evaluation of policies over extended time horizons through imagination, counterfactual reasoning, and sophisticated inference (recursive expected free energy evaluation). Content must engage with the phenomenology of temporal consciousness (Husserl's time-consciousness, the specious present, protention and retention), the philosophy of imagination (is imagining a future the same cognitive operation as perceiving the present, merely "offline"?), and the narrative self (Gallagher, Ricoeur) — the idea that planning is possible only for beings who organize their experience temporally through self-narrative. The module must also confront the limits of the FEP's planning framework: can inference within a fixed generative model account for genuinely creative, open-ended planning, or does it reduce imagination to search within a pre-specified possibility space? This connects to the broader question of whether the FEP can accommodate genuine novelty or only optimization within known structures.

## Key Concepts

- **Imagination as offline policy evaluation**: The FEP treats imagination as the evaluation of policies under a generative model without executing them — "offline" active inference. Pezzulo (2008) and others have argued this captures the functional role of imagination in motor and cognitive planning. The philosophical question is whether this functional characterization is sufficient: does reducing imagination to "simulation" miss its phenomenological character (the sense of possibility, the experience of "what if")?
- **Counterfactual reasoning and possible worlds**: Planning requires reasoning about states of affairs that do not (yet) obtain. In FEP terms, this is formalized as evaluating expected free energy under counterfactual policies. Does this formalism capture genuine counterfactual reasoning (Lewis's possible worlds, Stalnaker's conditional logic), or does it merely model a process that mimics counterfactual reasoning without the metaphysical commitments?
- **Temporal consciousness (Husserl, phenomenology of time)**: Husserl's analysis of internal time-consciousness — the tripartite structure of retention (just-past), primal impression (now), and protention (about-to-come) — provides a phenomenological framework for understanding the temporal depth of experience. Does the FEP's hierarchical temporal model (deep temporal models with policies evaluated over progressively longer horizons) correspond to Husserlian time-consciousness, or does it describe temporal processing without capturing temporal *experience*?
- **Mental time travel and the narrative self**: Suddendorf and Corballis's concept of mental time travel — the capacity to project oneself into past and future episodes — and Gallagher's (2000) analysis of the narrative self. Planning in a deep sense requires not just predicting future states but *imagining oneself* in those states, which presupposes a sense of self that persists through time. Can the FEP's generative model framework account for the self as a model or narrative, or does selfhood require something beyond what any model can provide?
- **Creativity and the limits of inference**: Can the FEP account for genuinely open-ended creativity — the generation of ideas, plans, and possibilities that were not contained in the prior model space? If imagination is evaluation within a generative model, and the model defines the space of possibilities, then creativity seems to require something the FEP cannot provide: transcendence of the model. This connects to Dreyfus's critique of AI as unable to capture the background understanding that makes creative coping possible.

## Key References

- **Pezzulo, G. (2008)**: "Coordinating with the future: The anticipatory nature of representation." Argues that representation is fundamentally anticipatory — geared toward future action — and that imagination, planning, and prediction are the core functions of cognitive systems. Provides the key bridge between pragmatist philosophy of mind and the FEP's treatment of planning as policy evaluation.
- **Buckner, R. (2010)**: "The role of the hippocampus in prediction and imagination." Reviews evidence that hippocampal mechanisms for memory retrieval are re-deployed for future simulation. While primarily a neuroscience paper, it has philosophical implications for the question of whether memory and imagination share a common inferential architecture.
- **Friston, K., Da Costa, L., Sajid, N., Heins, C., Ueltzhoeffer, K., Pavliotis, G., & Parr, T. (2021)**: "The world model — or how we come to constitute the reality we experience." Develops the concept of deep temporal models and sophisticated inference. Essential for understanding the formal framework that this module must philosophically interrogate.
- **Seth, A. (2021)**: *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness*. Develops the "beast machine" theory of consciousness within the predictive processing framework, arguing that consciousness arises from predictive models of the body and its relationship to the world. Relevant for the question of whether sophisticated planning requires phenomenal consciousness, and whether the FEP can account for consciousness or must presuppose it.
- **Gallagher, S. (2000)**: "Philosophical conceptions of the self: Implications for cognitive science." Distinguishes the minimal self (pre-reflective sense of agency and ownership) from the narrative self (the temporally extended self constituted by autobiographical memory and self-narrative). Essential for evaluating whether the FEP's planning framework presupposes a notion of selfhood that it does not — and perhaps cannot — provide.

## Prerequisite Modules

Modules 04-06 (Cognition, Action, Learning) must be completed first. Planning draws on all three: it requires the cognitive machinery of approximate inference (Module 04), the capacity for action and the action-perception cycle (Module 05), and the ability to revise models over time (Module 06). The philosophical analysis of planning integrates the questions raised in these prior modules — where cognition ends, whether action is inference, how models change — and extends them into the temporal dimension. Without this prior conceptual work, the specific questions about imagination, counterfactual reasoning, and the narrative self cannot be properly situated.

## Cross-Unit Connections

- **Advanced Theory (Module 08)**: The theory treatment covers deep temporal models and sophisticated inference — the recursive evaluation of expected free energy over policy trees. The philosophical treatment here should interrogate whether the mathematical framework of sophisticated inference (which involves evaluating the expected free energy of policies that include future inference steps) captures genuine deliberation or merely formalizes a process that lacks the phenomenological richness of deliberative experience.
- **Neuroscientific Frontiers (Module 08)**: The neuroscience treatment examines hippocampal replay, preplay, and the neural basis of model-based planning. The philosophical module should ask whether hippocampal replay constitutes imagination (phenomenally conscious future simulation) or merely a neural mechanism that supports planning without being imaginative in the phenomenological sense. The gap between neural mechanism and phenomenal experience is particularly pressing here.
- **Research Methods (Module 08)**: The methods treatment covers open problems and research frontiers — scale-free inference, consciousness and the FEP, embodied AI, computational psychiatry. The philosophical module should connect these open problems to the fundamental philosophical questions raised throughout the unit: the limits of formalization, the relationship between mechanism and meaning, the prospect of a genuinely unified theory of mind, and whether the FEP is best understood as a scientific theory, a mathematical framework, or a philosophical research program.

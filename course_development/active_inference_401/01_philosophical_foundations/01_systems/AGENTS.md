## Content Guidance

This module must engage with the ontological question of what a "system" is within the Free Energy Principle and how this concept relates to the autopoietic tradition in philosophy of biology. Content must critically examine whether the FEP's formal notion of a system — anything persisting at non-equilibrium steady state (NESS) with a Markov blanket — constitutes a genuine ontological category or merely a convenient mathematical abstraction. The module must trace the intellectual lineage from Maturana and Varela's autopoiesis through Di Paolo's adaptivity criterion to the FEP's statistical formulation, identifying where these traditions converge and where they diverge in their commitments about what counts as a system and what it means for a system to maintain itself.

## Key Concepts

- **Autopoiesis (Maturana & Varela)**: The self-producing character of living systems — a network of processes that continuously regenerates the very network that produces it. The module must address whether autopoiesis provides necessary and sufficient conditions for life, and whether the FEP's Markov blanket formalism captures or misses the self-producing character of autopoietic organization.
- **Organizational closure**: The circular topology of constraints in which each constraint in the system is both dependent on and enabling of other constraints (Moreno & Mossio, 2015). Distinguishable from thermodynamic openness: a system can be organizationally closed while materially and energetically open.
- **Autonomy and the boundary problem**: How system boundaries are individuated. Is the Markov blanket a discovered boundary (ontologically prior to the observer) or an imposed partition (dependent on modeling choices)? Barandiaran's conditions for autonomy — self-individuation, asymmetric interaction, normativity — provide a richer framework than blanket possession alone.
- **Process ontology**: The view that systems are not fundamentally things but ongoing processes — temporally extended patterns of activity. Whitehead, Simondon, and contemporary process philosophy (Dupre, Nicholson) challenge substance-based readings of the Markov blanket.
- **The boundary problem**: The question of where one system ends and another begins. Does the Markov blanket formalism resolve this problem or merely restate it in statistical language?

## Key References

- **Thompson, E. (2007)**: *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind*. The definitive synthesis of autopoiesis, phenomenology, and enactivism. Central reference for the claim that the mind-life continuity thesis grounds cognitive science in biology.
- **Di Paolo, E. (2005)**: "Autopoiesis, adaptivity, and teleology." Argues that autopoiesis alone is insufficient for cognition — adaptivity (regulation of states relative to viability boundaries) is additionally required. Critical for evaluating what the FEP adds to the autopoietic framework.
- **Kauffman, S. (1993)**: *The Origins of Order*. Self-organization and selection in evolution. Provides the complexity-theoretic background for understanding why self-organization at the edge of chaos is a precondition for the kinds of systems the FEP describes.
- **Froese, T. & Ziemke, T. (2009)**: "Enactive artificial intelligence." Raises the question of whether the formal characterization of system boundaries (including Markov blankets) is sufficient for understanding the autonomy and sense-making of living systems, or whether something is lost in the formalization.

## Prerequisite Modules

None. This is the entry point for the Philosophical Foundations unit. Students are expected to arrive with graduate-level familiarity with philosophy of biology, philosophy of science, and the basic formalism of the FEP (Markov blankets, NESS) as specified in the course prerequisites.

## Cross-Unit Connections

- **Advanced Theory (Module 01)**: The theory treatment derives the NESS formalism from Langevin dynamics and the Fokker-Planck equation, establishing the mathematical conditions under which a system possesses a steady-state density. The philosophical treatment here should motivate *why* we care about these conditions — what ontological picture is at stake — and identify assumptions that are mathematically necessary but philosophically contentious (e.g., ergodicity, the existence of a well-defined partition).
- **Neuroscientific Frontiers (Module 01)**: The neuroscience treatment examines neural systems as concrete instances of NESS — cortical dynamics, criticality, and oscillatory attractors. The philosophical module should provide the conceptual framework for asking whether neural systems are *paradigmatic* examples of self-organizing systems or whether the concept applies more broadly (and what is at stake in that extension).
- **Research Methods (Module 01)**: The methods treatment addresses how to identify self-organizing systems empirically — fitting Langevin models, testing NESS conditions. The philosophical module should clarify what empirical confirmation of NESS conditions would and would not establish about the ontological status of the system in question.

Lab: Motor Control and Active Inference

Learning Goal: Analyze motor systems through the Active Inference framework, mapping neural circuits to predictions, errors, and policy selection.

Part 1: Predictions Not Commands

Exercise: Compare the classical and Active Inference accounts of reaching for a coffee cup:

Step Classical Motor Control Account Active Inference Account
1 Motor cortex computes desired trajectory Motor cortex generates proprioceptive prediction ("arm will be at cup location")
2 Motor cortex sends commands to spinal cord Prediction descends to spinal cord as expected proprioceptive state
3 Spinal cord activates muscles Spinal reflex arcs detect prediction error between predicted and actual arm position
4 Muscles contract in correct sequence Reflex arcs contract muscles to fulfill proprioceptive prediction
5 Sensory feedback confirms movement Prediction error decreases as arm approaches predicted position

Now apply the Active Inference account to: (a) catching a thrown ball, (b) speaking a sentence, (c) playing a piano scale. For each, identify the prediction, the prediction errors, and the reflex mechanisms that fulfill the prediction.

Part 2: Cerebellar Forward Model Analysis

Learning Goal: Understand how the cerebellum predicts motor consequences and updates through error.

Exercise: Consider a novice learning to throw darts:

  2. What the cerebellum predicts (expected landing position)
  3. What actually happens (actual landing position)

  7. What happens if the dart's weight suddenly changes? Describe the re-learning process in terms of prediction errors and model updating.

Part 3: Basal Ganglia Policy Selection Simulation

Learning Goal: Map the direct/indirect/hyperdirect pathways to Active Inference policy selection.

Exercise: Consider choosing between two motor policies: (A) reach left for water, (B) reach right for coffee.

Scenario 1: You're thirsty (strong preference for water)

Pathway Activity Computational Effect
Direct (D1) to Policy A HIGH "Go" for water-reaching policy — selected policy gets high precision
Indirect (D2) to Policy B HIGH "No-go" for coffee-reaching — competing policy suppressed
Hyperdirect LOW No need to stop — no surprising events to trigger re-evaluation

Scenario 2: You hear a loud crash while reaching

Pathway Activity Computational Effect
Direct SUPPRESSED All ongoing policies halted
Indirect SUPPRESSED All ongoing policies halted
Hyperdirect HIGH Global stop signal — re-evaluate all policies because surprising event

Part 4: Sensory Attenuation Experiments

Learning Goal: Understand self-generated sensation attenuation as evidence for forward models.

Exercise 1 — The Tickling Thought Experiment:

You touch your own arm with your right hand. Then a friend touches the same spot with the same pressure.

Condition Forward Model Prediction Actual Sensation Prediction Error Perceived Intensity
Self-touch "Touch will occur at X, pressure Y" Touch at X, pressure Y Very small Low (attenuated)
Other-touch No prediction (unpredicted) Touch at X, pressure Y Large High (full intensity)

Part 5: Saccadic Eye Movements as Active Inference

Learning Goal: Analyze eye movements as proprioceptive predictions.

Exercise: Look at a natural scene photograph for 30 seconds. Then answer:

  1. Where did your eyes go first? (Probably areas of high contrast, faces, text — areas of high expected free energy / information gain)
  2. How long did fixations last? (Longer fixations = more information to extract = more prediction errors to resolve)
  3. Did you revisit any locations? (Revisiting = renewed uncertainty about that region)
  4. Where did you NOT look? (Low expected information gain — predictable regions like blank walls)

Now connect your observations to Active Inference: saccades are epistemic actions that direct the fovea toward regions of maximum expected free energy reduction.

Part 6: Free Will and the Readiness Potential

Learning Goal: Critically analyze the implications of the RP for Active Inference and volition.

Exercise: Read the following simplified description of Libet's experiment (1983):

"Participants were asked to flex their wrist whenever they wanted. EEG recorded a slow negative potential (the readiness potential, RP) beginning ~550ms before the movement. But participants reported conscious awareness of their 'intention to move' only ~200ms before the movement — 350ms AFTER the RP began."

  1. What does the classical interpretation conclude about free will?
  2. How does Active Inference reinterpret the RP? (Hint: the RP is gradual precision accumulation, not a "decision")
  3. Does conscious awareness of intention need to precede the RP for free will to exist? Why or why not?
  4. Write a 200-word argument for why the Libet experiment does NOT refute free will under Active Inference.

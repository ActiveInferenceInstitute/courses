## Introduction

This module explores **Agents**. In the **401** curriculum, we approach this topic with a focus on specific applications and theoretical depth appropriate for the audience. Agents is a critical component of the 8-part Active Inference spine, bridging the gap between Philosophical Foundations and Advanced Theory.

The neuroscientific frontiers unit pushes beyond textbook neuroscience into the active research edge of how Active Inference accounts for neural computation. At the PhD level, students are expected to engage with primary literature, evaluate competing neural implementations of the free energy principle, and identify open questions amenable to empirical investigation. The central question of this unit is: How does the brain, as a physical organ, instantiate the abstract computational principles of Active Inference? This requires grappling with neural process theories -- specific proposals about which circuits, neurotransmitters, and synaptic mechanisms realize variational inference in the brain.

The eight modules span the full spine. The systems module examines large-scale brain network organization through the lens of Markov blankets and functional connectivity. The agents module scrutinizes what makes a neural system an agent, drawing on interoceptive inference and the embodied brain. Perception covers predictive coding implementations at laminar and columnar resolution. Cognition addresses prefrontal and hippocampal mechanisms for hypothesis testing and model comparison. Action links motor control to active inference through the reflex arc reconceptualized as prediction fulfillment. Learning engages with synaptic plasticity rules that approximate Bayesian updating. Communication covers neural oscillations and message passing between cortical areas. Planning addresses temporal depth through hippocampal replay, prospection, and model-based reinforcement learning.

## Applications

In 401, we see Agents manifest in:
*   **Specific Example 1**: The neural process theory of predictive coding as implemented in canonical microcircuits (Bastos et al., 2012, "Canonical microcircuits for predictive coding"). This theory proposes that superficial pyramidal neurons encode prediction errors that propagate feedforward, while deep pyramidal neurons encode predictions that propagate feedback. The laminar specificity of these signals (supragranular vs. infragranular layers) maps onto the mathematical structure of hierarchical variational inference, with distinct roles for gamma-band (feedforward prediction error) and alpha/beta-band (feedback prediction) oscillations. PhD students should evaluate the empirical evidence for this scheme from laminar electrophysiology (e.g., Kerkoerle et al., 2014) and consider alternative architectures such as the Rao-Ballard model and dendritic predictive coding (Sacramento et al., 2018), assessing which aspects of active inference each implementation captures or fails to capture.
*   **Specific Example 2**: Interoceptive inference and the embodied agent, drawing on Seth and Friston's (2016) framework of interoceptive predictive processing. In this view, the agent is not merely a brain in a vat but an embodied system whose selfhood is constituted by predictions about its own physiological states -- heartbeat, respiration, gut signals. The anterior insular cortex serves as a key hub for interoceptive prediction errors, and allostatic regulation (maintaining physiological variables within viable bounds) is recast as active inference over interoceptive generative models. This perspective has clinical implications for understanding conditions such as anxiety, depression, and depersonalization as disorders of interoceptive inference (Paulus et al., 2019). Students should design experiments that could test specific predictions of the interoceptive active inference framework, such as the hypothesis that interoceptive precision modulates the intensity of emotional experience.

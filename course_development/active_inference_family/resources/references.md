# References: Active Inference for Families

> Organized by topic. These works form the evidence base for this curriculum. Parenthetical notes explain what each reference contributes.

---

## Active Inference & Free Energy Principle

1. Parr, T., Pezzulo, G., & Friston, K. J. (2022). *Active Inference: The Free Energy Principle in Mind, Brain, and Behavior*. MIT Press. (The definitive textbook — our primary source for the theoretical framework.)

2. Friston, K. (2010). "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?" *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 11(2), 127–138. (The landmark paper that introduced the FEP to the broader neuroscience community.)

3. Friston, K. (2013). "Life as we know it." *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*, 10(86). (Extends the FEP to all living systems — key for understanding why even bacteria "do" Active Inference.)

4. Constant, A., Clark, A., Kirchhoff, M., & Friston, K. (2019). "Extended active inference: constructing predictive cognition beyond skulls." *Mind & Language*, 34(2), 145–169. (Explains how cognition extends beyond the individual brain — foundational for understanding co-regulation.)

---

## Infant & Child Development

1. Gopnik, A. (2009). *The Philosophical Baby: What Children's Minds Tell Us About Truth, Love, and the Meaning of Life*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux. (Brilliant, accessible exploration of how babies think and learn — closely aligned with Active Inference ideas.)

2. Gopnik, A., Meltzoff, A. N., & Kuhl, P. K. (1999). *The Scientist in the Crib: Minds, Brains, and How Children Learn*. William Morrow. (The classic case for babies as natural scientists — perfect bridge to Active Inference.)

3. Piaget, J. (1952). *The Origins of Intelligence in Children*. International Universities Press. (Foundational work on cognitive development — object permanence, sensorimotor stages, and schema building.)

4. Vygotsky, L. S. (1978). *Mind in Society: The Development of Higher Psychological Processes*. Harvard University Press. (Zone of proximal development and scaffolding — parallels Active Inference's hierarchical generative models.)

5. Spelke, E. S. (2000). "Core knowledge." *American Psychologist*, 55(11), 1233–1243. (Evidence that infants are born with core expectations about objects, agents, number, and space — essentially innate priors.)

---

## Attachment & Co-Regulation

1. Bowlby, J. (1969). *Attachment and Loss, Vol. 1: Attachment*. Basic Books. (The foundation of attachment theory — maps beautifully onto Active Inference's picture of the caregiver as a reliable prediction source.)

2. Ainsworth, M. D. S., Blehar, M. C., Waters, E., & Wall, S. (1978). *Patterns of Attachment: A Psychological Study of the Strange Situation*. Lawrence Erlbaum. (The empirical classification of attachment styles — secure, anxious, avoidant.)

3. Porges, S. W. (2011). *The Polyvagal Theory: Neurophysiological Foundations of Emotions, Attachment, Communication, and Self-Regulation*. W. W. Norton. (Explains the vagal nerve's role in co-regulation — the body's prediction-error-reduction system for social safety.)

4. Siegel, D. J., & Bryson, T. P. (2011). *The Whole-Brain Child: 12 Revolutionary Strategies to Nurture Your Child's Developing Mind*. Delacorte Press. (Practical parenting strategies grounded in neuroscience — bridges clinical work and Active Inference nicely.)

---

## Play & Learning

1. Hirsh-Pasek, K., & Golinkoff, R. M. (2003). *Einstein Never Used Flash Cards: How Our Children Really Learn — and Why They Need to Play More and Memorize Less*. Rodale Books. (Evidence-based argument for play as the primary mode of learning.)

2. Pellegrini, A. D. (2009). *The Role of Play in Human Development*. Oxford University Press. (Comprehensive review of play across development — connects to Active Inference's epistemic exploration.)

3. Montessori, M. (1967). *The Absorbent Mind*. Holt, Rinehart and Winston. (Montessori's insight that children absorb the world through structured sensory exploration — a precursor to Active Inference thinking.)

---

## Technology & Childhood

1. American Academy of Pediatrics. (2016). "Media and young minds." *Pediatrics*, 138(5), e20162591. (Current screen time guidelines for children under 6.)

2. Radesky, J. S., & Christakis, D. A. (2016). "Increased screen time: implications for early childhood development and behavior." *Pediatric Clinics*, 63(5), 827–839. (Evidence review on screen time effects — used in Course 4.)

3. Zosh, J. M., et al. (2017). "Learning through play: a review of the evidence." *LEGO Foundation White Paper*. (Comprehensive review connecting play science to learning outcomes.)

# Glossary: Active Inference for Families

> Key terms explained in everyday parenting language. When you see these terms in a module, you can come back here for a quick refresher. Terms marked with 🧠 come from neuroscience; those marked with 👶 are parenting translations.

---

### Active Inference 🧠

The idea that all living things — including your baby — constantly make predictions about what will happen next, and then act to make those predictions come true. When predictions fail, the organism updates its model of the world.

👶 **Parenting translation**: Your baby expects your face when they hear your voice. If a stranger appears instead, they cry — that's prediction error in action. Over time, they update their model to include new faces, new places, new routines.

### Agent 🧠

Anything that acts on the world based on its own internal model. In Active Inference, even a simple creature like a bacterium is an "agent" because it moves toward food and away from danger.

👶 **Parenting translation**: Your baby is an agent from day one. When they root for the breast, reach for a toy, or turn toward your voice, they are acting based on predictions about what will feel good.

### Attachment 👶

The deep bond between a child and their primary caregiver. In Active Inference terms, a securely attached child has a reliable generative model that includes "my caregiver will respond to my needs." This reduces their baseline prediction error and frees up mental energy for exploration.

### Babbling 👶

The stream of sounds (ba-ba, da-da, ga-ga) that babies produce starting around 6 months. In Active Inference terms, babbling is the baby's active inference about speech — they are generating vocal predictions and listening to see if the output matches what they hear from adults.

### Co-regulation 👶

When a caregiver helps a child manage their emotions by staying calm and present. In Active Inference terms, the caregiver's nervous system is providing a stable set of predictions (calm voice, steady breathing, warm touch) that the child's brain can borrow to reduce its own prediction error.

### Exploration 🧠

Deliberately seeking out new or unexpected situations to learn more about the world. In Active Inference, this is called "epistemic action" — acting not to get a reward, but to reduce uncertainty.

👶 **Parenting translation**: When your toddler empties every drawer in the kitchen, they are exploring. They are gathering data about the world and updating their model. It's inconvenient, but it's exactly what their brain needs.

### Free Energy 🧠

A mathematical quantity that roughly captures how "surprised" an organism is by its current situation. Living things try to minimize free energy — meaning they try to stay in unsurprising, predictable states or update their models to accommodate the surprise.

👶 **Parenting translation**: Think of free energy as your child's discomfort level. When everything matches their expectations (familiar room, familiar face, familiar routine), free energy is low and they are calm. When something unexpected happens, free energy spikes, and they become distressed.

### Generative Model 🧠

An internal "map" or "story" that an organism uses to predict what will happen next. Your brain has one. Your baby's brain is building one from scratch.

👶 **Parenting translation**: Your baby's generative model starts simple: "When I cry, someone comes. When I'm fed, the discomfort goes away." Over months and years, it becomes incredibly rich: "Mom makes pancakes on Saturdays. The park has a slide. Dogs are soft but sometimes loud."

### Habituation 🧠

The process of getting used to something. When a stimulus is repeated many times, the brain stops reacting — it has been "predicted away."

👶 **Parenting translation**: The first time your baby hears a car horn, they startle. By the hundredth time, they don't even flinch. Their brain has updated its model: "car horns happen, and they're not dangerous."

### Joint Attention 👶

When a parent and child look at the same thing together, and both know that the other is looking. This is one of the most important cognitive milestones in early development.

👶 **In Active Inference terms**: Joint attention means two agents are aligning their generative models toward the same object. The child is learning that other minds have focus, and that shared focus creates shared understanding. This is the seed of all human communication.

### Markov Blanket 🧠

The "boundary" that separates the inside of a system from the outside. For a cell, it's the membrane. For a person, it's the skin plus the senses.

👶 **Parenting translation**: Your baby's skin, eyes, ears, and touch receptors form their Markov blanket — it's where the outside world meets the inside of their body and brain. Everything they learn comes through this boundary.

### Object Permanence 👶

The understanding that objects continue to exist even when you can't see them. Babies develop this around 8–12 months.

👶 **In Active Inference terms**: Object permanence means the baby's generative model has become complex enough to maintain a prediction about an object ("the ball is behind the blanket") even without sensory confirmation. This is a huge cognitive achievement.

### Prediction Error 🧠

The difference between what an organism predicted and what actually happened. Large prediction errors are uncomfortable and trigger either model updating (learning) or action (trying to make reality match the prediction).

👶 **Parenting translation**: When the pacifier falls out, the baby predicted "pacifier in mouth" but experienced "no pacifier." The prediction error is real and urgent. They cry — which is an action designed to make reality match the prediction (someone will put it back).

### Precision 🧠

How much confidence or weight an organism places on a particular prediction. High precision means "I'm very sure about this." Low precision means "I'm not sure; I should pay more attention to incoming data."

👶 **Parenting translation**: A newborn has very little precision — almost everything is surprising because they have few reliable predictions. As they grow and their models get better, precision increases for familiar things and stays low for novel things.

### Scaffolding 👶

When a caregiver provides just enough support for a child to accomplish something they couldn't do alone — then gradually removes the support as the child becomes competent.

👶 **In Active Inference terms**: Scaffolding is the caregiver temporarily becoming part of the child's Markov blanket, providing predictions and actions that the child can't yet generate alone. As the child's model improves, the scaffolding dissolves naturally.

### Surprise 🧠

In Active Inference, "surprise" has a technical meaning: it's the negative log probability of an observation given your model. In everyday terms, it's when something happens that you didn't expect.

👶 **Parenting translation**: When your toddler's tower of blocks falls down, that's surprise. The reaction (delight or frustration) depends on what they predicted. If they were testing "will it fall?", it's delightful. If they predicted "it should stand up," it's distressing.

### Turn-Taking 👶

The back-and-forth pattern that begins in early infancy — parent talks, baby coos, parent talks again. This is the foundation of all conversational language.

👶 **In Active Inference terms**: Turn-taking is two agents synchronizing their generative models through a shared temporal rhythm. Each turn is a prediction ("now it's your turn to respond") and each response confirms or updates that prediction.

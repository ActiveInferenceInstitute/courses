## Introduction

This module explores **Perception**. In the **Family** curriculum, we approach this topic with a focus on specific applications and theoretical depth appropriate for the audience. Perception is a critical component of the 8-part Active Inference spine, bridging the gap between Tiny Bodies, Big Brains and Screens, Toys, and Tools.

Patterns in Play is about the magic of play -- and the science hiding inside it. In the previous units, we discovered that families are systems and that even the youngest family members are agents. Now we explore how play is the child's primary way of perceiving and understanding the world. When children play, they are running little experiments: stacking blocks to see how tall a tower can get before it falls, mixing colors to see what new color appears, or playing peekaboo to test whether things still exist when you cannot see them. Every game is a pattern, and every pattern is a prediction waiting to be tested.

The eight lessons in this unit use play activities to illustrate the full Active Inference spine. We look at playrooms and playgrounds as systems with their own rules and boundaries. We see children at play as agents pursuing goals (build the tower! catch the ball!). We explore perception through games that sharpen the senses -- listening games, texture boxes, smell jars. We examine cognition through puzzles, sorting games, and pretend play where children try on different roles and models of the world. Action comes through in all the physical play -- running, jumping, climbing, drawing, building. Learning happens every time a child tries something new and adjusts their approach. Communication is practiced through cooperative games, storytelling, and imaginative play with others. And planning appears in games with rules, turn-taking, and building projects that require thinking ahead.

## Applications

In Family, we see Perception manifest in:
*   **Specific Example 1**: Play peekaboo with a baby and watch Active Inference happen in real time. When you hide your face behind your hands, the baby's brain makes a prediction: "the face is gone." For very young infants (under about 4 months), that prediction feels final -- out of sight, out of mind. But as the baby's generative model develops, they start to predict that the face will come back. Now the surprise happens when you reveal your face: the baby predicted it and their prediction was confirmed, which feels delightful. If you change the game -- popping up from a different side, or having someone else appear instead -- you generate a prediction error, and the baby's startled or confused reaction shows you their model being updated. The giggles and wide eyes of peekaboo are the outward signs of a tiny brain actively adjusting its model of a world where faces appear and disappear. Parents can vary the timing, the hiding spot, and who appears to give the baby rich perceptual learning experiences, all through simple play.
*   **Specific Example 2**: Set up a "mystery bag" game at home. Put several familiar objects in an opaque bag -- a wooden block, a spoon, a ball, a sock. Ask your child to reach in without looking and guess what they are touching. The child's brain immediately starts generating predictions based on texture, shape, size, and temperature: "It is smooth and round -- a ball? No wait, it has a handle -- a spoon!" Each touch sends information that either confirms or surprises their prediction. When the child pulls the object out and sees what it actually is, the final reveal closes the perception loop. If they guessed right, their model is confirmed. If they guessed wrong, they update their model for next time ("spoons feel like this, not like that"). This game is pure perception as Active Inference: predicting, sensing, comparing, and updating. It also builds vocabulary, fine motor skills, and the confidence that comes from learning to trust your own senses.

# Unit 3: Patterns in Play — The Science of Games

## Learning Objectives

1. Understand that **play** is a child's primary way of running experiments and testing predictions.
2. See that every game has a **pattern**, and every pattern creates **expectations** that can be confirmed or surprised.
3. Discover specific play activities that sharpen perception, build thinking skills, and support learning.

## Introduction: Play Is Learning

Patterns in Play is about the magic of play — and the science hiding inside it. In the previous units, we discovered that families are systems and that even the youngest family members are agents. Now we explore how play is the child's primary way of perceiving and understanding the world. When children play, they are running little experiments: stacking blocks to see how tall a tower can get before it falls, mixing colors to see what new color appears, or playing peekaboo to test whether things still exist when you cannot see them. Every game is a pattern, and every pattern is a prediction waiting to be tested.

The eight lessons in this unit use play activities to illustrate the full Active Inference spine. We look at playrooms and playgrounds as systems with their own rules and boundaries. We see children at play as agents pursuing goals (build the tower! catch the ball!). We explore perception through games that sharpen the senses — listening games, texture boxes, smell jars. We examine cognition through puzzles, sorting games, and pretend play where children try on different roles and models of the world. Action comes through in all the physical play — running, jumping, climbing, drawing, building. Learning happens every time a child tries something new and adjusts their approach. Communication is practiced through cooperative games, storytelling, and imaginative play with others. And planning appears in games with rules, turn-taking, and building projects that require thinking ahead.

## Key Concepts

### 1. Play = Prediction Testing

Every act of play is a tiny experiment. A child stacking blocks is predicting: "If I put one more on top, the tower will stay up." When the tower falls, the child gets a burst of information — a prediction error! And then what? They try again, adjusting their approach. Play is the most natural, joyful form of prediction testing in the world.

### 2. Peekaboo = Active Inference

Peekaboo is the perfect example of Active Inference for babies. You hide your face → the baby's brain predicts "the face is gone." You reveal your face → surprise and delight! As the baby's brain gets better at predicting, the game has to evolve: hiding longer, popping up from unexpected sides, having someone else appear. The giggles are the outward signs of a tiny brain actively adjusting its model of a world where faces appear and disappear.

### 3. Sensory Play Builds Rich Models

A "mystery bag" game (reaching into an opaque bag to identify objects by touch alone) is pure perception as Active Inference. The child's brain generates predictions based on texture, shape, and temperature: "Smooth and round — a ball? No, it has a handle — a spoon!" Each touch confirms or surprises, and when the child pulls the object out, the reveal closes the loop.

## Activities

### 🫣 Activity 1: Peekaboo Laboratory

Play peekaboo with your baby or toddler, but vary the game systematically. Hide behind your hands. Then behind a pillow. Then from behind a door. Change the timing (short hide, long hide). Notice: what makes your child laugh the hardest? That is probably the version where their prediction was *almost* right but not quite — the sweet spot of surprise.

### 🎒 Activity 2: Mystery Bag

Put 5 familiar objects in an opaque bag (block, spoon, ball, sock, key). Your child reaches in without looking and guesses by touch alone. After they guess, they pull the object out. Were they right? If not, what fooled them? This game builds vocabulary, fine motor skills, and a powerful trust in the senses.

## Summary

Play is not a break from learning — it *is* learning. Every game a child plays is an experiment: predict, act, sense the result, and adjust. Peekaboo, block towers, mystery bags, puzzles — they are all Active Inference in its most joyful form. Parents can amplify the learning by joining in, asking "What do you think will happen?" and celebrating both right guesses and delightful surprises.

# Unit 4: Screens, Toys, and Tools — The World of Objects

## Learning Objectives

1. Understand that **toys, screens, and tools** are part of the family system — they shape how children think and learn.
2. Learn that different objects create different **affordances** (invitations to act) and different kinds of prediction errors.
3. Make thoughtful family choices about which tools support rich, active learning.

## Introduction: Objects Shape Minds

Screens, Toys, and Tools is the final unit in the Family curriculum, and it brings Active Inference into the world of objects and technology that families interact with every day. In Growing Together, we discovered families as systems. In Tiny Bodies, Big Brains, we marveled at children as agents. In Patterns in Play, we explored perception through games. Now we look at the things children use — screens, toys, and tools — and ask: how do these objects shape the way children think, learn, and make sense of the world? This unit helps families make thoughtful choices about the tools in their environment, understanding that every toy or screen is part of the larger system that shapes a child's developing mind.

The eight lessons in this unit apply the Active Inference spine to the material world of childhood. We examine toys, screens, and tools as systems with affordances (things they invite you to do) and constraints (things they make difficult). We consider the child as an agent interacting with these objects. We explore how different tools shape perception — how a magnifying glass changes what you see, how headphones change what you hear, how a touchscreen changes how you interact with images. We look at cognition as the thinking that toys provoke: puzzles force logical reasoning, building sets encourage spatial thinking, and pretend-play props support narrative thinking. Action is explored through the physical manipulation of objects. Learning is examined through how children master increasingly complex tools over time. Communication is studied through shared screen time, collaborative building, and how families talk about what they are making together. And planning comes alive through projects that require gathering materials, following steps, and working toward a finished creation.

## Key Concepts

### 1. Affordances: What Does This Object Invite?

Every toy or tool sends an invisible invitation. A ball says "throw me!" A crayon says "draw with me!" A puzzle says "solve me!" Scientists call these invitations **affordances**. Rich toys offer many affordances (blocks can be stacked, sorted, counted, thrown, hidden). Simple apps may offer only one (tap, tap, tap). Families can ask: "How many different things can my child *do* with this?"

### 2. Shape Sorters and Prediction Errors

A shape sorter is a beautiful Active Inference machine. The child picks up a star block and pushes it toward the circle hole. It does not fit — prediction error! They try another hole. Still wrong. They rotate the block, try again. When the block finally drops in — confirmation! Over time, the child builds a richer model: stars go in star holes, circles in circle holes. Eventually they hold up each block, look at the holes, and *choose the right one before trying*. That is cognition growing through structured play.

### 3. Active vs. Passive Screen Time

Not all screen time is equal. Watching a nature documentary and pausing to say "What do you think we will see next?" turns passive watching into active cognition. The child predicts (fish! sharks!), the screen reveals (a giant squid!), and the prediction error generates learning. Asking "What surprised you?" after the show strengthens the updated model. Families can transform *any* screen time into a rich exercise by asking questions before, during, and after.

## Activities

### 🧩 Activity 1: Shape Sorter Science

Play with a shape sorter with your child. Watch their strategy evolve. In the beginning, they try blocks randomly. Later, they look at the shape *before* pushing. Ask: "Which hole do you think this one goes in?" You are helping them practice prediction *before* action — the hallmark of more mature cognition.

### 📺 Activity 2: Active Documentary Night

Choose a short nature documentary. Before pressing play, ask: "What animals do you think we will see?" Write down the predictions. Watch together. Pause at surprises: "Did you expect that? What do you think happens next?" After watching, review the predictions: which ones came true? What was the biggest surprise? Draw the most surprising creature.

## Summary

Every toy, tool, and screen in your home is part of the system that shapes your child's thinking. Rich toys offer many affordances. Active screen time beats passive watching. And the simple question "What do you think will happen?" transforms any interaction into a learning engine. By the end of this unit, families have a new lens for looking at the objects in their lives — and a new appreciation for the science hiding inside play.

## Introduction

This module explores **Cognition**. In the **Family** curriculum, we approach this topic with a focus on specific applications and theoretical depth appropriate for the audience. Cognition is a critical component of the 8-part Active Inference spine, bridging the gap between Patterns in Play and course synthesis.

Screens, Toys, and Tools is the final unit in the Family curriculum, and it brings Active Inference into the world of objects and technology that families interact with every day. In Growing Together, we discovered families as systems. In Tiny Bodies, Big Brains, we marveled at children as agents. In Patterns in Play, we explored perception through games. Now we look at the things children use -- screens, toys, and tools -- and ask: how do these objects shape the way children think, learn, and make sense of the world? This unit helps families make thoughtful choices about the tools in their environment, understanding that every toy or screen is part of the larger system that shapes a child's developing mind.

The eight lessons in this unit apply the Active Inference spine to the material world of childhood. We examine toys, screens, and tools as systems with affordances (things they invite you to do) and constraints (things they make difficult). We consider the child as an agent interacting with these objects, asking what the child brings to the interaction versus what the object provides. We explore how different tools shape perception -- how a magnifying glass changes what you see, how headphones change what you hear, how a touchscreen changes how you interact with images. We look at cognition as the thinking that toys provoke: puzzles force logical reasoning, building sets encourage spatial thinking, and pretend-play props support narrative thinking. Action is explored through the physical manipulation of objects -- the fine motor demands of crayons versus markers, buttons versus touchscreens. Learning is examined through how children master increasingly complex tools over time. Communication is studied through shared screen time, collaborative building, and how families talk about what they are making together. And planning comes alive through projects that require gathering materials, following steps, and working toward a finished creation.

## Applications

In Family, we see Cognition manifest in:
*   **Specific Example 1**: Watch a young child playing with a shape sorter -- a box with holes shaped like circles, squares, and triangles, and blocks that match each hole. The child picks up the star-shaped block and tries to push it through the circle hole. It does not fit. That is a prediction error: the child's model said "push and it goes in," but reality said "nope." The child rotates the block, tries another hole, rotates again. Each attempt is an action guided by a prediction and followed by sensory feedback. When the block finally drops through the correct hole, the child lights up -- the prediction matched reality! Over many rounds, the child develops a richer cognitive model: stars go in star holes, circles go in circle holes. They start holding up each block, looking at its shape, looking at the holes, and choosing the right one before even trying. That is cognition: building an internal model, testing it against the world, and refining it until you can think ahead instead of just guessing. Parents can support this by celebrating both successes and "interesting tries," helping the child see prediction errors as useful information rather than failures.
*   **Specific Example 2**: Consider family screen time -- watching a nature documentary together. Before pressing play, ask your child: "What do you think we will see? What animals live in the ocean?" The child generates predictions based on their current model: "fish, sharks, maybe a whale!" As the documentary plays, some predictions come true (there are fish!) and some are surprised by new things (a giant squid! bioluminescent jellyfish!). Pausing to talk about these surprises -- "Did you expect that? What do you think will happen next?" -- turns passive screen watching into active cognition. The child is practicing the full Active Inference loop: predicting what comes next in the show, perceiving what actually appears on screen, noticing the difference, and updating their model of what lives in the ocean. After the show, drawing or discussing what was most surprising strengthens the updated model. This approach transforms any screen time into a rich cognitive exercise, and it gives families a shared language for talking about learning: "What surprised you today?"

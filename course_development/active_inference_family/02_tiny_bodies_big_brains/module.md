## Introduction

This module explores **Agents**. In the **Family** curriculum, we approach this topic with a focus on specific applications and theoretical depth appropriate for the audience. Agents is a critical component of the 8-part Active Inference spine, bridging the gap between Growing Together and Patterns in Play.

Tiny Bodies, Big Brains celebrates the amazing things that babies, toddlers, and young children can do. In the Growing Together unit, we discovered that families are systems. Now we zoom in on the individual family members -- especially the littlest ones -- and marvel at how they are agents: beings who are constantly sensing, predicting, and acting on the world. Even a newborn is an agent, turning toward voices, rooting for food, and crying when something feels wrong. This unit helps parents see their child's everyday behaviors through the lens of Active Inference, turning ordinary moments into opportunities for wonder and understanding.

The eight lessons follow the Active Inference spine with a focus on child development. We explore the child's body as a system of growing parts. We discover what makes a baby an agent from the very first day. We watch how infants perceive -- tracking faces, startling at loud sounds, reaching for bright objects. We consider how young minds think -- sorting, categorizing, and testing little hypotheses about how the world works. We observe action in the form of crawling, grasping, babbling, and all the physical ways children engage with their environment. We celebrate learning as the rapid skill acquisition that characterizes early childhood. We examine communication from first cries to first words to full sentences. And we look at the beginnings of planning in toddlers who line up their shoes before going outside or hide a toy to find it later.

## Applications

In Family, we see Agents manifest in:
*   **Specific Example 1**: Watch a baby who is learning to reach for a rattle. At first, the baby waves their arms in big, uncoordinated sweeps. Their brain has a fuzzy prediction of where their hand is and where the rattle is. Each time the hand accidentally bumps the rattle and it makes a sound, the baby's brain gets a burst of new information -- a prediction error that says "something interesting happened here!" Over days and weeks, the baby's reaching gets more and more accurate. The arm movements get smaller and more targeted. The baby is an agent doing active inference: predicting where the rattle is, reaching out (acting), noticing whether the hand hit the target (sensing), and updating the prediction for next time. Parents can support this process by holding the rattle at just the right distance -- close enough to succeed sometimes, far enough to keep it interesting. That is called scaffolding, and it is a beautiful example of two agents (parent and child) coordinating their actions within a shared system.
*   **Specific Example 2**: Consider a toddler in a grocery store who sees a banana and says "apple!" The toddler is being an agent with a generative model -- they have built an internal category for "yellow fruit" based on their experience, and right now that category is labeled "apple" because apples are what they eat most often. When mom says "that is a banana," the toddler experiences a gentle prediction error: their model predicted "apple" but the world said "banana." Over the next few shopping trips, the toddler updates their model, learning to distinguish apples from bananas by shape, size, and context. This everyday moment -- a child misidentifying a fruit and being gently corrected -- is Active Inference in miniature. The child is predicting, the world is providing feedback, and the child's inner model is updating. Parents do not need to teach this process; they just need to keep providing the world and naming what is in it.

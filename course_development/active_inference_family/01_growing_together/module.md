## Introduction

This module explores **Systems**. In the **Family** curriculum, we approach this topic with a focus on specific applications and theoretical depth appropriate for the audience. Systems is a critical component of the 8-part Active Inference spine, bridging the gap between the course introduction and Tiny Bodies, Big Brains.

Growing Together is about discovering that families themselves are systems -- groups of people who are connected, who depend on each other, and who grow and change together. This unit is designed for parents and young children to explore side by side, using everyday family moments as windows into how systems work. No science background is needed. The ideas are woven into activities that families already do: cooking dinner, tidying up, taking walks, and talking about their day.

The eight lessons in this unit gently introduce each part of the Active Inference spine through the lens of family life. We start by noticing that our family is a system with members who each play a role. Then we discover that every family member is an agent -- someone who senses, thinks, and acts. We explore perception by paying attention to how we notice things together (sounds, smells, changes in mood). We look at cognition through the family conversations where we figure things out. We celebrate action as all the things we do to take care of each other. We marvel at learning by watching how the youngest family members grow and gain new skills every day. We practice communication by finding better ways to share our feelings and needs. And we wrap up with planning -- how families make decisions together about what to do tomorrow, next weekend, or next year.

## Applications

In Family, we see Systems manifest in:
*   **Specific Example 1**: Your family dinner table is a system. Think about all the parts that come together: someone chooses the recipe (planning), someone goes to the store or garden (action), someone cooks (more action!), and everyone sits together to eat (the system working). The table has a kind of boundary: the people sitting around it are "inside" the dinner system, and the rest of the world is "outside." When dinner goes smoothly, everyone knows what to expect -- pasta on Tuesday, grandma's soup on Sunday. But when something changes -- a new recipe, a guest joining, or a toddler throwing peas -- the family system has to adjust. The surprise of peas on the floor is like a little signal that things are not going as expected, and the family responds: someone picks up the peas, someone offers the toddler a spoon, and the system settles back into a comfortable rhythm.
*   **Specific Example 2**: Bedtime routines are a wonderful example of a family system in action. Bath, pajamas, story, song, goodnight. Young children thrive on this predictable pattern because their growing brains are building an internal model of "what happens at bedtime." When the routine is followed, the child feels safe and calm -- their predictions match reality. But skip the story one night, and watch what happens: the child protests or feels unsettled, because their expectation was not met. Over time, the family system adapts -- maybe the story gets shorter on busy nights, or a new lullaby replaces the old one. Parents and children are co-creating and adjusting their shared system together, and this process of mutual adjustment is exactly what Active Inference describes: predicting, noticing surprises, and updating our models of the world.

# Unit 04: Active Inference in Technology and AI

## Learning Objectives

1. Analyze how **artificial intelligence systems** -- including large language models, recommendation algorithms, and autonomous vehicles -- implement principles related to Active Inference.
2. Evaluate the similarities and differences between **biological inference** (as studied in Units 1-2) and **machine inference** in modern AI systems.
3. Apply the Active Inference framework to critically assess the technology you interact with daily, from social media feeds to voice assistants.
4. Engage with ethical questions about AI design, algorithmic bias, and the future of human-machine interaction using the vocabulary of Active Inference.

## Introduction

You interact with inference engines every day, and most of them are not biological. When your phone autocompletes a sentence, it is generating predictions about the next word. When TikTok or YouTube serves you a video, an algorithm has inferred what will keep you watching. When a self-driving car navigates an intersection, it is maintaining a generative model of its environment and selecting actions to minimize expected surprise.

These systems are not using Active Inference in its full biological sense -- most current AI systems lack the self-maintaining, embodied character of living organisms. But they share deep structural similarities: they build models, generate predictions, process prediction errors, and update their parameters to reduce uncertainty. Understanding Active Inference gives you a powerful lens for understanding what these systems are actually doing, what they are good at, and where they fail.

This unit bridges the gap between the biological and mathematical foundations of Active Inference (Units 1-3) and the technological world you are inheriting. More than any previous generation, you will need to understand how inference machines work -- not just as users, but as citizens who will shape how these technologies develop.

## Unit Structure

This unit contains eight modules that follow the Active Inference spine:

1. **Systems** -- AI architectures as systems: inputs, outputs, boundaries, and the computational Markov Blankets that define them.
2. **Agents** -- From chatbots to autonomous vehicles: what makes an AI system an "agent," and how does this compare to biological agency?
3. **Perception** -- Computer vision, natural language processing, and how AI systems process sensory data into internal representations.
4. **Cognition** -- Neural networks as generative models: how AI systems form internal representations and make inferences.
5. **Action** -- Reinforcement learning, recommendation systems, and how AI systems select actions to achieve objectives.
6. **Learning** -- Training, fine-tuning, and adaptation: how machine learning systems update their models from data.
7. **Communication** -- Large language models, human-AI interaction, and the challenge of shared understanding between humans and machines.
8. **Planning** -- Autonomous decision-making, AI safety, and the challenge of building systems that plan responsibly.

## Key Themes

### AI Systems Are (Partial) Inference Engines

Modern AI systems implement fragments of the Active Inference framework. A large language model like GPT minimizes prediction error over text sequences. A reinforcement learning agent minimizes expected free energy (approximately) when selecting actions. But no current AI system implements the full Active Inference loop -- the self-maintaining, embodied, hierarchical inference that characterizes biological agents. Understanding what is missing is just as important as understanding what is present.

### Algorithms Shape Your Generative Model

Social media algorithms do not just predict what you want to see -- they shape what you come to expect, believe, and desire. From an Active Inference perspective, recommendation algorithms are modifying your generative model by controlling the sensory evidence you receive. This is a profound form of influence, and understanding it in formal terms is the first step toward resisting manipulation.

### The Alignment Problem Is an Inference Problem

The central challenge of AI safety -- ensuring that AI systems pursue goals aligned with human values -- can be framed as an inference problem. An AI system's "generative model" of what humans want is always approximate and potentially wrong. The consequences of prediction errors in this domain are not just academic; they are existential. Active Inference provides a vocabulary for thinking precisely about these risks.

## Discussion Questions

1. When a large language model generates a response, is it "perceiving" and "acting" in the Active Inference sense, or is something fundamentally different happening compared to biological inference?
2. How might understanding Active Inference change the way you interact with recommendation algorithms on social media?
3. If an autonomous vehicle must choose between two dangerous outcomes, how should its generative model weigh different types of prediction error? Who decides?

## Summary

Technology and AI are not separate from the principles of Active Inference -- they are increasingly powerful implementations of those principles, albeit incomplete ones. By understanding AI systems through the lens of Active Inference, you gain both technical insight into how these systems work and critical perspective on their limitations, biases, and societal implications. You are not just consumers of this technology; you are the generation that will decide what it becomes. The concepts in this unit are tools for making those decisions wisely.

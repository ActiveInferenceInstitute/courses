## About Active Inference

### What is Active Inference in one sentence?

Active Inference is the idea that your brain is constantly predicting what will happen next, comparing those predictions to reality, and updating its model — and that this single process explains perception, action, learning, and planning.

### Why should I learn about this?

Active Inference is one of the most exciting ideas in modern science. Understanding it will help you understand how your brain works (Life M3, Bio M3), why you make the decisions you make (Life M5), how AI systems think (Tech M5), and even why you sometimes misunderstand other people (Life M7). It connects biology, psychology, math, and computer science into one unified framework.

### Is this just about brains?

No. While Active Inference started as a theory of brain function (Karl Friston, 2005–present), it applies to any system that maintains itself over time — cells, organisms, robots, ecosystems, even social organizations. Course 1 (Everyday Life M1) explores where the boundary of "system" lies.

### Who invented Active Inference?

Karl Friston, a neuroscientist at University College London, developed the Free Energy Principle and Active Inference starting around 2005. Many other researchers have expanded the framework since then. See our [References](./references.md) for key papers and accessible introductions.

## About This Curriculum

### What grade level is this for?

Grades 9–12 (high school). Course 1 (Everyday Life) is accessible to any high school student. Courses 2–4 progressively introduce more biology, math, and computer science.

### Do I need to take the courses in order?

We recommend starting with Course 1 (Everyday Life), as it builds the conceptual foundation. After that, courses can be taken in any order, though the recommended path is 1 → 2 → 3 → 4. See [Learning Pathways](./learning_pathways.md) for alternative study orders.

### What math do I need?

- **Course 1**: No math needed.
- **Course 2**: Basic biology is helpful but not required.
- **Course 3**: Algebra 1 is the minimum; Algebra 2 or Precalculus is ideal.
- **Course 4**: No prior coding experience needed — we start from scratch.

### Can I use this for self-study?

Absolutely. Each module has an interactive dashboard with self-assessment quizzes. The dashboards track your progress and give immediate feedback.

### Can this be used in a classroom?

Yes. See the [Learning Pathways](./learning_pathways.md) for teacher-specific pacing suggestions. The labs are designed for individual or group work, and the discussion questions in each module support class discussion.

## Common Misconceptions

### Is this about "inference" like in reading class?

No — well, sort of! In reading class, inference means figuring out what the author means from clues in the text. In Active Inference, inference means figuring out what's happening in the world from clues your senses give you. The underlying logic is similar: using evidence to figure out something you can't directly observe. (Life M3)

### Is Active Inference just another name for machine learning?

No, but they're related. Machine learning is a set of techniques for training computers to recognize patterns. Active Inference is a theory of how brains (and potentially AI systems) work. Active Inference can be implemented using tools from machine learning, but it's a broader theory about the relationship between perception, action, and learning. (Tech M6)

### Does the brain really "minimize free energy"?

This is an active area of debate. Some scientists think the brain literally computes something like free energy (the "process theory" view). Others think free energy is a useful mathematical description of what the brain does, without claiming the brain explicitly computes it (the "principle" view). We explore this question in Life M1 and Math M3.

### Is this related to "free energy" in physics (thermodynamics)?

They share a name and have a historical connection, but they're different quantities. In Active Inference, "variational free energy" is about the accuracy of predictions, not about heat and work. The name comes from an analogy with statistical thermodynamics. (Math M3)

## Technical Questions

### What programming language does Course 4 use?

Python. We start with very basic Python (variables, if-statements, loops) and build up to simple Active Inference agents.

### Do I need to install anything?

For Courses 1–3, everything works in your browser (dashboards are HTML files). For Course 4, you'll need Python installed — we recommend using Google Colab (free, runs in your browser) or installing Python locally with instructions provided in Tech M1.

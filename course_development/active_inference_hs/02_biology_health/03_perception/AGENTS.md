# Station: Perception (Biology & Health)

> **Quick Navigation**: [Module README](./README.md) | [Course AGENTS](../AGENTS.md)

## Conventions

- **Perspective**: Biological mechanisms -- neural pathways, sensory transduction, and predictive coding
- **Topics**: Sensory Pathways, Predictive Coding, Sensory Precision, Sensory Disorders
- **Lab Style**: Investigation lab with safe sensory experiments and neuroscience research

## Content Guidelines

- Root all perception concepts in actual neuroscience: name brain regions, receptor types, and neural pathways.
- Use safe, hands-on sensory experiments (afterimages, two-point discrimination) to make abstract concepts tangible.
- Present predictive coding as a real biological architecture, not just a metaphor.
- Connect sensory disorders to prediction failures to show clinical relevance.

## Active Inference Integration

- Perception in biology is implemented through predictive coding: the cortex generates top-down predictions that are compared with bottom-up sensory signals.
- Prediction errors propagate up the cortical hierarchy; predictions propagate down.
- Sensory precision maps to receptor density and signal-to-noise ratio in neural circuits.
- Sensory disorders (phantom limb, tinnitus) arise from persistent, unresolvable prediction errors.

## Lab Design Principles

- Include at least one safe sensory experiment students can perform on themselves.
- Sensory pathway mapping should name specific structures (retina, thalamus, V1).
- Predictive coding should be explored as a biological architecture with a clear hierarchy.
- Disorder analysis should connect clinical conditions to Active Inference failure modes.

## Question Design Standards

- Questions should test whether students can trace a sensory signal from receptor to cortex.
- Include questions about precision: "Why is your fingertip more sensitive than your back?"
- At least one question should explain a sensory phenomenon using predictive coding.

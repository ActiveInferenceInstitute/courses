# Station: Planning (Biology & Health)

> **Quick Navigation**: [Module README](./README.md) | [Course AGENTS](../AGENTS.md)

## Conventions

- **Perspective**: Biological mechanisms -- prefrontal cortex, executive function, and adolescent brain development
- **Topics**: Prefrontal Cortex Mapping, Executive Function, Impulse Control, Goal-Directed Behavior
- **Lab Style**: Investigation lab with cognitive self-assessment, neuroscience research, and personal goal design

## Content Guidelines

- Name specific prefrontal cortex subdivisions and their planning functions.
- Connect delayed gratification and impulse control to the biological reality of teenage brain development.
- Frame executive function as a biological capacity that develops -- not a character trait that some people have and others lack.
- Use the marshmallow test as a concrete, well-known example of planning under temptation.

## Active Inference Integration

- Planning is implemented by the prefrontal cortex evaluating policies through forward simulation of future states.
- Executive functions (working memory, inhibition, flexibility) are the biological tools for policy evaluation.
- Impulse control is the tension between present sensory drive and future-oriented model predictions.
- Adolescent prefrontal development means the planning system is still being optimized.

## Lab Design Principles

- PFC mapping should name at least four subregions with specific functions.
- Executive function tasks should be hands-on and self-scored.
- Marshmallow test analysis should bridge famous psychology experiments with neuroscience.
- Personal goal-setting should apply all biological concepts to a real student goal.

## Question Design Standards

- Questions should test whether students can connect prefrontal regions to specific planning functions.
- Include questions about why teenagers find impulse control harder than adults (biological basis).
- At least one question should ask students to design a plan using executive function concepts.

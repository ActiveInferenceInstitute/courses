# Practice Quiz: Communication -- Aligning Models Across Minds

## Part A: Multiple Choice

1. In Active Inference, communication is fundamentally about:
A) Transmitting words from one person to another
B) Aligning generative models between agents to reduce mutual prediction error
C) Talking as loudly as possible
D) Sending messages that contain zero surprise

**Answer: B.** Effective communication brings two agents' models into closer alignment, reducing the surprise each generates for the other.

2. When your friend replies with just "k" to a long message, the spike in prediction error you feel occurs because:
A) The letter K is offensive
B) Your generative model expected a longer, warmer response and the mismatch drives you to seek more information
C) Short messages always mean anger
D) Your phone displayed the message incorrectly

**Answer: B.** The discrepancy between your predicted response and the actual one generates uncertainty that motivates further communication.

3. During a group presentation, a speaker adjusting their tone and pace based on audience facial expressions is an example of:
A) Nervousness
B) Active Inference communication -- reading sensory evidence of audience engagement and updating the delivery model in real time
C) Memorizing the script
D) Ignoring the audience

**Answer: B.** The speaker continuously minimizes the gap between intended and received meaning by using audience feedback as prediction error.

4. Why are misunderstandings more common in text messages than face-to-face conversations?
A) People type less carefully than they speak
B) Text strips away tone, facial expression, and body language, leaving fewer channels for prediction error correction and allowing priors to dominate
C) Phones are unreliable
D) Everyone types with bad grammar

**Answer: B.** With fewer sensory channels, your generative model fills in the missing social information from its own expectations.

5. Humor works in social settings by:
A) Making people forget their problems
B) Setting up a prediction (the setup) and then violating it in a satisfying way (the punchline), producing a controlled, pleasant prediction error
C) Always involving physical comedy
D) Being completely unpredictable at every moment

**Answer: B.** The structure of jokes mirrors Active Inference -- the laugh comes from the resolution of a deliberately created prediction error.

6. Active listening -- paraphrasing back what someone said -- improves communication because:
A) It wastes time
B) It provides the speaker with evidence of your generative model's interpretation, allowing them to correct misalignments immediately
C) It proves you are smarter
D) It is a required school skill

**Answer: B.** Reflecting back your interpretation creates a prediction error check, catching model mismatches before they compound.

7. When two people from different cultural backgrounds miscommunicate, it is often because:
A) One person is wrong
B) Their generative models have different priors about what gestures, tones, and silences mean, leading to divergent interpretations of the same signals
C) Cross-cultural communication is impossible
D) Only one culture has correct communication norms

**Answer: B.** Different life experiences create different priors, so the same sensory input generates different predictions.

8. Why does gossip spread so quickly through social networks?
A) People are bored
B) Novel social information generates high prediction error (surprise), making it highly salient and worth sharing to update others' models
C) Gossip is always true
D) Only teenagers gossip

**Answer: B.** Surprising information carries high informational value, driving rapid transmission through social Markov Blankets.

9. Effective teachers reduce prediction error in students by:
A) Giving easy tests
B) Building on what students already know (their existing priors) and introducing new information at a manageable rate of surprise
C) Talking as fast as possible
D) Never surprising students

**Answer: B.** Good teaching calibrates the rate of prediction error to the students' current generative models.

10. Apologizing after hurting someone can be understood in Active Inference as:
A) A meaningless social ritual
B) An action that acknowledges the prediction error you caused in the other person's model and signals your commitment to realigning your models
C) Admitting you are always wrong
D) Something that only weak people do

**Answer: B.** An apology addresses the surprise you introduced and helps restore predictive alignment between your models.

2. Explain how "reading the room" before speaking is an Active Inference process. What sensory evidence are you gathering, and what predictions are you generating?

3. Why might it be harder to communicate something deeply personal (like feelings about a relationship) than something factual (like directions to a location)? Use the concept of model alignment to explain.

4. How does the Active Inference view of communication explain why arguments escalate? What happens when both parties increase precision weighting on threat-related signals?

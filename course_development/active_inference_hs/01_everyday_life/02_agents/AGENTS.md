# Station: Agents (Everyday Life)

> **Quick Navigation**: [Module README](./README.md) | [Course AGENTS](../AGENTS.md)

## Conventions

- **Perspective**: Real-world intuition -- use familiar creatures and devices as examples
- **Topics**: Agency, Preferred States, Surprise Minimization, Biological vs. Mechanical Agents
- **Lab Style**: Classification and observation activity (no coding required)

## Content Guidelines

- Write at a high school reading level (grades 9-12). Define technical terms on first use.
- Connect the concept of "agent" to things students already know: pets, robots, apps, classmates.
- Use the question "Does this thing have goals?" as the entry point for understanding agency.
- Avoid implying that only humans are agents -- plants and simple machines can also qualify under Active Inference.

## Active Inference Integration

- An agent is any system that maintains preferred states by acting on the world.
- Surprise minimization is the core drive: agents act so that their sensory states match their predictions.
- Introduce the generative model as the agent's internal "theory" of how the world works.
- Distinguish biological agents (complex, adaptive models) from mechanical agents (simple, fixed models).

## Lab Design Principles

- Include a classification exercise where students sort familiar entities into agent/non-agent categories.
- Ask students to track their own agency over a period of time to make the concept personal.
- Include a comparison task between biological and mechanical agents.
- Use tables for structured observation and fill-in areas for reflective writing.

## Question Design Standards

- Questions should test whether students can distinguish agents from non-agents with justification.
- Include scenario-based questions: "Is a vending machine an agent? Defend your answer."
- At least one question should challenge students to think about edge cases (a virus, a growing crystal).

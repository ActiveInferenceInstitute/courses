## Introduction

This module explores **Agents**. In the **101** curriculum, we approach this topic with a focus on specific applications and theoretical depth appropriate for the audience. Agents is a critical component of the 8-part Active Inference spine, bridging the gap between Cognitive Science and Mathematical Frameworks.

Having established what systems are and how they are bounded, this unit turns to the computational neuroscience perspective on agents -- the entities within systems that actively maintain themselves by minimizing free energy. At the 101 level, we connect the abstract notion of an agent to concrete neural architectures: predictive coding circuits, cortical hierarchies, and dopaminergic prediction error signals. The goal is for students to see that the brain is not a passive stimulus-response machine but an active inference engine that constantly generates and updates predictions about the world.

This unit's eight modules trace the full arc of agency from a neuroscience vantage point. You will learn how neural systems define the boundary between self and world, how populations of neurons implement generative models, how perception arises as inference, how cognition selects among hypotheses, how action is the body's way of confirming predictions, how synaptic plasticity enables learning, how neural communication coordinates distributed processing, and how prefrontal planning circuits evaluate future trajectories.

## Applications

In 101, we see Agents manifest in:
*   **Specific Example 1**: A foraging rat navigating a radial arm maze serves as a canonical example of an Active Inference agent in computational neuroscience. The rat maintains an internal generative model of which arms contain food rewards. Hippocampal place cells encode spatial predictions, while dopaminergic signals from the ventral tegmental area register prediction errors when expected rewards are absent or unexpected rewards appear. The rat does not simply react to stimuli; it actively samples the environment (choosing which arm to explore) in a way that resolves uncertainty about its model, balancing exploitation of known rewards with epistemic exploration of unvisited arms.
*   **Specific Example 2**: Consider a student studying for a midterm exam as an Active Inference agent. The student has a generative model of what the exam will cover, shaped by the syllabus, lecture notes, and past exams. When reviewing material, they experience prediction errors -- moments where their understanding fails to predict what the textbook says. These errors drive further study (active inference), and the student preferentially allocates time to topics that generate the highest uncertainty (epistemic foraging). The prefrontal cortex coordinates this planning, the hippocampus encodes episodic memories of studied material, and the emotional valence of anxiety about the exam provides a precision signal that sharpens attention.

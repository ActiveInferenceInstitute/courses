## Content Guidelines

This course introduces Active Inference from the perspective of cognitive science, emphasizing how the framework unifies perception, action, learning, and planning under a single principle. All content should:

1. **Use accessible language** appropriate for first-semester undergraduates with no assumed background in neuroscience, mathematics, or philosophy of mind.
2. **Frame all concepts through cognitive science**: Emphasize mental processes (perception, attention, memory, language, reasoning) and behavioral evidence. Draw on classic cognitive science experiments and paradigms.
3. **Define jargon on first use**: Key Active Inference terms must be explicitly defined when introduced:
   - **Generative model**: The brain's internal model of how hidden states in the world cause sensory observations
   - **Prediction error**: The mismatch between expected and actual sensory input
   - **Free energy**: A tractable upper bound on surprise that the brain works to minimize
   - **Markov blanket**: The statistical boundary separating internal from external states
   - **Precision weighting**: The brain's confidence assignment to different sources of information
4. **Integrate real-world examples**: Every concept should be illustrated with everyday human cognition examples before any formalism is introduced.
5. **Build cumulatively across modules**: Systems (M01) through Planning (M08) form a coherent narrative arc. Each module should reference prior modules and preview upcoming ones.

## Lab Design Principles

Labs in this course are **essay and discussion** format:

- Provide a scenario or case study drawn from everyday cognition or psychological research
- Ask students to analyze the scenario using Active Inference concepts from the module
- Include group discussion prompts that encourage students to compare interpretations
- Use `{fill:textarea}` for open-ended written responses
- Target 2500-4000 bytes per lab exercise

## Question Standards

- Study questions should test conceptual understanding, not rote memorization
- Include questions at multiple Bloom's taxonomy levels: recall, comprehension, application, and analysis
- Practice quizzes should mix multiple-choice and short-answer formats
- All questions should connect back to Active Inference principles
